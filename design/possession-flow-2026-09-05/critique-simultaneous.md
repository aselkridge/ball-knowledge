# Critique: Lock and Play (the simultaneous concept)

Judged 2026-09-05 against brief.md, scout.md and the critic's job sheet. Three lenses, then the doubt, flaws, strengths, verdict.

## Lens 1 · The first-time player

I am one of twenty testers, on a 390 pixel phone, never read a rule. I walk the ten beats as written.

**Beat 1, ball in.** My end shows four name chips stamped OPEN or TRAFFIC and a five second clock in my colour. I know it is my turn because buttons and a clock are in front of me. I do not know what TRAFFIC costs me. Confusion 1. If I am the defender, I see SHADE LEFT, STAY, SHADE RIGHT, HELP. Left of what? The phone is lying between us and his left is my right. Confusion 2, and it repeats every beat. Nobody is on the ball, it is out of bounds, so which of my five men is doing the shading? Confusion 3.

**Beat 2, the reveal.** Rows fade, a light follows the pass, "Curry takes it. Ball in." I know what happened. As the defender I do not know whether my shade did anything, because the line does not mention me. Confusion 4, small.

**Beat 3, first look.** Curry wears a ring, three lanes fan out, my row says DRIVE, PASS, SHOOT. This is the clearest beat in the concept: I know it is me, I know my three verbs. I tap DRIVE and the three buttons turn into LEFT, MIDDLE, RIGHT in the same place under my thumb. If I meant PASS there is no way back shown. A second tap where DRIVE was is now MIDDLE, which is a question card I did not choose. Confusion 5, and it is the kind that loses a possession. The middle lane has a card mark and the right lane has a hatch mark. I do not know what either mark means until I have been burned by one. Confusion 6. As the defender I have the same four buttons and nothing on them says what each one beats. That STAY is the anti-shot call and HELP opens a man is learned by losing. Confusion 7, repeats every beat.

**Beat 4, the reveal.** A helper slides, Curry runs one tile and stops. The line says "Draymond beat him to the spot." Draymond is on my team. I look for what Draymond did. Confusion 8. Did I turn it over? The row relights, so I guess not. The OPEN tag lighting over Wiggins is good and I read it without help.

**Beat 5, kick-out.** Tap PASS, four names with prices. Clear. As the defender, HELP now means the helper runs back, the opposite of what it did two beats ago. Nothing tells me. Confusion 9.

**Beat 6.** Clear. The tag "3 PTS · HARD CARD · CONTESTED" is the best single thing on the screen for a first-timer.

**Beat 7.** SHOOT is one tap and it locks. If my thumb slipped, that was my shot. Same flaw as 5.

**Beat 8.** "He bit! Wiggins is open from three." Clear, and the tag flips in front of me.

**Beat 9, the card.** Shipped already, clear.

**Beat 10.** Buzzer, colour rolls across the court, my row changes shape. Three cues at once. I know the ball changed hands without reading anything.

**Between every beat, two things the table does not show.** When I lock, my row goes dark. If he is still thinking, I sit looking at a dark row with no words on it for as long as he thinks. Nothing says "waiting for him." Confusion 10, every beat, worst for the defender (see Lens 2). And on one shared phone the court is drawn in 3D from one end. The tester at the far end reads the court upside down and foreshortened the wrong way for the whole game. The buzz race posture the concept borrows lasts seconds; this lasts fifteen minutes. Confusion 11, permanent.

**Count:** eleven moments, four of them repeating every beat (left and right mirrored, what the call beats, the dark wait, the upside-down court). Do I know it is my turn: yes, always, because it is always my turn until I lock, and that is genuinely simpler than a sequence. Do I know what I can do: yes on offense, no on defense. Do I know what just happened: yes when the line is right, and it is wrong once in its own example. Do I know the ball changed hands: yes, three cues, the strongest part. Is the hand-off between beats visible without reading: yes, rows fade, light, rows relight.

**Score: 5 / 10.** One repeating state and one row are real clarity wins; the defense row is unreadable without a rulebook, and the shared phone breaks it.

## Lens 2 · Tempo and waiting

**The concept's own table, added up.** Offense taps: 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 7. Defense taps: 4. Seconds: 5 + 2 + 5 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 15 + 3 = 44. The concept says "11 offense taps" under the table. That is wrong by four; the table shows 7. The "about 45 with the card, 30 without" holds. The Numbers section's "offense 9 to 14" would need a possession two beats longer than the one shown.

**The shot clock.** The 24 runs only while picking, by the concept's own rule. The table spends 5 + 5 + 4 + 4 = 18 of it on four picks, at the concept's brisk pace. One more beat, or one slow beat, and it is a shot clock violation. A first-timer who takes eight seconds to read three lanes with two kinds of mark gets three picks, then the buzzer. "Four or five snaps fit inside it" is true only for a player who already knows the game. The 15 second card clock is untouched and correct, and the block card inside the contested window is the shipped mechanic.

**The waiting, checked against the rule.** Open question 4 says the defense gets three seconds after the offense locks, then defaults to STAY. Read that as a defender: locking first gains me nothing, and locking second lets me see the offense commit and, on a shared phone, watch the thumb. So the defense always waits. Every beat, the defender sits with a dark row for the offense's whole think, 4 to 20 seconds, then has a three second fuse. That is not "nobody waits." That is the shipped shape, offense then defense, with a panic timer bolted on and no words on the screen while you wait. The concept's "longest wait 3 seconds" is false under its own rule; the honest number is the offense's think time, up to the shot clock, four or five times a possession. The offense for its part waits two seconds a reveal and three for the fuse, which is fine.

**Over a game.** Race to 11: six to ten possessions, five to seven minutes, brisk. Twenty-four possessions: about 16 minutes plus cards, about 110 pick-lock-watch snaps, about 110 blind four-button guesses for the defender, and close to four minutes of reveal watching. The reveal is two seconds and the same every time; the concept calls that rhythm. A hundred and ten of them is where rhythm becomes a metronome.

**Shared phone and two phones.** Shared: two thumbs on one phone at once, at opposite ends, on a 3D court that faces one of them. Two phones: both locks must arrive before the reveal, so latency lands inside the three second fuse; the concept admits this and has not measured it.

**Against what is shipped (16 to 20 taps, 45 to 75 seconds).** Fewer taps, yes: 7 against 16 to 20 for the offense. Shorter, yes: 30 to 45 against 45 to 75. But look where the saving comes from. Every saved tap is an off-ball tap, and it is saved by putting the four off-ball men on rails. An alternating one-move design with the same rails saves the same taps. The simultaneous lock itself saves at most the two or three seconds of a separate defensive beat, and pays that back with the dark wait and the fuse. So it is faster than today because of the rails, and different because of the lock.

**Score: 6 / 10.** Genuinely quicker and lighter for the offense than what is shipped. The defense's experience is the same four buttons a hundred times with a dark wait between, and the concept's waiting number is not honest.

## Lens 3 · Rules and research

**Question gates on shots and crossovers.** Shots: kept, priced by the tile, the card comes up. Crossovers: kept where a lane is drawn through a man with a card mark. Not specified: what happens when one defender steps into an open lane during the beat. If the drive stops short with no card, the defense's blind step has skipped a question gate the offense never got to try. If a card fires, the offense is asked a question it never saw priced. The concept must pick one; I would rule one defender in the lane is the crossover card as today, and only a two-defender wall stops the drive short. The concept should give here.

**The tile sets the price.** Kept. CONTESTED and OPEN LOOK do not change the card; they add or remove the block card. Consistent with what is shipped.

**Eight tiles and closed lanes.** Kept in words. But four of five defenders move by a script nobody is tapping, so closed lanes appear from moves neither player chose, and the hatch mark only warns about the ball-side helper. Acceptable only if the scripts are shown running before the pick, which the concept promises and does not draw.

**The 24.** Changed: it counts deciding time only and holds during reveals and cards, said out loud. The file also calls it a per-turn clock for both sides; the concept makes it a possession clock without saying so. The rule should give on both counts: one big clock top right is what he asked for, and holding it during a card is how the 15 already works.

**Plays picked once, offense-only timeouts.** Kept, and extended into three-beat scripts on both sides. A fair extension, said out loud. The cost is authoring six plays times three beats per side before anyone can play, and after the three beats the four off-ball men freeze, which is beats four and five, exactly when the kick-out matters.

**The free off-ball move.** He said on 09-05 "movement of the person with the ball or somebody off the ball is free, so we're still keeping that as free." The concept removes it and says so. The reason given, that his two sentences conflict, is not true: one free move a turn is consistent with "not everybody every time." This is his ruling to make, not the concept's; the concept should give, with MOVE A MAN on the row from day one or a question to him.

**Live-ball continuation.** Kept, cleanly.

**The research verdicts.**
- No mid-beat prompts to the defender, ever: the three second fuse after the offense locks is a prompt with a timer, four or five times a possession. Fails.
- One defender, one small capped move, made last with full information, reads fair: the concept keeps the size and drops both "last" and "informed," and says so in Borrowed From. The reason the research gave for Kill Team reading fair was the timing, not the size: "not because it matches volume but because it moves LAST with full information." The concept keeps the part that did not matter and discards the part that did. Fails.
- Several small reaction windows read as unbearable: four or five blind calls a possession plus a fuse each. The concept argues they are not reactions; under the three second rule they are. The scout notes the sources behind this finding are thin, so he can overrule it, but he should do it knowing.
- Safe moves first, the gamble last: partly kept. A drive or pass costs a beat and never the ball; the card is last. This is the concept's best fit with the research.
- Blind pre-commitment and the hidden simultaneous pick, rejected by name (XCOM overwatch, Tecmo Bowl): the concept is that shape and says so. Its defence, that the stakes are one price step and the options are public, is fair as far as it goes. But the research rejected Tecmo Bowl for being "basically rock, paper, scissors," and the concept lists rock-paper-scissors as a source.

**Does it quietly reintroduce tap-every-player?** No. It goes the other way: nobody can tap any off-ball player on either side, and the defense cannot put one defender on one tile at all. The nearest shipped cousin's loudest verified complaint was its defense being on rails ("an absolute nightmare"), and the auto-defense game the research read was found hard to tune and luck-heavy. This concept builds toward both.

**Score: 4 / 10.** It keeps the gates, the lanes, the plays-once rule and live ball, and it names its contradictions, which earns a point. It drops the one defensive finding the research checked and stood behind, removes a freedom the owner reaffirmed this week, and adds a fuse the standing rule forbids.

## The owner's doubt

**No.** His sentence has three parts: the offense moves, the defense moves, the offense shoots. He asked how to make that sequence clear and fluid, not whether to have it; in the same breath he said the defense "gets their turn to move," it "goes back and forth," and "that part needs to be more like chess." Chess is the opposite of a simultaneous lock. The concept removes the defense's turn and its response entirely: the defense never sees a move before answering, it predicts. A guess is not a response, and his own worry was that the defense should "have the option to respond." The concept does make the flow simple to digest, one repeated state, one row, and that deserves credit. But it gets there by answering a different question. He will read it and ask "when does the defense respond?" and the honest answer is never. On a shared phone it turns his chess into rock-paper-scissors with thumb-watching.

## Flaws

1. **The defense predicts instead of responding.** Serious. It is the shape he did not ask for and the research rejected by name, and it throws away the "moves last, informed" part of the one finding the research stood behind. Fix: the defense picks its call after seeing the offense's pick, not with it. That is a different concept (one-move alternating with a four-call defense), so this is the flaw that decides the verdict.
2. **"Nobody waits" is false under the three second rule.** Serious. The defense always locks second, so it waits the offense's whole think with a dark row, then has a fuse. Fix: one shared window for both sides from the moment the rows light, both locked or defaulted at the same buzzer, no advantage to locking second. That also kills thumb-watching, because both thumbs are moving at once.
3. **The free off-ball move is removed against his words.** Serious. Fix: MOVE A MAN as the fourth offense button from day one, one man, his range, no defensive answer to it, as the concept's own fallback says. Or ask him and build nothing until he rules.
4. **The shared phone breaks it.** Serious. Two thumbs at once on one phone, a 3D court facing one end, and LEFT and RIGHT mirrored between the two players. Fix: name the calls by the court (BALL SIDE, HELP SIDE, or SIDELINE, MIDDLE), never by hand; a top-down court on a shared phone, which is his 3D-chess question anyway; or drop the lock on a shared phone and pass it, which the concept's first open question already half offers.
5. **HELP means two different things in beats 3 and 5.** Serious for a first-timer. Fix: every call has one fixed meaning, and before locking, the court draws a ghost arrow for the defender each call would move and where. The defender then picks a picture, not a word.
6. **The offense row replaces itself under the thumb and the second tap is final.** Serious on a phone. A double tap on DRIVE lands on MIDDLE, a question card. Fix: the lane or name chips open above the verb row, not in its place; the chip lights on the first tap and locks on the second, or a separate LOCK button. One more tap, worth it.
7. **The defense cannot place a defender.** Serious over a season, minor in a first game. The four calls are the whole defense; the research's nearest cousin was hated for exactly this. Fix: on two phones, tap a defender then a tile as one of the calls, with the same one-tile cap.
8. **The crossover gate is unspecified when one defender steps into a lane during the beat.** Minor, must be ruled before code. Fix in Lens 3 above.
9. **The 24 is tight for a new player.** Minor. Four picks at his pace use 18 of 24. Fix: the 24 only runs while the offense's row is lit, and the first game of any account gets a slower clock. Otherwise expect shot clock violations in the first three possessions of every tester.
10. **The table's numbers are wrong in two places.** Minor but it is the honesty check. "11 offense taps" is 7 by the table; the 24 "lights up and starts" at beat 2 and then "holds during reveals" but reads 22 at beat 3. Fix: recount and print the sum.
11. **"Draymond beat him to the spot" names an offensive player as the helper.** Minor as a typo, telling as a risk: the announcer line is the concept's main clarity device and it is wrong in the concept's own example. Fix: the line is built from the piece that moved, never typed by hand.
12. **Scripts freeze after three beats and must be authored for every play.** Minor. Fix: the script loops or the fourth beat is "find the open spot," and the authoring cost goes on the board as its own row.

## Strengths

Only what the table shows.

- Beats 3, 5 and 7: one row, three verbs, and the price written on the chip before the pick ("3 · HARD · CONTESTED", "GREEN (traffic, easy card)"). That is the choice row he described and it is the most readable thing in any of this.
- Beats 4 and 8: the announcer line names the read, "beat him to the spot," "He bit!" That is the fun moment and it is a sentence, not a coordinate.
- Beat 10: three cues at once for the change of possession, buzzer, colour roll, rows swapping shape. Nobody misses it.
- Beats 2, 4, 6 and 8: the reveal is the same two seconds every time, defender's step first, then the ball. Learnable by the second possession.
- Beat 4: a drive into a wall costs a beat, not the ball. The defense taxes, it never forbids. The possession keeps moving.
- Beats 1 to 10: the camera never moves and every pass target is on screen at every moment. His zoom complaint is answered by design, not by a fix.
- Beat 1 to beat 9: seven offense taps against 16 to 20 today, all of it from putting the off-ball men on rails. Whatever wins, that saving should survive.

## Verdict

**KILL,** as an answer to this brief, and harvest it. Three reasons. First, he asked for a back and forth like chess where the defense gets a turn to respond, and this concept's one idea is that nobody gets a turn and the defense guesses; that is not a repair, it is the concept. Second, its central promise, that nobody waits, is false under its own three second rule: the defense waits the offense's whole think every beat, with a dark row, then panics for three seconds, four or five times a possession, and on a shared phone the lock is rock-paper-scissors with thumb-watching on a court one player reads upside down. Third, it keeps the size of the one defensive answer the research stood behind and discards the timing that made it fair, while removing the free off-ball move he reaffirmed this week. What should survive into whichever concept wins: the three-verb row with the price on the chip, the tags over the players (OPEN, TRAFFIC, CONTESTED), the two-second reveal that plays the defender's step first, the three-cue possession change, the off-ball men on rails from the once-picked play with MOVE A MAN as the one hand-moved exception, and the camera that never moves. If he wants a simultaneous option on the board anyway, it needs flaws 2, 4, 5 and 6 fixed before it goes in front of him, and a paper playtest before a line of code.
