# Lock and Play

**One line:** Every beat, both of you pick at the same time, the ball side picks where the ball goes and the defense picks one call, you both lock, and the beat plays out on the court in front of both of you.

## The loop

Nobody waits for a turn, because there are no turns inside a possession. There are beats. In every beat the offense answers one question, where does the ball go: drive it down one of three lanes, pass it to one of four teammates, or shoot it from here. In the same beat the defense answers one question, what does the man on the ball do: shade left, stay home, shade right, or send help. Both sides lock their pick on their own row. The beat plays: the defender's step lands first, then the ball moves, then one announcer line says what happened. The four players without the ball, on both teams, run the play that was picked once at the start of the quarter, so nobody taps them. It repeats, snap after snap, until a shot goes up. A shot is a question card. A make, a miss that the defense rebounds, a steal, or the shot clock hitting zero ends the possession; a steal or a defensive rebound keeps the ball live and the rows simply swap ends.

The thing that makes the guessing a read and not a coin flip: a guess never decides the ball. A guess only moves the price one step. Guess right and the drive stops short or the shot is contested. Guess wrong and the lane is a free walk or the shot is open. The question card still decides who scores. Nobody ever loses the ball to a guess.

## Move then shoot?

No, and in this shape the question dissolves rather than being ruled. Each beat moves the ball once: drive, pass, or shoot. A drive lands you on a tile; shooting from it is the next beat. The defense never needs a separate turn to answer the shot, because its call for the shooting beat is made at the same moment as the shot. His instinct, "if they can move then shoot the defense doesn't really have the option to respond", is fully honoured: the defense responds to every shot, always, by definition. And the offense loses nothing it should keep, because a catch-and-shoot is two beats that take six seconds together.

What the defense gets on every beat: one small, capped move by one man, the four-call row, landing before the ball moves. That is the size the research found fair. What it gives up: it commits without seeing the pick. What it gets back for that: its wrong guesses cost only position, never the ball, and its right guesses are the best moments in the game, "Draymond beat him to the spot."

## Why it reads clearly

There is one state for the whole game and it repeats: your row is lit, so pick; your row goes dark, so you are locked and the other side has three seconds; the rows vanish, so watch the court; a card fills the screen, so answer it or watch them answer. A first-time player never asks "is it me?" because it is always them until they lock, and the row at their end tells them which side they are on by its shape: name chips and lanes mean you have the ball, four calls mean you are guarding. There is no sequence to memorise. There is no hand-off to miss. His doubt, "the offense moves, then the defense moves, then the offense chooses to shoot or pass, then the offense goes again", is the exact tangle this removes: nobody goes first, nobody goes again, every beat is the same snap.

The court says who has the ball with one device: the ball handler wears a ring in his team's colour, and the shot clock top right wears the same colour. The clock is the biggest thing on the screen after the court, amber at ten, red and pulsing at five, a tick each second from five, a buzzer at zero. The change of possession is a buzzer, a colour roll across the court, and the rows swapping shape at each end, three things at once so it cannot be missed. On a shared phone the buzzer is the same for both; on two phones the announcer line says whose ball it now is in words, "Lakers ball", never a coordinate.

The hand-off between beats is the reveal. Both rows fade, a cone of light follows the defender's step and then the ball, the announcer says it in one line, and the rows come back lit. It is the same two seconds every time, like a card flip, so it becomes a rhythm: pick, lock, watch, pick, lock, watch.

The screen carries only the beat: the announcer line, the score small, the clock, the court with the handler ringed and the lanes and tags, and one row. No step ladder, no tray, no "tap a player" note, no dock strip, no zoom. The camera never moves on a pick, so every pass target is always on screen (his row 237 complaint). Only the reveal gets a light cone, and the whole court is back before the rows relight.

On one shared phone, plainly: the phone is held the long way between the two of you, the posture he already ruled for the buzz races. Your basket to defend is at your end; you attack toward your friend. Each end has its own row facing its own player. The offense's chips and the defense's calls are all at the ends; nobody ever taps the court, so a pick never shows on the shared surface. The instant you lock, your row goes dark, so the screen holds no trace of what you picked. Could your friend watch your thumb? Yes, exactly as they could watch your hand in rock-paper-scissors across a table. Two things keep that from mattering: the defense has only three seconds after the offense locks, so a defender watching thumbs is not picking; and a guess moves a price one step and never decides the ball, so a peek at best buys a contest. The card faces whoever is answering; the announcer line is drawn once per end, both ways up, as the ruled question sandwich already does.

On two phones, the screen is the same with your end at the bottom and the other end reduced to one word, PICKING, then LOCKED, then the three-second ring. Against the computer, the computer locks the instant your row lights, and the check that ships with it proves it never reads your pick.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

Every beat is a small reveal that both of you are watching for, which is the thing rock-paper-scissors gets right and slow turn-based games lose. The pleasure is not the guess; it is the moment the court shows whether you read them. "He bit" and "beat him to the spot" are the two best sentences in basketball and this shape produces one of them every six seconds.

It is a read, not a flip, because everything except the pick itself is public: the three lanes are drawn on the court with their prices, the hatch mark shows which lane a defender can reach this beat, the open tags show who the help left, the once-picked plays on both sides are on the court as scripts you have already seen run, and the announcer line keeps the last calls in words, so tendencies are readable the way a poker player reads history. The four calls pay off against four different shapes: shade left beats the left drive, shade right beats the right drive, stay beats the shot, help beats the deep drive but opens the kick-out. No two calls do the same job, so there is always a reason to pick one over another, and a player who keeps calling STAY will get driven on, and know why.

It is fast because there is no waiting. The longest anyone sits without a decision is the 15 seconds of a card, and on a contested shot the defense has its block card inside that window. The taps per beat are one or two, the beat resolves in two seconds, and a possession is four or five snaps. It is short because a drive that hits a wall costs a beat, not a card, so the offense keeps moving instead of stalling, and the 24 makes stalling impossible anyway.

It rewards knowledge twice: knowing the answer on the card, and knowing basketball on the row. Someone who knows help defense will call HELP when the drive is deep and STAY when the shooter is hot, and that is the game's name paying off in a second place.

## Fit with the locked rules

- **Question gates.** Untouched. Shooting is a card priced by the tile; a crossover through a man is a card; a pass through traffic is a card. Every card is chosen by the offense with the price written on the chip before the pick. A defensive guess can remove a card (the defender stepped away) but never add one (a lane that closes stops the drive short instead of asking).
- **Eight-tile guarding and closed lanes.** Untouched. Every defender guards all eight around him; a lane two defenders gate is closed and cannot be entered. The one clarification the simultaneous beat needs: when a lane closes because of this beat's defensive step, the drive stops at the last open tile, no card, no turnover, the ball stays. The offense saw the hatch mark and took the risk.
- **The 24.** Untouched as a number. It runs while the offense is picking and holds during the two-second reveal and during any card, which has its own 15. Four or five snaps fit inside it. Zero is a shot clock violation with a buzzer and the colour roll.
- **Plays picked once.** Kept and leaned on harder. This is the one place I extend a locked decision, out loud: the play he picks once per quarter is not only a starting shape, it is a shape plus a short script, three beats of steps for the four men without the ball, then hold. That script is what moves the off-ball players with zero taps, and the small 2D board above his list is exactly where it animates. The defense's pick works the same way: a coverage shape plus how the four off-ball defenders follow. A fast break, his exception, runs the default script, everyone runs the floor.
- **Off-ball movement by hand.** The thing I would change, out loud. He said off-ball movement stays free, and he said tapping each player is too much. I take the second sentence over the first: nobody taps an off-ball player in a possession. Off-ball movement is free in the truest sense, it costs nothing and no taps. The way to redirect it is the timeout, which he has already made the way to change the play. If a playtest shows the offense missing one manual step badly, the fix is a fourth offense button, MOVE A MAN, and I would rather not start there.
- **Offense-only timeouts.** Kept. A timeout can be called from the offense's row between beats, replaces the row for the play list, and returns to the same beat.
- **Live-ball continuation.** Kept, and it is cleaner here than in an alternating game: a steal or a defensive rebound does not reset anything; the ring jumps to the new handler, the buzzer sounds, the colour rolls, and the two rows swap shape. The next snap is the same snap.
- **Contradicting the research, said plainly.** The research rejected blind pre-commitment and hidden simultaneous calls, from XCOM overwatch and Tecmo Bowl, and said not to build simultaneous resolution on research grounds. I am building it on design grounds, and the rejection does not reach this shape for two reasons: those systems were blind, and mine shows every option and every reach on the court before anyone picks; and those systems were decisive, a right guess collapsed the play, and mine only moves a price by one step while the card decides. It is unshipped, so it owes a paper playtest before code, the same precedent as the spacing call in August.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 4 to 6 picks plus 1 to 3 cards.
- Defense decisions per possession: 4 to 6 calls plus 0 to 2 block cards.
- Total taps: offense 9 to 14 (one or two per beat, one per card); defense 4 to 8. About 15 to 20 for both sides, against roughly double that today for the offense alone.
- Seconds per possession: 30 to 55, most of the spread being cards.
- Longest wait for the other player: 15 seconds (a card they are not answering), with a block card inside it on a contest; otherwise 3 seconds, the after-lock window.

## Weaknesses

- On a shared phone the hiding is social, not technical. A determined thumb-watcher gets a small edge. The three-second window and the one-step price limit it; they do not remove it.
- The four-call defense is coarse. A player who wants to move a specific defender to a specific tile cannot. The scripts move the rest. Some players will feel the defense is "on rails" until they learn that the four calls are the whole matchup.
- Two-level offense row (DRIVE then a lane) is two taps. A flat row of eight chips would be one tap and would not fit at 390 wide.
- Scripts must be authored for every play on the list, offense and defense, three beats each. That is real design labour before anything is playable.
- Online, both locks have to arrive before the reveal, so the relay adds its latency to the three-second window. Needs measuring on real phones.
- Stopped-short drives, if the defense reads well, can make a possession feel like running into a wall three times. The 24 keeps it short but does not make it feel good. The counter is that HELP always opens a man, and the tags say who.
- Wholly unshipped. Nothing in the archive proves it.
