# Three Moves, Then Shoot

**One line:** Every possession you get three moves, they get one slide after each one, and when your moves are gone you shoot from wherever the ball is.

## The loop

The offense starts each possession with three moves, shown as three balls in the strip at the bottom of the screen. A move is one thing: move one player (ball or off-ball, full role range), or pass. After every move the defense gets exactly one slide: one defender, full role range, then it is the offense again. A shot can be taken instead of any move, and when the third ball is spent the only thing left on the row is SHOOT, so every possession ends in a shot, a turnover, or the shot clock. Crossovers, contested passes and shots are priced by questions as today; a wrong answer ends the possession on the spot and the ball is live. The defense never moves on the inbound and never moves after the shot is up; its only extra is the block card on a contested look and the steal when a defender stands next to the ball. The shot clock is 24 seconds of offense decision time per possession; it stops while the defense slides and while a card is up.

**His doubt, answered first.** Yes, it is move, slide, move, slide, move, slide, shoot. That is confusing only if the player has to count turns. Here nobody counts turns. The offense counts balls: three, then shoot. The defense counts the same balls, so it always knows how many moves are left and exactly when the shot is coming. The word "turn" never appears on screen. The strip says YOUR MOVE or YOUR SLIDE, one of them, in your colour, and the row under it holds the only buttons that exist. A slide is a reply, not a turn: it is smaller (one man), shorter (8 seconds), and always the same two taps. The offense writes the sentence, the defense edits it, and there are never more than three edits.

Ten beats. Offense 9 taps, defense 6, about 44 seconds, and the two sides never wait more than a card's length on each other.

If the offense shoots early (say on beat 5, Curry pulls up), beats 6 to 8 do not exist. The defense already had its slide after beat 3, so the look it left is the look Curry takes. If a card is missed anywhere (crossover, contested pass, shot), the possession ends there with a live-ball flip, exactly as today.

## Move then shoot?

Yes, always, because the defense always gets the last slide before the shot. The ball handler can move on one beat and shoot on the next, but a slide sits between the two every time, so the defense sees where the shot is coming from and gets one man toward it. What the defense never gets is a slide on the inbound and a slide after SHOOT is tapped. The offense's counter is spacing: one slide covers one shooter, and the last pass can go to whichever of two open men the slide did not reach. That is what the plays picked at the start are for.

## Why it reads clearly

One place says whose beat it is: the strip at the bottom, in the acting side's colour, with one line of words (YOUR MOVE, YOUR SLIDE, LAST MOVE, YOUR SHOT, or the dim THEIR MOVE and THEIR SLIDE on the waiting phone). The banner line at the top says only what just happened, announcer style, and leaves. The grey note bar and the step ladder are gone; the three balls replace the ladder.

The hand-off is the same every time and it is four signals with one meaning: the strip slides out and slides back in the other colour, a two-note chime (up for you, down for them), the phone buzzes once on your side only, and the lit player changes. On defense nothing is lifted until you tap, so the empty strip and the pulsing ball handler ask the question for you.

The last move never vanishes. Whatever the other side just did stays drawn on the floor as a coloured trail from tile to tile for the whole of your beat, the way a chess app leaves the from-and-to squares lit. That is the "zoomed in on, highlighted" movement he asked for: the piece lifts, slides, lands, the camera nudges to it for half a second, and the trail stays. You never have to have been watching.

The camera obeys one rule: everything tappable is on screen. On a pass beat that is the passer and all four receivers. On a move beat it is the mover and his full reach. On a slide beat it is the ball and every defender. It frames, it never chases a single man, and it never cuts off the corner shooter.

The clock is one number, top right, in the colour of the side it is timing: 24 for the offense's possession, 8 for each slide, 15 on a card. Under five it grows, glows and ticks. At zero, a buzzer and a slam that says what happened: SHOT CLOCK, or on defense, PLAY ON (the slide is forfeited, nothing worse). Only one clock ever runs, and it is always the clock of the side whose strip is lit.

The choice row is the whole menu, Pokemon style: MOVE, PASS, SHOOT with the points and the card level under it. Every move is the same two taps: who, then where. Every slide is the same two taps. Every shot is one tap and an answer. A first-time player learns one gesture and it works for both sides.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

Three is a number you can hold in your head. Each move is a real decision because each one is answered, and the last one is a puzzle with the answer visible: they get one man, where do you put the ball so one man is not enough? The defense's beat is short and pointed: you know how many moves they have left, so the first slide is about the ball, the last slide is about the shot, and the steal is always sitting there as a gamble when you have a man on the ball. The possession has a shape (set up, set up, gamble), which the research called the thing that gives a turn a story. Every possession is between six and ten beats long and never more, so the rhythm becomes familiar within one quarter: three, slide, three, slide, shoot, flip.

The waiting player is never idle for long. A move takes five seconds, a slide four. During the card the defender is watching the same question and, on a contested look, gets the block card. That is the research's advice on disengagement: give the defender something inside the question beat, not another turn system.

## Fit with the locked rules

- Question gates: unchanged. Free tiles are free, crossovers through a guarded lane are a card at the shipped price, passes through a hand are a card, every shot is a card by tile. A wrong answer ends the possession, live ball.
- Eight-tile guarding and closed lanes: unchanged, drawn on the reach when MOVE is tapped (price on the tile, dark for closed).
- The 24 second clock: kept, running only during the offense's decision beats and pausing for slides and cards, which is what the shipped clock already does. In practice the three-move cap ends most possessions before it matters; it stays as the dawdling guard and the buzzer.
- Plays picked once: the flip snaps both sides into their chosen shapes. The inbound pass is free and unanswered.
- Offense-only timeouts: untouched; a timeout is the way to change the play.
- Live-ball continuation: a steal, a missed card or a defensive rebound flips the ball where it lies, a fresh three balls, no snap. The team that took it keeps the advantage, and a fast break is that team spending its three moves going the other way with the defense out of shape.

Three things I would change, said out loud:

1. **The defense's slide clock goes from 24 to 8 seconds.** A slide is one man to one tile with full information. Eight seconds is enough to read the three balls and pick; twenty-four lets the defense stall the offense's rhythm. Expiry forfeits the slide and play goes on, no turnover.
2. **The free off-ball step stops being a separate rule.** Today one off-ball step per turn is free and draws no slide. Here an off-ball move is just a move: it spends a ball and draws a slide like any other. It is still free in his sense (no card), and it keeps every beat the same shape, which is worth more than the one unanswered step. The five-free-moves setup is gone with the ladder.
3. **After a made basket the walk-up is skipped.** Both plays snap into the attacking half. Three moves cannot cross fifteen tiles and also run a play, and the plays picked once are half-court shapes. If the defense's chosen play is a press, the snap is the press geometry instead and the inbound pass is priced. Live-ball flips do not snap, which is where the full court and the heave still live.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 1 inbound + up to 3 moves + 1 shot = 5 at most, 3 at least (inbound, one move, shoot).
- Defense decisions: one slide per offense move, 3 at most, plus the block card on a contested shot and the steal option when next to the ball.
- Total taps: offense 9 at most (1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 answer), defense 6 at most; 15 for a full possession, 5 for a quick one. Counted from the brief's description of the shipped flow (two setup picks, inbound, up to five free moves, DONE, slide, action, answer), today runs about 19 to 20, and about 12 of them happen before the defense sees anything. I have not measured that on the live game.
- Seconds per possession: about 44 typical (the table above), about 95 if every clock is run to zero (5 inbound + 24 offense + 3 × 8 defense + 15 card + 15 block card + 12 of animation and flips).
- Longest wait for the other player: 15 seconds (a card), or up to 24 if the offense spends its whole shot clock on one move. Typical wait between your own beats: 5 seconds.

## Weaknesses

- Every possession has the same skeleton, three exchanges and a shot. That is the point for learnability and it may feel formulaic after fifty games. The lever is the number: three now, and a mode or a ratings hook could vary it later.
- Hot seat is the hard case: seven hand-offs per possession on one phone. The design counts on the phone lying between two people and the strip colour doing the work, with no "pass the phone" card. Worth a real two-person test before it is ruled fair.
- The defense always knowing the shooter on the last slide may prove strong. The offense's counter is spacing and the early shot; if playtests show contested looks on most possessions, give the offense a fourth ball before touching anything else.
- A wasted third move (moving an off-ball man when the ball handler must then shoot) is a first-timer trap. The LAST MOVE label and the SHOOT price on the row are the warning; a coach line on first occurrence may be needed.
- Nothing shipped has been verified to use this exact economy for a sport; the pieces are borrowed from games that are not basketball.

## Open questions for the owner

1. Three balls or four? Three is my recommendation; four makes a possession feel like a real half-court set but adds a beat each way.
2. Live-ball flips: the same three balls, or one extra for the fast break?
3. Should the defense be able to save a slide? A variant gives the defense two slides for three moments with STAY as the way to bank one. It adds a real decision and one more idea to learn. I left it out; it is one line to add.
4. Eight seconds per slide: right number?
5. The made-basket snap to half court: rule it, or keep the walk-up and let the offense burn moves on it?
6. Does STEAL stay on the slide row as the defense's gamble, in the shipped sudden-death shape?
7. The two-note chime and the one-side buzz on the hand-off: sound and feel you want, or a whistle?
