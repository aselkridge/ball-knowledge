# Critique: The Clock Is The Turn

Judged against brief.md, scout.md and the critic's job sheet. Plain English. Harsh by instruction.

One thing before the lenses. The brief asks what happens inside a possession, turn by turn, in a turn-based game. This concept's answer is that there are no turns. It says so on its own first page and asks to be a separate streetball mode. I judge it as written, but the reader should hold that fact through every section: the concept is a proposal for a different game, delivered in the slot reserved for an answer about this one.

## Lens 1 · The first-time player

I am a tester with a phone, 390 pixels wide, no coach, no rulebook. The concept's beat table is written for a phone lying flat on a table with two people at either end. Most of the twenty testers will not play that way; they will hold the phone upright and play the computer or a friend online. The concept never draws that screen. I walked the table twice, once as the flat phone it describes, once as the upright phone it does not.

Beat 1, ball in. The inbounder has a ring, my teammates glow, a five second count sits where the clock goes. Nothing on the screen tells me to tap a teammate. The announcer says "Curry to inbound", which describes, it does not instruct. The concept's line "the court is the choice" is the designer's hope, not a cue. Confusion one. At the other end, the defender has nothing at all on their side of the phone and is told they may "press during the count". Told by whom? Nothing on their end says so. Confusion two.

Beat 2, the catch. The 24 appears. The handler's reach lights, guarded tiles carry a tint and a price, closed lanes go dark. The choice row shows two buttons: SHOOT and PASS. Move is not on the row. The owner asked for "move your player or shoot"; here moving is a tap on the court that nothing advertises. A first-timer with two buttons reads two options. Confusion three. Then the tiles: the court is eight tiles across the phone's short side, so a tile is about 48 pixels, and a guard's three-tile reach lights up to a couple of dozen tiles, several with a tint and a number in them. I have never seen this court before and the numbers are changing (see beat 3) while I read them. Confusion four.

Beat 3, the slide. A defender walks and my lit tiles reprice, quietly, by design ("slides are quiet"). On a 48 pixel tile, a price going from one number to another while I am looking at a different tile is invisible. Do I know what just happened? No. Confusion five. On the upright phone against the computer, the defender who just moved is a figurine among nine others with a thin ring; the ring is the only tell.

Beat 4, crossover. I tap a shaded tile and the floor freezes with a slam and a card facing me. This is good. It is the clearest moment in the concept. I did not know a tap would cost a question, but that is the game and it is the same surprise the shipped game gives.

Beat 5, resume. Right: the ring is on me. Wrong: buzzer, TURNOVER slam, the row slides to the far end. On the flat phone this is a hand-off you can watch. On the upright phone there is no far end; the concept never says what a turnover looks like when both players share one bottom edge or when the other player is a computer. Confusion six, a gap not a cue.

Beat 6, the pass. The beat says "Curry taps Thompson". The row has a PASS button. Two ways to do one thing, and the concept does not say whether both work. Minor, but a first-timer will try the button, get nothing, and lose a second of a running clock.

Beat 7, help. A REACH button appears on the defense's end when a defender arrives next to the ball. A button appearing out of nowhere is at least a visible cue. Fine.

Beat 8 and 9, shot and contest. Freeze, slam, card, then the card flips to the other end for the block. Clear on the flat phone. Fine.

Beat 10, result. Made basket: the pieces walk into their shapes, the other team's inbound count starts. There is no slam and no buzzer, because the concept reserves the buzzer for the 24 and turnovers and the slam for freezes. So the most common change of possession in basketball, a made basket, is the one hand-off with no callout. The ring moves to a figurine on the other end and a count appears. The owner's exact complaint was "you never really know that a turn ended". Confusion seven, and it is the one that will happen most.

Beat 11, shot clock. Buzzer, slam, ball over. Good.

Seven confusions across eleven beats, three of them structural (no move cue, silent make, the upright phone undrawn). Is the hand-off visible without reading? At the freezes and turnovers, yes, better than any turn game could manage: the card faces you and the other end dims. At a made basket, no. On an upright phone, the concept does not say.

There is also a confusion the table cannot show because it is continuous: the floor changes while I think. The turn game's problem was not knowing a turn ended; this game's problem is that there is never a moment to catch up. For a trivia player who came to answer questions, a defender closing a lane while they are reading a price is a new kind of lost.

**Score: 4 out of 10.** The freezes are the best-read hand-offs in the round. Everything between them is unreadable for a first-timer at phone size, and the made basket is silent.

## Lens 2 · Tempo and waiting

The arithmetic, from the concept's own table.

Taps. Offense: one inbound pass, one crossover tap, one answer, one pass, one shoot, one answer: six, matching the concept's "6 to 10". Defense: two per slide, two or three slides, one contest answer: five to seven, and the concept says "6 to 12". Total 12 to 22, the concept's own figure. The shipped game is 16 to 20. So the tap count did not fall. It moved: the offense lost its setup taps and the defense gained slides. The concept's pitch is "nothing waits", not "fewer taps", and the reader should know the difference. The defense, which today taps twice a possession (a pick and a slide), now taps six to twelve times and is the busier side of the phone.

Seconds. The concept claims 25 to 40 a possession and a ceiling of "24 plus 15 plus 15". Its own table has an inbound count (up to 5), a crossover card (up to 15), a shot card (up to 15) and a contest card (up to 15) on top of the 24, which holds during every card. The true ceiling is 5 plus 24 plus 15 plus 15 plus 15, 74 seconds, the same as the shipped ceiling. The floor of 25 is real: a quick possession with one card at eight seconds is about 25 seconds. A typical possession with two cards at eight to ten seconds each lands at 30 to 40. So the average claim holds and the ceiling claim is understated by twenty seconds.

Over a game. Twenty-four possessions at 30 to 40 seconds is 12 to 16 minutes, against the shipped 18 to 30. A race to 11 needs roughly twelve to eighteen possessions, 6 to 12 minutes. That is genuinely about half the shipped time, and it is the concept's strongest number.

Waiting. "Longest wait 15 seconds, only inside a card that concerns them" is true as a single number and misleading as a picture. A possession with a crossover card, a shot card and a contest card gives the defense two 15 second spectator stretches and the offense one, back to back to back, up to 45 seconds of sitting inside one possession. The wait did not vanish; it was gathered into the cards. And "on the floor the wait is zero" cuts both ways: on the floor the thinking time is also zero, because the other player's pieces are moving while you decide.

The clocks against the beats. The 24 holds during cards, so a question never eats the shot clock. Good. But the 24 runs against reading the floor, and reading the floor is where a first-timer spends their time. A slow reader is punished before a question is ever asked. That is not tedium, it is pressure of the wrong kind for a trivia audience.

Hot seat. The concept's beat table is written for one phone lying flat, two people, both tapping at once. On a 390 pixel wide phone the court's short side is eight tiles; two adult hands over that court, at the same time, cover the ball and the lanes for each other. Multi-touch from both ends has never been built in this game and the concept admits it must be proven. The honour rule (do not touch the other side's men) is a rule for a table with a referee, not a phone between two friends. The chips alternative adds a tap to every order and takes the defense to eight to eighteen taps. Hot seat is one of the three ways to play, and this concept's hot seat is a guess.

Online. Both play in parallel, latency lands on the freeze, the concept has an arrival rule with ties to the defender. That is a fair patch and an honest admission. It will still produce contests one phone saw and the other did not.

Versus the computer. A reaction delay by level. Sensible, and the only home where the concept clearly works as drawn.

Faster or just different? Faster, roughly half the game time, and the "click move click move" tedium is gone because nobody sets anybody up. But the defense's taps went up fivefold, the ceiling is the same as today, the waiting is concentrated into cards rather than removed, and the hot seat does not physically exist yet.

**Score: 6 out of 10.** The best seconds in the round, bought with an unproven hot seat and numbers that flatter themselves.

## Lens 3 · Rules and research

The locked rules, one by one.

- Question gates on shots and crossovers: kept, and passes through traffic too. The wrong answer on a shot is contradictory inside the concept: the loop says a wrong answer is a live turnover in the defender's hands, beat 10 says a miss goes to a rebound card between the two nearest pieces, and the concept never says which a wrong shot answer is. The locked rule reads "misses / turnover". Pick one.
- The tile sets the price: kept, read at the moment of the tap. But the price is live, so the number a player read a second ago may not be the number the card asks. The concept says the tap is the commitment. A first-timer will call that a cheat.
- Every defender guards eight tiles: kept, computed live. Then two rules for one body: a walking defender's shadow moves with him and reprices lanes, but a walking defender "does not count" as a contest. Either a moving man guards or he does not. The concept should say the shadow lands when he plants, matching the contest rule, and lose the live repricing that makes lens 1 unreadable.
- Closed lanes: kept.
- The 24 second clock: changed from a per-turn clock (both sides, on file since July) to a real running possession clock, and the defense's own 24 is dropped. Said out loud. The owner asked on 09-05 for one clock, big, top right, with a buzzer, and the brief already calls it a clock for the possession, so the rule can give here. What the concept adds is that the clock runs against thinking. That is the part that turns the game into another game.
- Plays picked once, offense-only timeouts: kept. The timeout chip freezes the floor whenever the offense likes, which is also a free way to stop the clock as a lane closes. Needs a count per game; the concept gives none.
- Live-ball continuation: kept and strengthened, the clock flips colour and play continues. Good.

The research verdicts.

- No mid-beat prompts to the defender, ever. REACH is a button that appears on the defense's end mid-play and lets the defense freeze the floor at will. The concept frames it as the defense's own freeze, not a prompt, and in real time that framing has some merit. But the cost of a wrong reach is "a foul" and a foul is never priced. If the foul is cheap, a defender standing next to the ball reaches every time, and the offense's possession becomes a chain of the defense's questions. That is the research's "several small reaction windows" in a new coat, and worse, chosen by the defender.
- One informed, last-moving, small defensive answer reads fair. Abandoned. The defense here moves at the same time with partial sight of a walk in progress. The concept argues the walk is the telegraph and the seconds are the defense's answer. It is an honest argument; it is also a straight contradiction of the finding that fairness came from moving LAST with FULL information, and the concept does not name the finding it is overturning.
- Safe moves first, then the gamble: the shape survives loosely (walk, pass, then shoot) but nothing enforces it; a catch-and-shoot at the inbound is legal. The failed gamble ending the possession is kept.
- The alternating-possession closure: respected.
- Reflex only ever as upside (the owner's law behind "knowledge is the jumpshot"): bent, by the concept's own admission, and I would say broken. A slow defensive thumb leaves a lane open and the offense draws an easier card; a slow offensive thumb loses seconds of the 24 to finding a tile. A right answer still scores, but which question you face is now decided by hands. The concept puts this in front of the owner honestly. It should be the first line of its weaknesses, not the fifth item of a list.

Does it quietly reintroduce tap-every-player? No. Pieces walk into their plays on their own and you move only who you choose. It reintroduces something else: tap-a-lot for the defense, six to twelve a possession, more than any side has ever tapped in any version of this game.

Where a rule breaks, who should give: the per-turn 24 can give (the owner asked for one clock). The reflex law should not give in the main game; the concept agrees, which is why it asks to be a mode. The no-mid-beat-prompt rule should hold until REACH has a real price. The fairness finding is design reasoning on thin sources and the owner may overrule it, but he should be told he is doing so.

**Score: 4 out of 10.** The gates and the guarding survive; the reflex law, the fairness finding and the mid-beat rule do not, and REACH is unpriced.

## The owner's doubt

**No.** The doubt was "offense moves, defense moves, then offense shoots, then again, that feels confusing." This concept does not answer it; it deletes the thing he was asking about. He also said, in the same breath, "then the defense gets their turn to move, that goes back and forth until there's a flip of possession" and "it has to be really clear that it's back and forth, that part needs to be more like chess." He was asking how to make the back-and-forth clear and fluid. The concept's reply is that there should be no back-and-forth, and no chess. That is a legitimate thing to put in front of him, and the concept says out loud that it is doing it. But it leaves the question he asked unanswered for the game on the tin, and it swaps his named confusion (not knowing a turn ended) for a new one (the floor changing under you while you read it) that his twenty testers will feel from the first possession.

## Flaws

1. **It is not an answer to the brief.** Fatal for this round. The brief asks what one turn contains inside a turn-based possession. The concept proposes a real-time game and asks to be a separate mode. Fix: file it as a mode for later; do not count it as one of this round's options.
2. **Hands decide the question.** Serious, and admitted. Thumb speed on both sides sets which card comes up and whether a shot is contested. Fix: none inside real time; this is what the concept is.
3. **Hot seat on one flat phone does not exist yet.** Serious. Two people tapping a 390 pixel court at the same time, hands covering each other's view, multi-touch never built, an honour rule in place of a referee. Fix: ship the mode online and versus the computer only, or use the chips and accept the extra tap per order.
4. **A made basket flips possession in silence.** Serious. No slam, no buzzer, only a ring moving and a count starting. Fix: a slam and a sound on every change of possession, makes included; the concept's own rule "a buzzer means the ball moved sides" should cover makes.
5. **Move is not on the choice row.** Serious for a first-timer. Two buttons, SHOOT and PASS, and the most common action is a tap on the court that nothing advertises. Fix: a MOVE button that lights the reach, or a standing line under the row, "tap a tile to move", until the player has done it.
6. **REACH is an unpriced freeze the defender can spam.** Serious. Fix: one reach per possession, or a foul that gives the offense a free easy card at the line, or a reach only after the defender has stood adjacent for a full second.
7. **The upright phone is never drawn.** Serious. The beat table's hand-offs ("the row slides end to end", "the other end dims") have no meaning when one player holds the phone alone against the computer or online. Fix: write the upright-phone beats; they are where nearly everyone will play.
8. **Live repricing on 48 pixel tiles.** Serious. Numbers change on tiles while a first-timer reads them. Fix: price only the tile under the thumb, on the choice row, and let the tiles carry tint alone.
9. **Wrong shot answer is both a turnover and a miss with a rebound card.** Minor, a contradiction inside the concept. Fix: pick one.
10. **A walking defender guards but cannot contest.** Minor. Fix: the shadow lands when he plants, same as the contest.
11. **The ceiling is understated.** Minor, honesty. The contest card and the inbound count are left out; the true ceiling is 74 seconds, the same as today.
12. **The timeout stops the clock for free.** Minor. Fix: a count per game.
13. **The coach cannot teach while the floor runs.** Minor, admitted. Fix: tips at freezes and dead balls only.

## Strengths

Only what the beat table shows.

- Beat 1: all ten pieces walk into their picked plays on their own; the offense's setup tapping is gone entirely. This is the direct answer to "click move click move".
- Beats 4 and 8: the freeze. A slam across the floor, the card facing the person who answers, the other end dimmed. The clearest hand-off in the round, because the phone does it with its face rather than with a message.
- Beat 5: the turnover. Buzzer, slam, the row sliding to the other end. A change of possession you can watch happen.
- Beat 11: the shot clock buzzer as the only hard ending of a possession; no DONE button anywhere.
- Beat 6: SHOOT reading "3 · hard" for the tile under the ball. One button tells the price.
- Beats 2, 6, 10: the announcer speaks in names about the ball and never in tiles.
- Beat 3: the walking figurine as an honest telegraph, which the research praised in other games.

## Verdict

**KILL** for this round, with salvage. The brief asked for the turn inside a turn-based possession, and this concept's answer is to have no turns, which the owner did not ask for and which contradicts the words he used ("back and forth", "more like chess", "then the defense gets their turn"). It breaks the law that hands never decide, it makes the defense the busiest side of the phone, it has no working hot seat, it is silent on the commonest change of possession, and it never draws the upright phone most testers will hold. What it does well is real and should be lifted into whichever turn concept wins: pieces walking into their plays with no setup taps, the freeze with a slam and a card that faces its answerer, the announcer in names, SHOOT carrying its price, and the buzzer as the only hard end. The streetball mode itself is worth a row on the board for later, judged then on its own terms, priced with a real hot seat and a real REACH cost.
