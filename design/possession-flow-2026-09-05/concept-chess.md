# One Piece, One Move

**One line:** Every turn is one piece doing one thing, and the other side always gets one move back before you do anything else.

## The loop

The offense's turn is one of three things: move one player (with the ball or without), pass, or shoot. The defense's turn is one thing: move one defender (or, if a defender is standing next to the ball, try the steal). After every single thing the board hands over. There are no phases inside a possession: no setup step, no free-moves step, no slide step, no action step. The possession is just a string of single moves, offense, defense, offense, defense, like chess, and the shot is the move that ends it. The shot can be taken on any offensive turn from wherever the ball is; it is the one move the defense cannot answer, which is why the defense always gets the move right before it. The possession ends on a made shot (dead ball, the other side inbounds, both teams snap into their chosen plays), a missed shot or a wrong answer (the defense takes it live), a steal (live), or the shot clock horn (dead ball).

## One possession, beat by beat

Two friends on one phone. The Warriors have just been scored on by the Grizzlies. The whole screen at every beat is four things and nothing else: the announcer line across the top, the score small in the top left, the clock big in the top right, the board, and the choice row at the bottom. The screen has a thin colour rim all the way round in the colour of the side whose move it is. Every hand-off is the same half-second "clack": the rim colour wipes to the other side, the clock swaps to that side's colour and number, the next piece in hand lifts off its tile with a ring under it, and a chess-clock click sounds. Nothing else ever announces a turn.

Five offensive decisions (inbound, move, pass, shoot, answer), three defensive moves, thirteen taps, about thirty-six seconds. A possession that goes to the shot clock is longer: about six offensive moves, six defensive moves, and the horn.

## Move then shoot?

No. Not in the same turn, ever, and this is the design's whole answer, not a limitation of it.

The reason is the owner's own worry, said back to him: "if they can move then shoot then defense doesn't really have the option to respond." Exactly. In a strict alternation the only thing that makes the defense's one small move feel fair is that it is always the last move before the shot, taken seeing everything. Take that away once and the defense's move never matters at the moment that matters. Every version of the game that has felt unfair so far was a version where the offense did two things and the defense answered one of them.

What the offense gives up is nothing it needs. "Move then shoot" still exists; it just costs two turns: move, they answer, shoot. And a crossover already is a move-and-beat-your-man in one turn, priced by the card, so driving past a defender does not need a second action bolted on. What the defense gets is a guaranteed look before every shot, and a real choice at that look: close out on the shooter, or stay in the lane, or go for the steal.

What the offense gets in return is that the shot is always one tap away from wherever the ball is, on any turn. Nobody can ever be locked out of shooting by the flow. The skill of the game becomes making the defense's one move not enough: put the ball where two defenders both have to move to fix it. In chess that is a fork. In basketball it is spacing. That is the game the owner has been describing since the first spacing rules.

## Why it reads clearly

The owner's doubt is that "move, then defense, then shoot, then the offense goes again" feels confusing. It feels confusing because he is picturing it as one three-part offensive turn with a defensive move wedged into it. That is what the shipped game is: setups, ball in, free moves, slide, action, a ladder of different kinds of turn. The fix is not a clearer ladder. The fix is no ladder. In this design there is no such thing as "then." There is only your move, their move, your move. Nobody playing chess is confused that white moves again after black. The offense is not "starting over" after the defense moves; it is making its next move on a board that remembers.

So a first-time phone player learns exactly two things and they are the same two things on both sides: the lifted piece is yours to move, and the dots are where it can go. Whose turn it is comes from three signals that are always in agreement and never in words: the colour of the rim around the screen, the colour and number of the clock, and which piece is lifted. Whose turn it is is spoken once, at the hand-off, by the half-second YOUR MOVE flash in the squad's colour, and never again. What to do is the choice row, and only the buttons that can be tapped are lit: PASS alone on the inbound, MOVE PASS SHOOT with prices when you have the ball, MOVE alone on defense until a defender stands next to the ball and STEAL lights.

The hand-off is the clack: rim wipe, clock swap, piece lift, click, all inside half a second, the same every single time, both sides, against the computer too (the computer's move plays with the same clack so the rhythm is learned from the first possession). The last move stays painted on the board, from-tile and to-tile tinted in the mover's colour, until the next move replaces it. That is the one thing 3D chess apps all do and it is the direct cure for "sometimes defense moves and you don't even realize it's your turn again": the move that just happened is on the floor in their colour, and your rim is gold.

The camera never zooms on a tap. It holds the attacking half with all ten pieces in frame and drifts only to keep the ball and the rim in view. The lift and the ring are the highlight; the camera is not. That closes the pass-target problem in one stroke: every receiver is always on screen because the whole half always is.

The announcer never instructs. It only reports, one line, replaced every beat, in the NBA Street voice: "Curry drifts to the wing." "Smart trails him." "Swing to Curry!" "Curry from deep... GOOD!" It reads like a game, not a manual, and it is the same line on both phones, so online both players are watching one story.

The clock is the chess clock: one number top right, always in the colour of the side on the move. On offense it is the 24, and it runs only while the offense is deciding; it freezes at every clack and during every card. On defense it is a fresh 6 every move. Under five it swells and glows; at zero the buzzer. A defense that lets its 6 run out simply forfeits that move (the pieces stay), so the offense is never held up longer than six seconds. A shot clock that runs out is the horn and the stamp: "Shot clock! Grizzlies ball."

## Why it is fun, not tedious

Every tap is a decision. There is no tap in this design that is a chore. The shipped complaint was "click each player, move, click move, click move" because moving all five was free and skipping it was a waste, so everyone moved everyone. Here moving a piece costs the one thing that matters, the tempo: you hand the other side a move. So you move the piece that matters and nobody else, which is what a real offense does anyway.

The back-and-forth is the fun, and it is fast because each side's turn is small: one or two taps, three or four seconds. The defense turn in particular is quick and never dead: its one move is always an answer to something it just watched, with full information (the research's Kill Team finding, and Into the Breach's: a small, fully informed reaction reads as tactical, not as a crumb). The steal being available only when a defender is next to the ball gives the defense a gamble to hunt, which gives it a reason to press.

The shape of a good possession is the Blood Bowl shape the research liked: safe moves first, the gamble last, and the gamble ends the turn. Here that is built into the grammar rather than taught: every move is safe (or priced by a card you chose), and the shot ends the possession.

And the deep game is real. Because the defense gets exactly one move per offensive move, a possession is a tempo fight. A pass to the corner is a threat that demands a close-out; if the close-out leaves the lane open, the drive is the punish. A range-three point guard against a range-one center is a mismatch you can hunt, and the defense feels the mismatch on its own clock. That is basketball's actual content, delivered in chess's actual grammar, and neither side ever has nothing to do for more than a few seconds.

## Fit with the locked rules

- **The question gates.** Untouched. Shoot from a tile and the tile sets the points and the difficulty; the crossover tile is priced on the dot before you tap it; a pass through traffic or under pressure shows its price on the receiver's ring. A wrong answer on the shot is a miss the defense takes live; a wrong crossover or pass is a turnover, live.
- **Eight-tile guarding and closed lanes.** Untouched, and they do more work than before: every defensive move reshapes the dots the offense will see next turn. A lane two defenders gate shows no dot. This is the chess feel exactly: your move changes their legal moves.
- **The 24 second clock.** Kept at 24 real seconds for the possession, with one thing said out loud: it runs only during the offense's turns and freezes during the defense's turns and during cards. Otherwise the defense could stall the offense's clock, which would make the defense's move a weapon of delay instead of position. The defense gets its own 6-second move clock. Both are the same top-right number in the mover's colour.
- **Plays picked once.** Exactly as ruled. At every dead-ball inbound both teams glide into their chosen plays with zero taps, then the inbound pass is the first move. After a live change of hands there is no glide; the pieces stay where they are and the new offense moves first.
- **Offense-only timeouts.** A timeout is called by tapping the score in the top left, on any offensive turn, never from the choice row (it is rare, and the row carries only the beat). It is a dead ball: the play screen opens, both sides may re-pick, then both glide into their plays and the offense inbounds.
- **Live-ball continuation.** Kept: a steal or a defensive rebound puts the ball in the taker's hands where he stands, the rim wipes, the stamp shows, a fresh 24 starts in his colour, and he moves first. The team that took the ball keeps the advantage.
- **One shipped rule I would retire, out loud:** the free off-ball step that draws no defensive answer (shipped 08-11 and locked as the defensive half of that day). In a chess grammar there is no free move; the price of any move is the tempo you hand over. Keeping a free step would put two offensive things in one turn again, which is the exact shape the owner has now walked away from.
- **The shipped phases I would delete whole:** the per-possession setup pick, the free-moves round, the DONE button, the single slide phase, the step ladder, the grey note bar, and the camera zoom on tap. The plays-once ruling has already killed the first; the rest are the ladder this design replaces.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 4 to 7 (inbound, one to four moves or passes, the shot, the answer). Typical 5.
- Defense decisions per possession: 3 to 6. Typical 4. Each is a two-tap move or a one-tap stay or steal.
- Total taps: 12 to 20. Typical 14. The shipped possession spends about that many before the card is even reached, most of them on off-ball players nobody needed to move.
- Seconds per possession: 30 to 45 typical; 24 offense clock at most, about 3 to 4 seconds per defensive move, 6 to 10 on the card, 3 on the hand-offs.
- Longest wait for the other player: the card, up to 15 seconds and usually 6 to 10; the defender next to the shooter has his block card inside it. Every other wait is 6 seconds or less by construction.

## Weaknesses

- **The catch-and-shoot never beats the close-out by itself.** The defense always gets a step after the pass. A basketball player may feel that is wrong the first time it happens. The answers are the fork, the mismatch, and the fact that contested is not denied (the release meter is upside only). It wants a playtest count: how often is a shot open at the moment of release, and does that number feel like basketball. If it is too low, the first lever is defender range, not the grammar.
- **A ditherer hears the horn.** Six moves fit in 24 seconds only if each takes four seconds. A first-timer who studies the board will run out. The swelling clock and the always-available SHOOT are the mitigation; the shot clock is the teacher.
- **Defense has one verb.** Move, plus the steal when adjacent. It is fast and it is a real choice, but a possession spent purely reacting can feel thin. The research's standing advice applies if playtests show it: give the defender more inside the card beat (the block card already does this), not more phases.
- **Online, every move is a round trip.** Eight to twelve exchanges a possession. The 6-second defensive clock has to include the network, so it may need to be 8 online.
- **Off-ball moves are expensive.** A screen or a cut costs a full tempo, so set plays cannot be "run" inside a possession the way the setup shapes suggested; the play is the starting shape and the rest is improvised. That matches the research (starting shapes only), but it is a change of feel from the shipped setups.
- **Tap count is not tiny.** It is comparable to the shipped possession. What changed is that none of the taps are a chore. If the owner wanted fewer taps outright, the only place to cut is the defense's two-tap move (tap a dot with the man on the ball already in hand is one tap; that is most defensive moves).

## Open questions for the owner

1. The defense's own move clock: 6 seconds with a forfeited move at zero, or should the 24 simply run through both sides the way a real shot clock does (real, but it lets the defense stall the offense)?
2. Is an off-ball move a full turn (this design), or do you want your 08-11 free off-ball step back inside the ball handler's move? I recommend the full turn; the free step is the two-things-per-turn shape you walked away from.
3. May the defense stay put (tap the lifted defender's own tile), or must it move something every turn?
4. The inbound as the first move (one tap, a 5-second count, a real pass that can be pressured) or automatic to the play's designated receiver?
5. After a live steal or rebound, the taker moves first (this design) or the new defense gets one move first?
6. The announcer: text only, or a short bark with it on the big moments?
7. The one-second stamp at a change of possession (WARRIORS BALL) plus the half-second YOUR MOVE flash at every hand-off: is that one thing said once, or is the stamp one too many?

## Borrowed from

- **Chess**, all of it: one piece, one move; legal moves shown as dots on the board; the last move left painted on the board in the mover's colour; the piece in hand lifted; the chess clock, one number in the colour of the side to move; pass-and-play on one phone. The 3D chess apps the owner named do exactly these things and nothing more.
- **Guild Ball**, named in the defense research as the negative-space proof: every game that reached true move-for-move fairness did it by abolishing the team turn and alternating single pieces. The owner's new direction points straight at it, so this design does it on purpose rather than by accident.
- **Kill Team's answer move**, the research's closest shipped match: one defender, small and capped, moving last with full information. Here it is not a special rule; it is every defensive turn.
- **Into the Breach**: a few small, fully informed reactions against a fully revealed board are praised on a phone; the cost is think time, so the legal set stays small (one defender, his range).
- **Blood Bowl's shape**: safe moves first, the gamble last, and the gamble ends the turn. Built into the grammar rather than taught.
- **Strat-O-Matic**: a possession is one matchup, the ball handler against his man. On both sides the piece in hand by default is exactly that pair.
- **Pokemon's choice row**, at the owner's request: the verbs at the bottom, only the live ones lit, the price on the button.
- **NBA Street's announcer**, at the owner's request: one line, what just happened, never what to do.
- **Basketball itself**: the fork is spacing, the mismatch is range, the close-out is the defense's one step, and the shot clock is the teacher.
