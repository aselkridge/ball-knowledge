# Build, Answer, Finish

**One line:** You set the floor with one move, they answer with one move, you take the shot; every possession is those three words and then the ball changes hands.

## The loop

The offense gets two decisions a possession, never more. First the BUILD: one gesture, either move one player (ball or off-ball, full role range, a crossover priced by a question if it goes through a defender) or pass the ball once (priced if it goes through traffic). Then the defense gets its one ANSWER: seeing the whole floor and the price of every shot on it, it moves one defender up to his range, or stays. Then the FINISH: the offense shoots from where the ball is, or passes once to a teammate who shoots from where he stands; the shot is the question card, and a contested shooter answers a question one step harder. The finish always ends the possession: a basket (dead ball, the other side's play snaps in and their build starts), a miss (the rebound goes to the body nearest the rim, live ball, that side builds at once), or a wrong answer anywhere (turnover, live ball, the other side builds at once). It never starts over.

## Move then shoot?

Yes, across the two beats, never inside one. The ball handler can drive in the build and shoot in the finish, but the defense's answer sits between the two, made with the whole floor and every price in view. That is exactly the owner's worry answered: the defense always gets to respond to the move, because the move and the shot are in different beats by rule. The defense gets one informed move at full role range (the research's one capped move that comes after, with full information), and it gets a price on every contested shot: a defender on one of the eight tiles around the shooter makes his question one step harder, shown on the chip before the offense taps. What the defense does not get is a second look after the pass, and it does not need one: the pass moves the ball, not the men, so every catch-and-shoot the offense can take was already on the board, chip and all, when the defense chose its answer.

## Why it reads clearly

**His doubt, answered directly.** The offense picks a player and moves them: yes. Then the defense picks a player and moves them: yes. Then the offense shoots or passes: yes. Then the offense goes again to start it over: no, never. The finish ends the possession every time, with a basket, a miss, or a turnover, and the next thing on screen is the other side's build. It is three words in a fixed order, build, answer, finish, and a player only has to learn that the shot is the third thing you do. Basketball already says it that way: set it up, they adjust, take the shot.

Whose turn it is comes from three things that always agree, and no words that repeat them. The pieces: your five are bright and ringed only when it is your turn; the other five are dim but fully visible so you can read the floor. The clock: the face top right wears the colour of the side whose turn it is and counts their number (24 for the offense's two beats, 8 for the answer, 15 for a card); when it turns your colour, it is you. The buttons: the bottom row only holds buttons you can press right now, and the same three verbs sit there every offensive beat, MOVE · PASS · SHOOT, with the lit ones telling you which beat you are in. The instruction is the button's own label ("MOVE · who?", "MOVE · where?"), said once, in the one place you are about to tap. There is no step ladder, no grey bar, no coach card inside a beat, no second line saying "tap a player."

The hand-off is a fixed ritual with sound, so it cannot be missed on a shared phone or a quiet one. The move that just happened replays with the camera leaning on the piece and both tiles glowing, the announcer says it in one plain line, then a full-width slam in the new side's colour names the side and the beat (**SHOWTIME · YOUR ANSWER**) with a whistle, the lights flip, and the clock changes colour and number. Four signals, two seconds, every time. A change of possession uses the same ritual with the horn instead of the whistle and the words **TOWN BALL**. The clock glows and pulses from 5 down and the buzzer at 0 is a turnover said out loud ("Shot clock! Showtime ball."). The camera never zooms during a decision: the whole half court stays on screen whenever there is something to tap, so every pass target is reachable; it leans in only for replays and the shot itself.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

Every tap is a decision, none is bookkeeping. The build is a real choice with a visible price on every tile. The answer is a small chess puzzle with everything revealed: five shooters, five prices, one move to make the best of them worse, and the chips update as you hover so the puzzle solves in your hand. The finish is the gamble, and it comes last, so a possession has a shape (the research's Blood Bowl finding: safe moves first, the gamble at the end, a failed gamble ends the turn). Each possession is one matchup story told by the announcer (the research's Strat-O-Matic finding), and it runs in about 30 seconds, so a race to 11 is a handful of minutes and the back-and-forth is fast enough to feel like chess with a shot clock. The defender's longest wait is the shot card, and on that card they are reading the same question and rooting for a miss, which is the research's own advice for the waiting player (something to do inside the question beat) with nothing to tap.

## Fit with the locked rules

- **The question gates.** Kept whole: the shot from a tile at that tile's points and difficulty, the crossover through a defender, the pass through traffic. Movement is movement, the cards are the price. One change, said out loud: a contested shot is priced in the question's tier (one step harder per defender on the eight tiles around the shooter, shown on the chip before the tap) and the defender's block card and the release meter leave the finish. Reason: they are a prompt to the non-acting player in the middle of the other side's beat, the one thing every game in the research bled on, and they double the seconds of every contested shot. Aaron's own 08-02 correction asked for a graduated price; this is that price with nothing else.
- **Eight-tile guarding.** Kept exactly; it is what makes the answer matter, since one slid defender changes the price of up to three shooters.
- **Closed lanes.** Kept for moves and for passes: a pass line through two defenders' guarded tiles is refused, drawn dark.
- **The 24 second clock.** Kept as the offense's clock: it runs across the build and the finish and does not reset between them, so a slow build eats the finish; it holds during the defense's answer (which counts its own 8) and during any card (which counts its 15). Zero on the offense's clock is a turnover with the buzzer. An offensive rebound resets it to 14, as in the real game.
- **Plays picked once.** Kept and leaned on: the play is the build's starting shape at every dead ball, so one gesture is enough to make a possession different from the last. One change, said out loud: the ball is live in the play's named receiver's hands at the whistle, and the inbound tap goes away. Reason: a tap that decides nothing is a third decision, and the press plays still work because they put defenders up the floor where the build's first move is priced. If Aaron wants the inbound pass back as a decision, it is the build verb PASS from the inbounder and the possession costs one more beat.
- **Offense-only timeouts.** Kept: the small Timeout chip shows only during the offense's build, the one dead-ball moment the offense owns.
- **Live-ball continuation.** Kept and made the rhythm's reward: a steal, a deflection, a shot-clock buzzer or a defensive board hands the ball over where it lies, the slam says whose ball, and that side's build starts at once with no play snap.
- **Role ranges.** Kept for both the build move and the answer (point guard 3, wings and forwards 2, center 1).
- **Rebounds**, not in the brief's locked list but sudden-death cards in the shipped game: the miss goes to the nearest body to the rim, ties to the defense, announced. Reason: it keeps the defender free of mid-beat prompts and makes where the center stands worth a play. Named as an open question below.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 2 (build, finish), plus the card answers (1 to 3).
- Defense decisions per possession: 1 (the answer), and 0 prompts anywhere else.
- Total taps, both sides: 7 to 11 for a full possession (build 2 or 3, answer 1 or 2, finish 1 or 2, shoot 1, cards 1 to 3). Shipped today: about 18.
- Seconds per possession: 25 to 35 (build 6, hand-off 2, answer 5, hand-off 2, finish 4, shot card 8 on average, result 3; more if a crossover or pass card fires).
- Longest wait for the other player: the finish plus the shot card, about 19 seconds at the worst, 12 typical, with the same card on their screen.

## Weaknesses

- No swing-swing-swing possessions: two passes at most (one in the build, one in the finish), and every possession ends in a shot or a turnover. Probing the defense over several passes is not a thing this game does. If playtests miss it, the fix is one optional extra build that costs shot clock, never a loop.
- The defense has one decision for every two of the offense's. The research says information, not volume, buys fairness, and the chips are that information, but this is the number to measure in the first hot-seat games: if the defense feels thin, its answer becomes two defenders one tile each, still one beat.
- The three-word rhythm is the same every possession. The variety has to come from the plays, the prices and the announcer; if the plays are few, it will feel rote by the second quarter.
- Retiring the block card, the meter and the rebound card removes three moments the shipped game already has and Aaron has seen. That is a lot of shipped feeling to take away on one ruling.
- On a shared phone, the hand-off ritual is load-bearing. If the two seconds feel slow after twenty possessions, a swipe-to-skip on the replay is the escape, but the slam and the sound stay.
- The chips add five small labels to the board during the answer and the finish. They must stay legible on a tilted court at phone size; that needs a render test before anyone plays it.

## Open questions for the owner

1. Contest as a price on the chip, or keep the block card as the defense's one mid-possession card?
2. Rebounds by position (nearest body, ties to the defense), or a rebound card both players answer?
3. Is PASS a build verb (two passes a possession at most) or is the build movement only?
4. Is 8 seconds right for the answer, and should the game clock run only while the shot clock runs?
5. The dim SHOOT button during the build is tappable and means "no move, they answer, then I shoot": teaching device or trap?
6. Automatic inbound at the whistle, or the inbound pass as the first build gesture?
