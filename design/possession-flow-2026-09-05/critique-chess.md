# Critique: One Piece, One Move

Judged against brief.md, scout.md, the concept file, and the shipped rules in DESIGN.md (sections 2, 3 and 4, read again for this critique: the role ranges, the per-turn 24, the live contest rule and the block card).

## Lens 1 · The first-time player

Walked as a tester on a 390 pixel phone, sharing it with a friend, never read a rulebook. The concept's promise is that I learn two things (the lifted piece is mine, the dots are where it goes) and the same two things work on both sides. That promise mostly holds. The chess picture is one almost everyone already has in their head, and "lifted piece plus dots" needs no reading. Where it breaks is at the seams the concept did not draw.

Beat 1 (the glide and the stamp). I know it is Warriors ball because the stamp says so. I do not yet know that gold means Warriors for the rest of the game; the stamp is the only time colour is tied to a name in words. No confusion yet, but the colour key is being taught in one second.

Beat 2 (the inbound). Row says PASS, but the table says I tap Green directly and the rings are already showing. Fine, until beat 6, where a pass is tap PASS, then tap the receiver. Two pass grammars in one possession. Confusion one. Add a 5 second count and five rings in three colours I have never seen before: I will tap the nearest ring, not the best one. Not confusion, but not a choice either.

Beat 3 (first defensive move). The rim goes blue, a flash says YOUR MOVE for half a second, Ja is lifted, a 6 second clock starts. On one phone the half second is spent turning the phone toward my friend; the flash is gone before the phone lands. My friend has 6 seconds to learn that the lifted man is his, that dots are legal, that he may tap a different defender first, and that staying put is done by tapping the lifted man's own tile. Nobody discovers "tap your own tile to stay" in six seconds. If he dithers, the clock hits zero, the pieces stay, the rim wipes gold. He experiences that as the game skipping him. Confusion two (the six second clock on a first defensive turn) and confusion three (stay is invisible).

Beat 4 (moving Curry). Green is lifted with the ball. I want Curry, so I tap Curry, and Curry lifts to move. But a first-timer who taps a teammate is at least as likely to mean "pass to him" as "move him". Beat 6 says passing needs PASS first. So tapping a teammate does one thing on this beat and a different thing after PASS. Confusion four. The clock line "24 running only while this side is deciding, lands on 20" is clear enough.

Beat 5 (Smart trails). Two taps, and the from-tile and to-tile stay blue afterwards. This is the best beat in the table: after it, nobody can miss what just moved. No confusion.

Beat 6 (the swing pass). Clear once the two-grammar problem is fixed. I see the ball move from Green to Curry and Curry lights. Good.

Beat 7 (STEAL lights). The row reads "STEAL · Smart on Curry" and my friend has no idea what a steal card is or what it costs him if he gets it wrong. The concept does not say either. Confusion five, and it is a rules hole as much as a reading hole (see lens 3).

Beat 8 (SHOOT with its price). "SHOOT · 3 · hard" and a CONTESTED tag before I tap. This is exactly what the owner asked for: the choices at the bottom, the price on the button. No confusion.

Beat 9 (the card). Full screen, nothing else, the ring replaces the clock. Clear for me. But the concept says the defender's block card runs "on the other side of the card" and the screen shows "nothing else". On one phone, whose hands is it in, and when does my friend get his card? Not drawn. Confusion six, in hot seat only.

Beat 10 (the flip). Made: horn, stamp, glide, the other inbounder lifts. Clear. Missed: buzzer, stamp, Bane lifted where he stands, and the Grizzlies move first. I know the ball changed hands because the stamp says so and the lifted piece is in blue. Good.

One more, across every beat: the top-right number is 5, then 24, then 6, then 20, then 6, then 17, then the 15 ring. One slot, three different clocks, told apart by colour alone. A first-timer sees a number that jumps around. Confusion seven, low-grade and constant.

Is the hand-off visible without reading? Mostly yes: a piece lifts, the last move stays painted, a click sounds. But whose turn it is rests on colour (the rim, the clock) and a half-second flash. The rim of a phone screen is the part covered by thumbs, notches and rounded corners. The owner's exact complaint was "you don't even realize it's your turn again", and the concept's answer to it is the weakest signal on the screen. It needs one persistent word.

Seven moments of confusion, three of them in the first three beats, two of them fixable with one button each. Score: 6 out of 10.

## Lens 2 · Tempo and waiting

The table's own arithmetic checks: taps 0+1+2+2+2+2+2+1+1+0 = 13, seconds 2+3+3+4+3+3+3+4+8+3 = 36. That possession is a quick one: inbound, one off-ball move, one pass, shoot. The concept admits a possession that runs to the shot clock has about six offensive moves and six defensive moves. Counting that honestly: six offensive turns at one or two taps, six defensive turns at one or two taps, the shot, the answer: about 20 to 22 taps, and about 24 (offense clock) plus 18 (six defensive moves at three seconds) plus 8 (card) plus 5 (stamps and glide) = roughly 55 seconds. The Numbers section says 12 to 20 taps and 30 to 45 seconds. The typical possession lands inside those ranges; the full-clock possession does not. Slightly rosy, not dishonest, and the concept says out loud that the tap count is comparable to shipped.

Against what is shipped (16 to 20 taps, 45 to 75 seconds): taps are about the same, seconds are 25 to 40 percent fewer on a typical possession. The difference the concept is right about is that none of its taps are a chore. The shipped possession spends most of its taps moving players nobody needed to move; here every tap hands the other side a move, so nobody moves anyone for free. That is real, and it is the concept's best argument.

Now the claim that fails. "Every other wait is 6 seconds or less by construction." False for the defender. The offense has no per-move clock, only the 24 for the possession. One offensive turn can eat all 24 seconds while the defender sits. So the defender's longest wait is 24, then the card for 15 more. The 6 second cap is one-directional: it protects the offense from the defense, not the defense from the offense. The concept should say that or add an offense per-move clock inside the 24.

The defence's 6 seconds is the other tempo problem. On 07-23 the defence clock was made 24 "because it's chess, both sides get to think". The concept turns that into 6 and does not name it as a change. Six seconds is a reflex, not a think, and on one phone it includes the physical hand-off. Eight to ten is the floor for a first-timer; the concept's own online note ("may need to be 8") suggests it already knows.

Hot seat. The count nobody has done: the concept hands the phone over at every clack, 8 to 12 times a possession. The shipped possession hands it over about four times (defence pick, offence pick, offence setup, slide, action). Over a 24 possession game that is roughly 250 hand-offs against roughly 100. Each is a half-second clack plus a second or two of two people reaching for one phone. That is a minute or two of pure hand-off per game and, more to the point, it is the exact shape the research called "several small reaction windows". The concept borrows Guild Ball and Kill Team and never mentions Hero Academy and Magic Arena. The scout notes those two rest on snippet-level sources, so the concept may be right to shrug, but it has to shrug out loud.

Online is this design's better home. Round trips are short, each side is engaged every few seconds, and the defender never sits longer than one offensive turn. The waiting-player problem the research worried about (the defender disengaging) is smaller here than in anything shipped.

Over a race to 11: about 10 to 14 possessions, roughly 7 to 9 minutes. Over 24 possessions: 15 to 18 minutes plus cards, against about 24 minutes shipped. Faster, and the shot-clock horn is a real teacher: the ditherer hears it, as the concept admits.

Is it faster and less tedious, or just different? Faster, yes. Less tedious per tap, yes. But it triples the number of times the game says "your move", and whether ten of those a possession feels like chess or like being nagged is the one thing no arithmetic settles. The numbers are mostly honest, one claim is false, one rule change is unnamed. Score: 6 out of 10.

## Lens 3 · Rules and research

The locked rules, one by one.

- Question gates on shots and crossovers: kept exactly. The tile sets the price, the crossover tile shows amber before the tap, a pass through traffic prices the receiver's ring. Nothing moved.
- Every defender guards eight tiles, closed lanes: kept, and the concept is right that they do more work when a defender moves after every offensive move. One thing is unmeasured: the shipped floor study found 45 percent of the half guarded and three clean lanes with static shapes. With a defender re-placing himself every turn, the handler's dots may close faster than the possession can develop. Nobody has counted that. It wants the floor analysis rerun with a responsive defender before a build.
- The 24 second clock: DESIGN.md section 3 says ":24 shot clock per turn", and the 07-23 change made the defence's clock 24 too. The concept makes it 24 for the whole possession, running only on offensive turns, and 6 for each defensive move. That is two rule changes. The first is said out loud (the freeze during defence turns, to stop stalling; a good reason). The second is not said at all. The scout already flagged that the owner must rule which clock the 24 is; the concept should put both changes in its "rules I would change" line, not one.
- Plays picked once, both teams glide with zero taps: exactly as ruled.
- Offense-only timeouts: kept, called by tapping the score. Two quibbles. A hidden tap target is the opposite of "the screen has only what we need". And the concept lets both sides re-pick at the timeout; the owner's words give the pick to the side that called it. Open question, not a break.
- Live-ball continuation: kept, and cleaner than shipped: the taker moves first, fresh 24, no snap.
- Move then shoot: no, never in one turn. This agrees with the owner's doubt and with the Kill Team finding (the defence's move reads fair because it moves last with full information). It is also the concept's honest answer to the pass-then-catch-and-shoot question that has been open since 08-02: the defence always gets a step after the pass. The concept names the basketball cost (the catch-and-shoot never beats the close-out by itself) and asks for a playtest count. That is the right way to handle it.

The research verdicts.

- No mid-beat prompts to the defender: the STEAL card happens on the defence's own turn, so it is not a mid-beat prompt. The block card inside the shooter's card is the shipped contest rule (DESIGN.md section 4, rule 2), which the archive's own advice endorses ("give the defender something to do inside the question beat"). Consistent. But the concept says the card screen shows "nothing else" and then puts a second player's card behind it. On one phone that is a hand-off inside a 15 second clock, and it is not drawn.
- One informed last-moving small defensive answer reads fair: the concept makes every defensive turn exactly that, one defender, his range, after seeing the move. This is closer to Kill Team's actual rule (one free capped move between each enemy action) than the shipped one-slide-per-possession ever was. Correct borrowing.
- Several small reaction windows read as unbearable: unaddressed by name. The concept's implicit answer is that these are real turns, not interrupts inside someone else's turn, which is a fair distinction. It needs to be made in the Weaknesses section, with the playtest named as the judge.
- Safe moves first, then the gamble: built into the grammar. Every move is safe or priced, the shot ends it. Correct.

New rules the concept adds without costing them.

- The STEAL card. A defender next to the ball may, on his turn, take a question to steal. What happens on a wrong answer is not stated. If the answer is "nothing", every adjacent defensive turn becomes a free lottery ticket against the handler, and the correct defensive play is to stand next to the ball and tap STEAL forever. DESIGN.md has no defender-initiated steal today (the only thing near it is the A-defender's "jump a lane" pre-commit, once per possession). This is the concept's one real balance hole. It needs a cost: a wrong answer is a reach-in foul, or the defender is frozen for the offense's next move, or one attempt per defender per possession.
- Rebounds. A missed shot goes to "the nearest Grizzly to the rim", live. DESIGN.md section 2 gives rebounding a duel window. The concept drops offensive rebounds and the duel without saying so. Minor, but it should be in the "I would retire" list, since the brief only says "wrong answer = miss / turnover" and does not cover a right-answer miss.

Does the concept quietly reintroduce tap-every-player? No. It is the one concept shape that cannot: moving a piece hands the other side a move, so nobody moves five. The price it pays is honest and named: set plays cannot be run inside a possession; the play is the starting shape and the rest is improvised. That matches the research's "starting shapes only" reading.

It keeps every lock, borrows the research's strongest adopt correctly, and does not sneak the tap ladder back. It changes two clocks and names one, adds a steal without a cost, drops the rebound duel silently, and skips the one research warning aimed straight at it. Score: 7 out of 10.

## The owner's doubt

Yes. This is the cleanest answer on the table to "offense moves, defense moves, then offense shoots, then again". The concept's reply is that the doubt describes a three-part offensive turn with a defensive move wedged in, and that the fix is not a clearer ladder but no ladder: there is no "then", only your move, their move, your move, and the shot is a move like any other. Nobody playing chess is confused that white moves again after black. The offense never "starts over"; it makes its next move on a board that remembers. That is digestible in one sentence, and the beat table proves it: every offensive beat has the same three buttons and every defensive beat has the same lifted man and dots.

The caveat: it answers the digestion doubt by multiplying the hand-offs. It trades "what phase am I in" for "how many times do we go back and forth", and it does not count the second one. The doubt as the owner stated it is answered. The doubt he will have after playing it (ten hand-offs a possession) is not yet addressed.

## Flaws

1. The STEAL card has no cost on a wrong answer. Serious (fatal if left as is: it becomes a free lottery every adjacent turn). Fix: wrong answer is a reach-in foul, or the defender skips his next move, or one attempt per defender per possession.
2. "Every other wait is 6 seconds or less by construction" is false; the offense has no per-move clock, so the defender can wait 24 seconds on one move. Serious. Fix: an 8 to 10 second per-move clock inside the 24 for the offense, or state the real number.
3. The defence's 6 second move clock is too short for a first-timer and for one phone changing hands, and it silently overturns the 07-23 ruling that the defence clock is 24 "because both sides get to think". Serious. Fix: 8 to 10 seconds, named as a change of that ruling.
4. Whose move it is rests on colour (rim, clock) plus a half-second flash, with no persistent word. This is the weakest signal on the screen aimed at the owner's loudest complaint. Serious. Fix: one word next to the clock in the mover's colour all turn long ("WARRIORS" or "YOUR MOVE"), never removed until the clack.
5. Ten hand-offs a possession on one phone, uncounted, and the research's "several small reaction windows" warning unmentioned. Serious, and inherent: it cannot be fixed without reintroducing phases. Fix: count it in Numbers, name the warning in Weaknesses, and make hot seat a playtest gate for this concept.
6. Two pass grammars: the inbound taps the receiver directly, an in-play pass taps PASS then the receiver. Minor. Fix: one grammar for both.
7. Tapping a teammate selects him to move; a first-timer will mean "pass to him". Minor, becomes serious under a shot clock. Fix: one grammar, or tap-and-hold to move, tap to pass while PASS is lit.
8. Staying put is "tap the lifted man's own tile", undiscoverable in six seconds. Minor. Fix: a STAY button in the defence's row.
9. One top-right slot carries three clocks (5, 24, 6, 15) told apart by colour. Minor, constant. Fix: the defence's 6 as a ring on the lifted defender, the 24 alone top right.
10. The block card inside the shooter's card is not drawn for one phone. Minor (it is the shipped rule), but the concept claims the card screen shows nothing else. Fix: draw the beat.
11. The rebound duel is dropped silently; misses go to the nearest defender. Minor. Fix: say it in the retire list, or keep the duel.
12. The timeout is a hidden tap on the score. Minor. Fix: a small chip beside the score, lit only on offensive turns.
13. The smothering risk is unmeasured: a defender re-placing himself every turn against eight-tile guarding may close the handler's dots faster than possessions can develop. Minor until measured. Fix: rerun the floor analysis with a responsive defender.
14. The Numbers ranges leave out the full-clock possession (about 22 taps, about 55 seconds). Minor. Fix: state it.
15. Both sides re-pick at a timeout; the owner gave the pick to the calling side. Minor, open question.

## Strengths

- Beat 5: the from-tile and to-tile of the last move stay tinted in the mover's colour until the next move. The direct cure for "defense moves and you don't even realize it's your turn".
- Beat 8: the price is on the button before the tap ("SHOOT · 3 · hard", CONTESTED), and the crossover price is on the dot before the tap. The owner's Pokemon row, done properly.
- Beats 3 and 7: the man on the ball is the piece in hand by default, so most defensive moves are one tap, and STEAL lights only when adjacent, which gives the defence a reason to press.
- Beats 2 and 6: receivers wear rings in price colour with the whole half in frame, which closes the "zoomed in, cannot see who to pass to" complaint in one stroke.
- Beat 4 and the clock line: the 24 freezes at every clack and during cards, so the defence cannot stall the offense.
- Beat 10, the miss: the taker is lifted where he stands with a fresh 24 and moves first. Live-ball continuation, cleaner than shipped.
- The table's 13 taps and 36 seconds add up, and the concept admits in its own Weaknesses that the tap count is not tiny.
- No beat moves a player nobody needed to move. Tap-every-player is structurally gone.

## Verdict

REPAIR. Not KILL: this is the one concept that answers the owner's doubt by removing the thing that causes it, it keeps every locked rule, it borrows the research's strongest adopt (one small informed move, last, after every offensive action) more faithfully than the shipped game does, and it cannot reintroduce tap-every-player. Not KEEP: the steal card is a balance hole with no cost, the "6 seconds or less" claim is false, the defence clock overturns a ruling without saying so, and the turn signal is colour plus a half-second flash aimed at the owner's loudest complaint. The repairs are small and named: cost the steal, give the offense a per-move clock or say the true wait, lengthen the defence clock to 8 to 10 and name the change, put one persistent word for whose move beside the clock, add STAY to the defence row, use one pass grammar, and count the hand-offs honestly with the research's warning beside them. What no repair settles is whether ten hand-offs a possession on one phone feels like chess or like being nagged; that is a hot-seat playtest, and it should be the gate before any build.
