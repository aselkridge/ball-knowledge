# The turn-based role-playing games, read for a phone basketball possession

The owner asked why the panel did not look at other turn-based role-playing games. This is that look, from someone who has played them, measured against the memo's flows: A "one thing, then their step", B "move one, then pass", C "three moves, then shoot", D "build, answer, finish", E "lock and play". The short answer: this family confirms A's grammar, adds one rule to it worth ruling on (from Persona 5), and settles three screen details the owner complained about. It does not produce a new flow.

Where a fact is verified I name the page. Where it is not, I say "from memory". Every game below is a fight where one side picks from a menu, then the other side acts, so the family is the nearest cousin to what the owner described ("almost like Pokemon, you have your choices on the bottom").

## Pokemon (the main games, Red through Scarlet)

**What one turn contains.** Both trainers pick one thing in secret: one of four moves, a switch, or an item. Then everything resolves in one pass, fastest first (a move with priority goes first; otherwise the higher Speed stat goes first). A switch is a whole turn: the outgoing Pokemon leaves before any attack lands, and the incoming one takes whatever was coming. Nobody moves on a board; there is no board. Verified: Bulbapedia, "Priority" ("when two moves have the same priority, the users' Speed statistics will determine which one is performed first"; "Switching fundamentally happens before any moves can be performed").

**How it shows the turn.** A two-level menu bottom right: FIGHT, BAG, POKEMON, RUN, then the four moves in a fixed two by two grid with the type and the remaining uses printed on each. A message box across the bottom narrates every result one sentence at a time ("Pikachu used Thunderbolt! It's super effective!") and waits for a press before the next sentence. The menu never appears while the box is narrating; the story finishes, then the choice comes back. Your Pokemon's name plate and health bar sit bottom right, theirs top left, and that is the only "whose side" mark on the screen. No clock in the single player games; the online ladder puts a per-turn timer on the screen.

**Worth stealing.** The sequencing rule: narration first, then the menu, never both at once. The owner's complaint "you never really know that a turn ended" is exactly what the message box prevents: the result of the last action is said, then the choice rises. The panel's announcer line should land and sit for about half a second before the row rises, every time, both sides.

**Would hurt us.** The hidden simultaneous pick with speed deciding the order. That is flow E, and the August defense research rejected hidden simultaneous calls by name. Also the press-to-continue on every sentence: on a 24 second clock that is a tap tax.

**Verdict.** Adapt: the fixed four-slot row and the narration-then-menu order. Reject the simultaneous resolution.

## Final Fantasy: Active Time Battle, Final Fantasy X's turn strip, Final Fantasy 7 Remake

**What one turn contains.** In the Active Time Battle games (Final Fantasy 4 through 9), each fighter has a bar that fills at their own speed; when it fills, their menu opens and they take one action. Turns arrive unevenly, faster fighters act more often, and in "active" mode the bars keep filling while you read the menu, in "wait" mode they pause. Final Fantasy X replaces the bar with a list: the game pauses on your turn with no timer, and the list shows who acts next and how your choice would reorder it. Verified: Wikipedia, Final Fantasy X ("a graphical timeline along the upper-right side of the screen details who will be receiving turns next, and how various actions taken will affect the subsequent order of turns"; "the CTB design allows the player to select an action without time pressure"). Final Fantasy 7 Remake is the hybrid: you fight in real time, attacks fill the bar, and when it fills you can halt the action and pick a special from a menu. Verified: Wikipedia, Final Fantasy VII Remake ("the ATB bar gradually fills up slowly, or can fill faster with attacks"; "the player can halt the action").

**How it shows the turn.** Active Time Battle: a small filling bar next to each name in a box at the bottom, and a blinking cursor arrow on the fighter whose menu is open. Final Fantasy X: the strip of portraits top right, the acting one at the top and biggest; a highlighted portrait moves down the strip when you pick a slow action. Final Fantasy 7 Remake: the world slows to a crawl and a menu slides in from the left; the slowdown itself is the "your turn" signal.

**Worth stealing.** Two things. From Final Fantasy X, the strip: a tiny order strip that says NOW and NEXT without words. For strict alternation it is two chips (your colour lit, theirs dim), and for flow C it is the three balls. From Active Time Battle's two modes: the clock runs while you read the menu (active mode) and pauses inside the question card (wait mode). That is the panel's clock rule already, offense decision time only, frozen during cards and during the defense's step.

**Would hurt us.** Uneven turns from a filling bar. It is tempting (Curry's speed rating earns him more turns than a center) but it kills the back-and-forth the owner asked for: "it has to be really clear that it is back and forth." And Final Fantasy 7 Remake's hybrid is the real-time game the memo killed and parked as Blacktop.

**Verdict.** Adapt the two-chip order strip and the clock modes. Reject the filling bar and the hybrid.

## Persona 5: One More, Baton Pass, All-Out Attack

**What one turn contains.** Strict turns, one action per fighter. Hit an enemy's weakness or land a critical and that fighter is knocked down and you get one more action at once; the enemy does not act in between. With Baton Pass you hand that extra action to a teammate, who acts with a damage bonus. When every enemy is down, the party gets a Hold Up and can launch an All-Out Attack that hits all of them. Verified: Wikipedia, Persona 5 ("If a character strikes an enemy's weakness, they knock the enemy down and are awarded an additional turn"; "If all enemies are knocked down, a 'Hold Up' is triggered"). Baton Pass details from memory.

**How it shows the turn.** A big red "1 MORE" stamp slams across the screen with a hit sound, the camera cuts to the character, and the menu snaps back open without the enemy moving. Baton Pass is a shout and a quick cut to the teammate. The All-Out Attack is a full screen comic panel. The menu is a fan of words on the left (Attack, Skill, Guard, Item...) in red on black, and the whole screen tints to the acting side.

**Worth stealing.** The One More rule is the most direct answer this family has to the owner's move-then-shoot doubt. He said "if they can move then shoot then defense doesn't really have the option to respond." Persona's answer: the defense already responded, it put a body there, and you beat it, and beating it out loud earns you the next action before help can arrive. In basketball that is the blow-by: shake your man and the shot goes up before the help gets there. Our version: a drive through a defender's tile is priced by the crossover card as today; a RIGHT answer earns ONE MORE, the row rises again in gold at once with no defensive step, capped at one per turn. A wrong answer is the turnover it already is. That makes the crossover card worth taking, which in flow A as written it barely is (go around the man instead and the defense recovers anyway). Baton Pass is the pass version and needs no extra rule: the One More can be spent on PASS, and the pass ends it.

**Would hurt us.** The chain. Persona lets you pass the extra action around the whole party; four One Mores in a row is a normal turn there. Here a chain would be drive, pass, drive, shoot with the defense watching, the exact thing the owner is afraid of. One per turn, hard cap, and never off a pass card (a threaded pass is a pass, not a beaten man).

**Verdict.** Adapt: add the One More rule to flow A as a candidate for the owner to rule; it is the one change this family makes to the recommendation.

## Paper Mario and Super Mario RPG: the timed hit

**What one turn contains.** One action per character per turn, picked from a menu. During your attack a timed press adds damage (Super Mario RPG: half again for close, double for perfect); during the enemy's attack a timed guard press cuts the damage, sometimes to nothing. The press never takes you below the base hit. Verified: Super Mario Wiki, "Action Command" ("It allows a character to do significantly more damage to an enemy than with a normal hit"; perfectly timed guards can negate damage).

**How it shows the turn.** A pointer over the acting character, the menu as a ring of icons around Mario's head (Paper Mario) or a small box (Super Mario RPG), a flash or a star when the press lands, "Nice!" text on a perfect one. The enemy's turn is signalled by the camera turning to the enemy and a wind-up animation that is the cue for the guard press.

**Worth stealing.** The guard press: the waiting side has a reflex to make inside the acting side's turn. The panel already has this shape in the block card the defender holds during the shooter's 15 seconds and in the release meter that only adds. This family says that shape works and is the reason the enemy turn in these games is never dead time.

**Would hurt us.** Putting a timed press on every pass and move. The owner's law is knowledge is the jumpshot; reflex is the seasoning, not the meal, and on a phone with a 15 second question already on the screen a second reflex per beat is noise.

**Verdict.** Adopt as confirmation: keep the release meter and the block card, add no more reflexes.

## Darkest Dungeon: positions decide what you can do

**What one turn contains.** Four heroes in a line, positions one to four. Each skill lists the positions it can be used from and the positions it can hit; a hero out of position may have nothing legal but a move. Moving forward or back is itself an action (some skills move you as a side effect). Turn order is speed plus a roll, drawn as a strip of portraits across the top. Position rules verified: Wikipedia, Darkest Dungeon ("characters in the front could have powerful melee attacks but take more damage, while characters in the back would be more protected"); turn strip and the roll from memory.

**How it shows the turn.** The strip at the top with the acting portrait lit; the skill bar bottom centre with each skill button showing four little dots for the positions it works from, lit or dim; hover a skill and the legal targets glow while the rest go dark; a torch meter over everything.

**Worth stealing.** The dots on the button. The player learns the geometry from the button, not from the floor: SHOOT reads "3 pts, hard" because of the tile you stand on, DRIVE greys with "lane closed", PASS shows a price per receiver. The panel's flows already print the price on the button (from Slay the Spire and Hoplite in the research); Darkest Dungeon is the proof that a button can carry "where you are" as well as "what it costs."

**Would hurt us.** The shuffle. Getting knocked out of position and spending turns walking back is the most complained-about thing in that game. Our equivalent would be a defense whose one step keeps forcing the offense to spend its turns re-spacing instead of playing. Flow A's answer, that a beaten defender recovers one tile only, is the right size; do not let the defensive step un-space three men.

**Verdict.** Adapt the price-and-position on the button. Reject any rule that makes the offense spend turns fixing position.

## Slay the Spire: intents shown before you act

**What one turn contains.** You get three energy and a hand of cards; play any cards you can pay for, in any order, then press End Turn. Each enemy shows, above its head, what it will do next (attack for 12, block, buff) and then does exactly that. Verified: Wikipedia, Slay the Spire ("Each opponent on the field will telegraph what move they will make"; "Each turn the player receives a fresh hand of cards and three energy points").

**How it shows the turn.** The intent icon and number above each enemy, the energy count as a big orb bottom left, one End Turn button bottom right in the same place always, cards fan across the bottom, a red glow on the enemy about to attack.

**Worth stealing.** Honesty on the board: the price of the next thing is drawn before you choose. Flow E's tags (OPEN, TRAFFIC, CONTESTED) that the memo folded into A are the same idea. And the three energy per turn is flow C's three balls, drawn as one orb that counts down, which is the cleanest cap picture in the family.

**Would hurt us.** Intents as a defensive turn. For the defense to show its intent, it would have to commit before the offense acts, which is blind pre-commitment, the XCOM overwatch problem the research found reads as luck or dominates. Intents work in Slay the Spire because the enemy is a machine with no feelings about being read.

**Verdict.** Adapt the honest price; reject intents as a way for a human defense to take its turn.

## Bravely Default and Octopath Traveler: banking and spending turns

**What one turn contains.** Bravely Default: each character has Brave Points; Default guards and banks a point, Brave spends points for extra actions now, up to four in one turn, and you may go negative, after which you stand still until the count returns to zero. Verified: Wikipedia, Bravely Default ("Party members can utilize BP down into negative figures, enabling up to four actions within a single turn"; "When the BP gauge is negative, they must wait for the BP counter to reach zero"). Octopath Traveler: one Boost Point a turn, bank up to five, spend up to three on one action; hit an enemy's weakness enough times and it is Broken, loses its next turn, and takes more damage. Verified: Wikipedia, Octopath Traveler ("may store up to five at a time"; "the enemy is 'Broken;' it loses its next turn").

**How it shows the turn.** The point count next to each name, always, and a big number that flashes when you brave or boost; Octopath draws the enemy's shield count and its weakness icons under it, with question marks for the ones you have not found.

**Worth stealing.** The Break. A beaten defender losing his next step is the honest reading of the crossover, and Octopath says it reads fair when the condition is visible. Flow A has it in the small form (one tile recovery); the One More rule above is the bigger form.

**Would hurt us.** The bank. Move-and-shoot as a spend ("brave" one turn, stand still next possession) is a real design and the panel's action-points concept already drew it. It teaches a currency to a first-time phone player under a 24 second clock, and the debt version means a whole possession where the defense gets two steps for each of yours, which will read as a bug. Also HOLD banking a step for the defense sounds free, but a defense that banks reads as passive and the offense feels stalled.

**Verdict.** Reject the bank for the first build; keep the Break as confirmation of A's beaten-man rule.

## Baldur's Gate 3: move and act in one turn, the budget on the floor

**What one turn contains.** Each character gets an Action, a Bonus Action and a movement budget, all refreshed each turn; you may spend the movement in pieces around the action (walk, attack, walk again). Leaving an enemy's reach gives that enemy a free swing at you. Resources verified: Baldur's Gate 3 wiki, Actions ("All creatures have 1 charge of each of the three resources... that recharges at the beginning of each turn"); split movement and the free swing from memory.

**How it shows the turn.** A portrait strip across the top with the acting portrait raised; a ring on the floor marking how far you can still walk, a dashed line to the cursor with the remaining metres printed, the line turning red past the ring; the enemy's free swing shown as a warning icon on the path before you commit; a big End Turn button bottom right. The camera follows whoever is acting, including enemies, and enemy turns play out at speed with a portrait banner.

**Worth stealing.** The dashed path with the warning icon on it: before you tap, the floor tells you this route crosses a man and will cost a card. Flow A's lit tiles (green free, amber priced, dark closed) are the same fact drawn as tiles; the path version is worth a look because on a 3D board at phone size a coloured path is easier to read than coloured tiles.

**Would hurt us.** Move-and-act for every piece. A full Baldur's Gate 3 turn moves and acts with each of four characters, and its own multiplayer is famous for the wait while the other player takes theirs. That is the shipped tedium in different clothes.

**Verdict.** Adapt the path-with-price drawing; reject the per-piece move-and-act turn.

## Chrono Trigger, briefly

Fights happen on the map where you stood, no battle screen; enemy positions matter for area attacks; two characters can combine into one Double Tech. Verified: Wikipedia, Chrono Trigger. Worth stealing: the pick and roll as a Double Tech, one button that moves two players inside one turn, which is the only form of "move two pieces in one turn" this family blesses because it is one named action, not two. Would hurt us: nothing; it is the game that proves the court can stay the screen. Verdict: adapt, later, as a play-list device, not a flow change.

## DOES IT CHANGE THE RECOMMENDATION

**No new flow. A stays, with one rule added for the owner to rule on, and B stays the shared-phone fallback.** Every game here that reads clearly does one thing this family agrees on: one side picks one thing from a fixed menu, the result is narrated, then the other side's menu rises. That is A. Nothing in the family supports the shipped "move everyone, then they get one" shape, and the two games that let one piece do several things in a turn (Baldur's Gate 3, Bravely Default) are the two whose waits are the most complained about.

**The addition, from Persona 5: One More.** In flow A, a drive through a defender's tile is priced by the crossover card. If the answer is right, the offense gets one more action at once, with no defensive step in between, capped at one per turn and never earned off a pass. The defense had its response (it put a man there); the offense beat it with knowledge, which is the game's thesis; and the blow-by is how basketball actually rewards a beaten defender. It answers the owner's doubt without breaking A's grammar: move then shoot in one turn is not free, it is earned out loud. The beat, as a row for A's table:

| Beat | Whose turn | What happens | What the phone shows | Taps | Seconds |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ball, then One More | Offense | Curry taps DRIVE and an amber tile through Davis. The crossover card opens, medium question. Right answer: Curry lands on the tile, Davis is beaten, and the offense acts again before the defense steps. Curry taps SHOOT from two tiles out. | Card owns the screen, 15 second ring. On the right answer: gold "ONE MORE!" slams across the court with the horn stab, Davis's ring goes grey, Curry stays lit, the row rises again in gold at once with SHOOT reading "2 pts, easy, open". Announcer: "Curry shakes Davis loose!" Clock resumes from where it froze. Wrong answer: the ball is Davis's, live, purple rim. | 2 + 1 | up to 15, then 3 |

**Three screen details this family settles, none of them flow changes:** narration first, then the row (Pokemon); a two-chip NOW and NEXT strip in the two team colours next to the clock (Final Fantasy X); the price drawn on the path before the tap (Baldur's Gate 3, Slay the Spire).

**What the family says about the other flows.** C's three balls have their cleanest picture in Slay the Spire's energy orb, so if the owner picks C, draw it that way. D has no cousin here; the family never removes the rally. E is Pokemon's simultaneous pick, and the research already rejected it. B is fine by this family but gets nothing new from it.
