# Menu Against Menu

**One line:** The player with the ball is your character; every beat you pick one verb from four at the bottom (Drive, Pass, Shoot, Hold), the defense answers with one word from its own four (Contest, Help, Double, Back off), and it goes ball, answer, ball, answer until a shot, a turnover, or the buzzer.

## The loop

The play you picked at the start of the quarter puts all ten players in shape and runs itself: every time the offense gets a beat, the cutters and screeners take their next scripted step on their own, nobody taps them. The offense's beat is one verb for the ball: Drive (the ball handler moves, priced by the crossover card if he goes through a man), Pass (the ball goes to a teammate and that teammate becomes the character), Shoot (the question card from the tile he stands on), or Hold (let the play run one more step). The defense's beat is one word that moves exactly one defender a short capped distance, chosen after seeing what the ball just did: Contest, Help, Double, or Back off. Then it is the offense's beat again, with the same four verbs, and the prices on them have changed because of the answer. The possession ends on a shot (right answer scores, wrong answer misses), on a lost crossover or pass card (turnover, live ball), or on the shot clock buzzer (dead ball). Nobody ever taps a figurine to pick him; the ball picks the character, and the verbs pick the action.

## Move then shoot?

No, not in one beat. Drive is a beat and Shoot is a beat, and the defense always gets exactly one answer between them. That is the whole fairness of the design and it is the reason the doubt goes away: the offense never does two things while the defense watches. What the drive buys is a better tile, and the SHOOT button shows the new price the moment he lands (2 pts and an easy question near the rim instead of 3 pts and a hard one). What the defense buys with its answer is the shape of that shot: Contest puts a body on him and brings the block card in, Back off gives him the jumper but walls the rim, Help closes his next lane at the cost of an open teammate. The defense's answer is always one defender, always a short capped move, always made after seeing the drive, which is the shape the research found reads as fair (Kill Team's Counteract) and never the several small reaction windows it found unbearable.

Move then pass: also two beats in this design, and I will say why since the owner leaned the other way. The pass is already free when the lane is clean, so making it ride on the drive beat saves the offense nothing but a four second defense beat; what it would cost is the one rule a first-time player has to learn, "one verb, then they answer." Every exception to that rule is a sentence on the screen. And the defense's answer to a drive cannot take the pass away: Double even makes the pass out free. If the owner wants the pass to ride free after a drive, the change is small (the PASS button stays lit after a drive, the others go grey) and it is listed as an open question below.

## Why it reads clearly

Three things say whose beat it is, and they are the only three things on the screen apart from the court:

1. **The ring.** The player with the ball wears a ring in his team's color with his name tag. On the defense's beat, the defender who would move under each button gets a thin purple ring as the thumb rests on that button, and the one who moves keeps a trail on the floor until the next beat resolves (the way chess apps mark the last move; the owner's 3D chess instinct is right and this is the piece of it to take).
2. **The row.** Four buttons at the bottom, always the same four in the same places for each side, in that side's color, with one header sentence above them: "Curry has the ball. Your move." or "Curry drove left. Your answer." When it is not your beat (online, or versus the computer) the row is a quiet grey bar, "Lakers are answering...", with a slow pulse, and the board stays lit so you watch the move. On one shared phone the row simply changes color: nothing is hidden from the other side, because the defense answers after seeing the move, so there is no need for a cover screen or a pass-the-phone card.
3. **The announcer.** One line at the top in a broadcast voice. It says what just happened, never what tile: "Gobert slides over to help!" not "Gobert to C4." It is the only text on the screen besides the row.

The hand-off is felt, not read. When a beat resolves, the row drops with a soft thunk, the move plays with its trail and the camera nudges to it (about seven tenths of a second), the announcer line lands, and then the other side's row rises with a rise sound and a tap of vibration. The clock is the fourth signal without being a fourth thing: it ticks only on the offense's beats, so when it starts moving again it is your ball, and when it freezes the defense is up. It sits top right, big, gold, and glows amber from five down with a horn at zero. The defense's five seconds are drawn as a ring around its own row, not as a second clock.

What is gone: the step ladder, the grey note bar, "tap a player" in three places, tap-a-figurine, and the zoom that hid the pass targets. The pass beat pulls the camera back so all five are in frame and puts five face chips with prices above the row; nobody ever hunts for a figurine to tap. The turn is said once, by the header.

For a first-time player the rule fits in one breath: the ball is your character, pick a verb, they pick an answer, again, until you shoot. There is no picking a player and no deciding when a turn is over. A verb ends your beat.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

- **Every beat is a real read.** The prices on the four buttons change after every answer, so the right verb is different each time: after Help the middle is closed but a man is open; after Double the pass out is free; after Back off the three is clean. The defense reads the same way in reverse. This is the test the research put on menus (each option best against a different thing), applied every beat rather than once a possession.
- **The defense is playing, not waiting.** Four seconds after every offensive move it has a call to make with real payoffs, and on a contested shot it holds the block card during the shooter's fifteen seconds, which is exactly what the research suggested for the waiting player: give the defender something to do inside the question beat.
- **Tempo.** A beat is three to five seconds, the animation is under a second, and the hand-off is a sound and a color, so a possession plays like a rally. The four fixed positions mean the thumb learns the row in one game.
- **The play runs itself, and you see it.** Cutters and screeners move at the start of your beat, right in front of you, so the play you picked at the quarter is alive every possession without a tap. Hold is a real choice, not a pass: it lets the next step of the play happen while the defense has to answer nothing new.
- **The gamble is last.** Safe verbs first (Drive into an open lane, a clean pass, Hold), the card last (the shot, or a drive through a man). The possession has the shape the research found in Blood Bowl: set up, set up, then bet.
- **It is a broadcast.** The announcer makes the beat log a story, "Lakers double Thompson!", "Curry from deep... SPLASH", which is the NBA Street voice the owner asked for.

## Fit with the locked rules

- **The question gates.** Untouched. Shoot opens the card from the tile (near the rim easy 2, mid-range medium 2, behind the arc hard 3, logo very hard 3). A drive through a guarded tile opens the crossover card at the shipped price (full head-on, one step easier from the corner, one step harder deep). A pass through a defender's reach or off a pressured passer opens the pass card. Wrong answer is a miss or a turnover. Movement is movement, the cards are the price.
- **Eight-tile guarding and closed lanes.** Untouched. The defensive calls are how a human closes a lane on purpose: Help and Double put a second guard on a lane and the tile goes dark. The lit tiles under DRIVE read the same colors as today (free, priced, closed) and the prices come from the same functions that price the card.
- **The 24 second clock.** Kept, with one change said out loud: it ticks only on the offense's beats and freezes on the defense's five second answer ring. Otherwise a slow defender would burn the offense's clock. It stops when a card opens and resumes when the ball is live again. Buzzer at zero, dead ball, other side's ball.
- **Plays picked once.** This is the engine of the idea. A play is a starting shape plus three or four scripted steps; the step advances at the start of every offensive beat. Both sides pick one at the quarter (or half, or game, by format). Off-ball players are moved only by the play. **Rule I would change: the free off-ball step and the free-move setup half are gone.** The owner walked that back himself on the fifth; this concept is what replaces it.
- **Offense-only timeouts.** A small chip under the clock, only on the offense's beats, greyed when none are left. Tapping it stops the clock and opens the play list with the small 2D board above it, as ruled. The defense gets its play list at the same stop.
- **Live-ball continuation.** A lost card or a defensive rebound makes the winner the character where he stands, at once: no slam, no snap, no play step. His teammates run one fast-break step (sprint to lanes) and the other side is out of shape. The first defensive answer comes after his first beat, as always. A made basket is the only thing that triggers the hand-off slam and the shape reset.
- **Rule I would change, the walk-up.** After a made basket the inbound and the walk over half court happen inside the hand-off animation, unless the defense's picked play is a press, in which case the inbound is a real Pass beat with the press pricing it and a five second inbound limit. Full-court dribbling beat by beat was the tedious part he named, and Curry at three tiles a beat needs three beats to cross the floor against nobody.
- **Rule I would change, how the slide is entered.** Today the defense taps a defender, then a tile. Here the defense taps a word and the engine moves the one defender the word names (the nearest to the ball for Contest and Back off, the nearest off-ball man to the open lane for Help and Double), capped at his role range. The guarding rules under him do not change; only the input does.
- **Small one, flagged.** An offensive rebound resets the clock to 14, the real rule, not 24.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 3 to 5 (the common case is drive, pass, shoot).
- Defense decisions per possession: one per offensive beat, 3 to 5.
- Total taps: 10 to 15 (offense 7 to 11, defense 3 to 5). Today's shipped possession is about 20.
- Seconds per possession: 30 to 45 with one card, up to about 70 with two cards. Real time on the shot clock is 24 at most because it ticks only on the offense's beats.
- Longest wait for the other player: the 15 seconds of a question card; on a contested shot the defender is holding the block card for those 15 seconds, so the true idle wait is about 8 seconds (one offensive beat plus its animation).

## Weaknesses

- **The engine picks the body.** Contest moves the nearest defender; a player who wanted a different defender to step up cannot say so without a fifth tap. The four words cover the four things a defense wants, but a coach who thinks in names may feel steered. A "tap a defender first, then the word" variant exists and costs one tap a beat.
- **A dull defender makes the defense beat a tax.** If someone taps Contest every time, the answer ring becomes four seconds of nothing for the offense. The prices punish that (Contest every time leaves the drive lanes open), and the five second default keeps it short, but it needs a playtest.
- **The play's steps can railroad.** If a scripted cut runs into a closed lane the cutter just stops, and if the shape is bad for this defense the offense has only Hold and the timeout. Play authoring carries weight it did not before.
- **Four verbs do not name a screen or a post-up.** Those live inside the plays. A player who wants to call one on the fly cannot.
- **The walk-up automation is a real rule change** for full-court play, and the press exception is unbuilt.
- **Two questions in one possession** (a priced pass, then the shot) pushes a possession past a minute. That is today's rule too, but the fast rhythm makes it feel longer by contrast.
- **None of this is shipped anywhere.** The research found no mobile turn-based sports game with menu-against-menu play on a real court; the pieces are borrowed from games that work, the whole is a bet.

## Open questions for the owner

1. The fourth offensive verb: Hold (let the play run) as designed, or Timeout in that slot with Hold folded into a long press?
2. The four defensive words: Contest, Help, Double, Back off as written, or your names?
3. Pass after a drive: two beats (my recommendation) or the PASS button stays lit after a drive so move-then-pass is one beat?
4. Does the defense's picked play run scripted steps like the offense's, or is it a standing shape that only the four calls ever move?
5. Five seconds for the defense's answer, and the defense holds if it runs out: right numbers?
6. The walk-up: automatic after a made basket unless the defense pressed, or keep beat-by-beat backcourt play?
7. Blind or sighted play pick at the quarter, and who picks first: still yours, and this concept works either way.

## Borrowed from

- Pokemon: the fixed four-slot row that never moves, the character with the name plate, the text box that narrates each turn, one action per side per beat.
- Kill Team (the research's closest match): one capped defensive move made after seeing the offense's move, with full information.
- Avalon Hill's Basketball Strategy and Statis Pro Basketball (the research): defense as one declared call, help as a decision with an automatic cost elsewhere.
- Strat-O-Matic Basketball (the research): the possession is one story about the ball handler and his defender; everyone else is context that the play moves.
- Blood Bowl (the research): safe moves first, the gamble last, a failed gamble ends the turn.
- Slay the Spire and Hoplite (the research): the price is honest and drawn on the board and the buttons, never in a pop-up, and reading it costs no taps.
- Sirlin's menu test (the research): each call best against a different thing, applied to both rows.
- Chess apps: the last move stays drawn until the next one.
- NBA Street: the announcer.
