## The short version

The idea is right and it fixes a real hole. Every piece of it has support in
the archive except two: the defense getting a second thing every turn, and a
secret pass-lane pick. Three of the four reviewers score it low as he spoke
it and high with a handful of one-sentence fixes, and all four land on the
same fixes.

## What he got right, checked against the game

- **The filibuster is real.** The 24 restarts at every turn on both sides
  and nothing caps the number of turns in a possession. A team can pass all
  day. He is the first to say it out loud; the archive saw the hole and
  left it to him.
- **The two clocks are what he thinks.** 24 seconds to take your turn,
  restarting every turn for both sides. 15 seconds to answer a question.
- **The half-court reset fixes the fast break.** Three moves could not cross
  the floor after a steal; that was the flaw that sank the three-moves idea
  on the board. His reset is the exact fix the critic of that idea proposed,
  and he arrived at it on his own. A point guard's steal-and-go is a
  two-turn, 32-second possession.
- **One More can still be defended by a big man in position.** That is
  already how the shipped shot works: a defender standing next to the
  shooter and between him and the rim contests the shot, and the centre is
  preferred when two qualify. No new rule is needed. One catch below.
- **The steal must cost.** Today a wrong steal answer costs the defender
  nothing but the slide. He is right.
- **Setups picked at each team's first offense and first defense** is
  supported by the archive's answer to blind-or-sighted (the defense picks
  first and shows it; whoever commits blind eats the bad feeling), on one
  condition: at both moments the defense reveals before the offense picks.

One honest correction to my own memo. The board said the three-moves idea
was "retired on evidence" from Football Tactics and Glory. That game's
complaints are about three actions in a row with nothing for the other side
to do, and an automatic defense. His version answers every offensive turn
with a defensive turn, so neither complaint applies. It was retired on an
analogy, and nobody should cite that game against his count.

## The problems, worst first

**1. The reset rewards holding the ball in the backcourt.** Cross half court
on turn one and the possession has four offensive turns in all. Cross on
turn three and it has six, plus two more free off-ball moves. The slow team
gets more, and it picks when to cross. That is the opposite of what a shot
clock is for. In real basketball the eight-second count sits inside the 24;
crossing does not give you a fresh 24. Fix: one count for the possession,
drawn as balls, with a mark on the ball you must have crossed by. The rules
reviewer says cross by three, shoot by six. The basketball reviewer says
cross by two, shoot by four. Same shape, different number; the paper test
picks the number.

**2. As spoken it is the slowest flow on the table.** A typical possession
after a made basket, practiced players:

| Flow | taps | seconds | times the turn changes sides |
|---|---|---|---|
| His proposal as spoken | 25 | 83 | 8 |
| A (one thing, then their step) | 12 | 46 | 6 |
| B (move one, then pass) | 15 | 54 | 6 |
| Today | 21 | 56 | 3 |

First-timers on his proposal: 141 seconds. Over a 24-possession game: 33
minutes of play against 18 for A and 22 for today. Two passengers cause
almost all of it, and neither is the idea he likes:

- **The free off-ball move every turn.** Four of them in a typical
  possession: 8 taps and 16 to 32 seconds. That is the same number of
  off-ball moves as today's complaint ("click each player, move, click
  move"), spread over the turns, now with a defensive step after each. Free
  things get taken because skipping one feels like waste.
- **Playing the walk-up after a made basket turn by turn.** 13 taps, 38
  seconds and 5 side changes from the inbound to half court, and three of
  those five are defensive turns where the honest play is STAY. Over a game
  that is 7 to 9 minutes. Flows A and B glide both teams into their plays
  after a made basket and play the walk-up only on a live ball (a steal or a
  rebound), which is where the half-court count matters anyway.

**3. The cap bounds turns, not seconds.** With the 24 per turn kept, the
longest legal possession is six offensive turns at 24, six defensive steps
at 24 and five questions at 15: six minutes. And the defense holds a
144-second stalling lever, six steps at 24 where letting the clock run out
costs nothing. His filibuster worry moves from the offense to the defense
unless the defensive step gets a short clock (8 to 10 seconds). Two of his
own earlier rulings sit near this: "don't shorten the shot clock" (08-01,
about the offense's thinking time) and the defense's own 24 ("both sides get
to think", 07-23). The turn cap does not cut thinking time; the short
defensive step does. His call, out loud.

**4. The defense getting a move AND an action.** The archive leans against.
Kill Team's answer, which the defense research adopted, is one thing after
each enemy action, taken with full information; it never studied two. Into
the Breach's lesson is that an informed reaction under a clock needs a
small set of options, and a second action every turn widens it. A steal
card on the defensive turn is a new stop, and it makes the offense wait,
which nobody has measured. The basketball reviewer ran the numbers with
assumed hit rates: move-then-steal reaches the ball almost every turn, and
with the shipped steal (two cards plus a buzz race) one attempt takes the
ball roughly a third of the time from a point guard and nearly half from a
centre; four attempts in a possession and the offense keeps the ball about
one time in five. That is the Hoop League Tactics complaint word for word:
"you can neither dribble nor pass without risk of having the ball stolen",
"annoying to play". The blow-by cost does not fix that half; it only fires
when the defender misses. The only two basketball games in the archive that
mirrored offense and defense as move-plus-action both stalled at prototype
(two data points, not proof). Fix: one thing a defensive turn. Step one
man, or, if a defender already stands next to the ball when the defensive
turn starts, try the steal instead of stepping. "Already" matters: the
offense had a turn to get away and chose to stay in traffic. A wrong steal
is a blow-by. And the steal becomes one card (defender right, steal;
defender wrong, blow-by), not two cards and a buzz race.

**5. Picking a pass lane.** His question: *"you have that in the game
somewhere right?"* No. Today the passer pays: a pass longer than three
squares with a body near its flight is a medium question, anything seven or
longer is hard, and a short pass with a defender on the passer's rim side is
never free. So a defender picks a lane by standing in it, and the offense
sees the price on the receiver's ring. The hidden version, where a special
player secretly names a lane and a pass down it triggers an interception
challenge, is his own 07-23 idea, filed as a signature skill with the
no-look pass as its counter, online first, because on a shared phone
nothing is hidden: both people look at the same screen. The archive on a
hidden pick: XCOM's overwatch (a blind commitment dominates if it is free
and feels wasted if it is penalised; one game, one thread) and Tecmo Bowl
(hidden picks become rock paper scissors). A third way nobody weighed:
Blood Bowl's interception, where the defense names the interceptor when the
pass is thrown, seeing everything. Recommendation: no lane pick in the base
rules; the step is the lane pick, and when a defender is lifted on his turn
the lanes his new square would cut are tinted so he sees what his square
does. The hidden lane stays a signature skill.

**6. One More needs a trigger and a choice.** Today the crossover is a
chain: the ball handler answers, then the beaten defender answers a
stay-in-front card, both right is an ankle battle, and a wrong first answer
gives the defender a pick-the-pocket card. "Blow-by" has to mean the chain
ended with the ball handler past his man. Then: One More is the player's
choice of shoot or pass, never another crossover. Read literally, "the shot
in range, a free pass out of range" deletes drive-and-kick, the most basic
play in basketball (beat your man at the top, the big steps up, kick to the
corner). Let him choose. The pass is free of the turn, never of its
question: otherwise a right crossover buys a guaranteed clean catch anywhere
within six squares and the defense's positions stop mattering the moment a
card is won. Last, the shipped contest has no memory of who was just
beaten; in some positions the man you just crossed still counts as the
contester. One line fixes it: the beaten man is out of the One More; anyone
else next to the shooter and between him and the rim contests as normal.

**7. Gaps the rulebook has to close before two people can play it on paper.**

- The plain dribble is missing from his list (pass, crossover, shot). A
  dribble to an open square must be a turn-ending ball action like the
  other two, or the ball handler walks the floor for free.
- Two things are called the shot clock: the 24 on screen today and his
  count. Name the count "turns" (or "dribbles", his own teaching line) and
  the 24 keeps its name.
- Does the inbound pass count as turn one. Suggest the count starts on the
  first turn after the catch, as the eight-second count starts on the touch.
- The count after an offensive rebound (suggest two more turns, the shape of
  the 14-second rule), after a timeout (nothing changes, or timeouts become
  the filibuster), after a steal (the same count from where the ball lies).
- The last turn of the count when no shot exists from where the ball is
  (beyond 38 feet nothing is a shot). Either the row says LAST TURN and the
  player wears it, or a last-turn pass to a man who shoots at once is
  allowed. That is the catch-and-shoot question open since 08-02.
- Move-then-shoot through the back door: the free move can slide a screener
  next to the shooter, or screen the ball handler's man, and then the ball
  action shoots or drives with no defensive answer between. Accept it as the
  point of a screen, or read shot prices before the free move.
- A defense that picks the press once keeps it for the whole span of the
  pick, and a press against a three-turn count is the strongest thing in the
  rulebook: every inbound pressured, every crossover a card, one miss a
  violation. Maybe right; nobody has priced it.
- The camping big man he wants to build strategy around is illegal in the
  NBA (defensive three seconds) and legal in college and FIBA ball. Rule it
  legal and say so in the rulebook.
- Three in the key counts "three of your actions"; under the new turn it
  must count turns, the same unit as the count, or a player is whistled by
  a rule counting differently from the one on screen.
- The setup pick at the first flip is a mid-game stop the shipped game does
  not have, and the information is uneven (the second team picks its
  defense having seen both of the first team's picks). Unstudied.

## The smallest rulebook that makes his idea work

Eight sentences. His numbers where he gave them; MINE where marked.

1. Your turn: one free move by a teammate without the ball, then one thing
   with the ball: dribble, pass or shoot. Any of the three ends your turn.
   (MINE: the free move costs nothing to skip and is not offered on the
   inbound turn.)
2. Their turn: one defender steps, or one defender already next to the ball
   goes for the steal. Not both. A wrong steal is a blow-by. (MINE: one
   card, not two and a buzz race.)
3. Dribbling into a guarded square is a crossover, priced as today. If you
   end up past your man, you act once more right away, shoot or pass, with
   no defensive turn between. Once a turn, never off a pass. The pass pays
   its normal question. The beaten man cannot contest.
4. A missed steal gives the ball handler one square past the reacher, then
   his turn as normal. (The accepted change, named apart from the crossover
   blow-by.)
5. The count, drawn as balls: the ball must be over half court by the
   marked ball, and a shot must go up by the last one. Either miss is a
   turnover. The number (cross by two and shoot by four, or cross by three
   and shoot by six) is the paper test's job. An offensive rebound gives
   two more. A timeout changes nothing. (MINE on the rebound and timeout.)
6. The 24 stays as it is and keeps its name. The count is called turns. The
   defense's step gets its own short clock, 8 to 10 seconds. (MINE.)
7. No lane pick. A defender standing in the lane is the lane pick, and the
   lanes his step would cut are drawn while he is lifted. (MINE.)
8. After a made basket both teams glide into their plays; the walk-up and
   the half-court count are played on live balls. Over-and-back, three in
   the key (counted in turns) and out of bounds stay as today. (MINE on the
   glide.)

With those eight, the typical possession is 15 taps, 55 seconds and 4 side
changes. Underneath, it is flow B with a crossover reward and a cap, which is
what his proposal was all along.

## What only a paper test can answer

1. Whether four to six hand-offs a possession feel like chess or like
   ping-pong on one phone. Run it with the walk-up glided and with it
   played, so he feels the 38 seconds himself.
2. The number: four turns or six, and whether the marked ball is the second
   or the third.
3. The defense's turn, one thing or two: the same ten possessions both
   ways, counting stops and who waits.

## His rulings, in order

1. One count or two, and the number (the paper test can decide the number).
2. The defense: one thing a turn (recommended) or move then action.
3. Glide after a made basket, or play the walk-up.
4. The lane pick: none in the base rules (recommended), or built now.
5. The plain dribble is a turn-ending ball action (confirm).
6. One More is shoot or pass, and the beaten man is out (confirm).
7. The defense's step clock: 8 to 10 seconds, or the 24 stays.
8. The camping big man is legal (confirm).
