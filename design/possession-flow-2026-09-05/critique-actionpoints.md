# Critique: Three Moves, Then Shoot

Judged against brief.md and scout.md, through the three lenses the critic brief asks for, in order. Default is doubt.

## Lens 1 · The first-time player

I am on a 390 pixel phone, never read a rule, nobody is coaching me. I walk the ten beats.

Beat 1, the flip. A slam says THEIR BUCKET · YOUR BALL in my colour and ten figurines jump to new tiles at once. I know it is my ball. I do not know why everybody teleported. Nothing on screen says "plays". First confusion, small.

Beat 2, the inbound. Jokic is lifted, four teammates have gold rings, the strip says INBOUND and shows three balls. There is no button row. Nobody tells me to tap a teammate. I probably work it out from the rings within a few seconds, but a 5 is counting down next to the 24, so I have two numbers top right on my first beat, and the three balls mean nothing to me yet. Second confusion. Also the concept says the clock reads "one number, top right"; this beat has two.

Beat 3, my first move. Curry pulses, the strip says YOUR MOVE, the row says MOVE · PASS · SHOOT with a price under SHOOT. This is the clearest beat in the concept. I know it is my turn and what I can do. One gap: the concept says I can move an off-ball player, but the row's MOVE lights Curry's reach. How do I move Wilson instead? The table never shows it. Either I tap Wilson on the floor (not stated) or it is MOVE, then Wilson, then a tile, which is three taps, not the "same two taps" promised. Third confusion, and it is the one move the owner said should stay free.

Beat 4, my first defensive beat (online, or the other tester in hot seat). The strip turns blue, says YOUR SLIDE, and the row says STAY. That is the only button. The real action, tapping one of five defenders and then a tile, is on the floor with no words pointing at it. The concept says "the empty strip and the pulsing ball handler ask the question for you". A first-timer sees a button that says STAY and a pulsing enemy. I would tap STAY. Fourth confusion, and it is the first thing the defense ever does.

Beats 5 to 8. Once I have done one move and one slide, these read well. The balls drain, LAST MOVE · then the shot and LAST SLIDE · shot coming are two lines a first-timer understands without a rulebook. The other side's move stays drawn as a trail, so if I looked away I can still see what happened. The hand-off is colour plus chime plus one buzz plus a new lit player; it is readable without reading.

Beat 9, the shot. One wide button, the card takes the screen. Clear.

Beat 10, the flip. Slam, colour swap, clock resets. I know the ball changed hands.

The beat the table does not draw is the one a first-timer will need most: the wrong answer, the steal, the shot-clock buzzer. The concept says "the ball is live and the defense has it" and "flips the ball where it lies". Which defender has it? What does the strip say? What lights? A concept whose whole pitch is readability leaves the messiest hand-off in the game undrawn. Fifth confusion, and it is the only one that could make a tester put the phone down.

Hot seat on one phone: seven hand-offs per possession. The concept's answer is the phone lying between two people. On a 390 pixel phone lying flat, one person reads the strip upside down and taps a 3D court from the wrong end. That is not a design, it is a hope.

Moments of confusion: five, at beats 1, 2, 3, 4 and the undrawn miss. Is the hand-off visible without reading: yes, once you have seen it twice. Score: 6 out of 10.

## Lens 2 · Tempo and waiting

The arithmetic, using the concept's own table.

Taps: offense 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 9, defense 2 + 2 + 2 = 6, total 15. The count is honest for the table it draws. It is 1 to 5 taps fewer than today's 16 to 20. That is not the "something's gotta give" the owner asked for. The saving is small; what changed is the order. Today about twelve taps happen before the defense sees anything; here they are spread across seven hand-offs.

Seconds: the table says 44. That rests on 5 seconds per offensive move and 4 per slide, animation included. A move on a 15 by 8 court with priced lanes, closed lanes and a price on SHOOT that changes with every tile is a decision, and a decision on a phone is 8 to 15 seconds, not 5. A slide with five defenders and a visible countdown is 6 to 8. Re-run the table at those numbers: inbound 4, three moves at 12, three slides at 7, the shot tap plus a 10 second answer, two flips at 2, and seven hand-off animations at about 1.5 (strip out, strip in, chime, camera nudge, trail). That is about 78 seconds, the top of today's 45 to 75. The concept's own ceiling of 95 is honest. Its typical of 44 is not. And the sentence "the two sides never wait more than a card's length on each other" is contradicted two sections later by "up to 24 if the offense spends its whole shot clock on one move".

The shot clock. The concept keeps 24 seconds and runs it only during the offense's decision beats: inbound, up to three moves, the shoot tap. That is 24 seconds spread over four or five decisions, about 5 or 6 seconds each. The concept says the three-ball cap will end most possessions before the 24 matters. The opposite is true for a first-timer: the 24 will buzz mid-possession on most of their early possessions, and the buzzer is a turnover. The concept has built a shot clock that punishes thinking, three times a possession, then says it will rarely fire.

The slide clock at 8 seconds, glowing and ticking under 5, three times a possession: that is nine seconds of a glowing clock every possession on defense, and expiry forfeits the slide. For a first-timer on defense this is anxious, not fair.

The 15 second question clock: fits, one card per shot, the 24 pauses. Fine. The block card the defender gets on a contested look is mentioned twice and drawn nowhere, so I cannot check it against the beats.

Waiting, online. The offense waits 4 to 8 seconds three times. The defense waits 5 to 24 seconds three or four times, plus 15 during the card. Nobody waits long, but the defense sits, acts for four seconds, sits again, seven times a possession. That is exactly the shape the research called unbearable (several small reaction windows). The scout says that finding rests on thin sources, so I do not treat it as proof, but the concept walks into that shape and never mentions it.

Over a game. Twenty-four possessions at 7 hand-offs is 168 hand-offs, each with a strip animation, a chime and a buzz, in a game of 20 to 30 minutes. Race to 11 is roughly 8 to 12 possessions, 60 to 85 hand-offs, 8 to 15 minutes. The rhythm the concept promises (three, slide, three, slide, shoot, flip) is real and will be learned inside a quarter. Whether it feels like chess or like ping-pong is the whole bet, and the concept has no evidence either way.

Hot seat. Seven physical hand-offs of one phone per possession, 168 a game. Today it is two or three. This is a large regression for one of the three ways to play, and the concept flags it as "the hard case" without a fix.

Is this faster and less tedious, or just different? Just different. Slightly fewer taps, the same seconds, and more hand-offs than any design on file. Score: 4 out of 10.

## Lens 3 · Rules and research

Rule by rule.

Question gates on shots and crossovers: kept, priced by tile, drawn on the row and the reach. Good.

Every defender guards eight tiles, closed lanes: kept, drawn when MOVE is tapped. Good.

The 24 second clock: kept in name, changed in meaning (offense decision time per possession, paused for slides and cards), and the defense's 24 cut to 8. Said out loud. The change itself is defensible; the number is wrong (see Lens 2). The concept should give here, not the rule: either the 24 becomes per beat at a smaller number, or the possession clock grows and the balls are the real cap.

Plays picked once, offense-only timeouts: kept, untouched.

Live-ball continuation after steals and rebounds: kept in words, broken in practice. A steal near your own basket gives you three moves on a 15 tile court with movement ranges of 1 to 3 tiles, and a pass spends a move. Three dribbles cover 9 tiles at best, so the ball reaches the far arc with no moves left and shoots a hard or very hard question, or the ball handler shoots from the logo. The owner's rule is that the team that took the ball keeps the advantage. Under this concept the team that took the ball gets the worst shot in the game. The concept half-knows this (open question 2, "one extra for the fast break") and leaves it to the owner. The concept should give: live-ball possessions need moves that do not count until the ball crosses half court, or a fast-break ball, or the cap only counts in the front court.

The half-court snap after a made basket (the concept's third change). Two problems. First, the beat table contradicts it: beat 2 has Jokic inbounding "on the baseline" and beat 3 has Curry "dribbling up" and "walking it up top", which is a walk-up, the very thing the change removes. If both teams snapped into Horns and Man in the attacking half, Curry is already at the top and there is no baseline inbound from that end. Second, it removes the full-court walk for every dead-ball possession, which quietly shrinks a 15 tile court to a half court for most of the game, and the concept does not say so. The owner should rule on that, and the table has to match whichever way he rules.

The research verdicts.

No mid-beat prompts: honoured. Every slide sits between beats, never inside one.

One informed, last-moving, small defensive answer reads fair: each slide is informed and last, and the concept cites Kill Team correctly. But there are three of them per possession, not one, and the same research file rejects "several small reaction windows" by name. The concept cites the adopted finding and is silent on the rejected one. It should say out loud that it is choosing the rejected shape and why (the reason is available: the balls make each window purposeful, and the scout says the rejection is thinly sourced). It does not.

Safe moves first, then the gamble: honoured exactly, and the early-shot rule keeps the shot available at every offensive beat. This is the strongest rule fit in the concept.

Does it quietly reintroduce tap-every-player: no. Three moves, one piece each, and a pass costs a move. If anything it errs the other way, the offense cannot afford to move more than two off-ball players in a possession, so spacing comes almost entirely from the play picked once. That is consistent with the owner's direction.

The owner's own words the concept departs from without saying so: "they can still move and then pass" was said as one turn. Here move and pass are two turns with a slide between. That is a choice, and not a bad one, but it should be stated.

Score: 6 out of 10.

## The owner's doubt

"Offense moves, defense moves, then offense shoots, then again... that feels confusing. Is that easy to digest?"

Half yes. The concept does not remove the back-and-forth; it has more of it than anything on file. What it adds is a countdown: three balls that both sides watch drain, LAST MOVE · then the shot, LAST SLIDE · shot coming. That answers the half of his doubt that is "when does it start over" and "how many times", and it is the best single device I have seen for it. Nobody counts turns; they watch the balls.

It does not answer the other half, "is the repeated flow fluid and fun, not tedious". Seven hand-offs a possession is the confusing thing he described, done more often, with a chime each time. The balls make the repetition legible; they do not make it shorter. And it does answer his move-then-shoot worry cleanly: a slide sits between every move and the shot, so the defense always responds.

Verdict on the doubt: partly. Legible, yes. Digestible, unproven, and the tempo lens says probably not on one phone.

## Flaws

1. Hot seat needs seven hand-offs of one phone per possession. Severity: serious, fatal for that mode if not solved. Fix: none clean inside this concept. The two-slides-for-three-moves variant (the concept's own open question 3) cuts it to five. A different hot-seat shape (the defense picks its three slides as one beat after seeing all three moves, or the concept only ships online and versus the computer) is the honest fix.

2. The three-ball cap breaks the fast break. Severity: serious. Fix: on a live-ball flip, moves that carry the ball forward in the backcourt do not spend a ball, or the flip grants a fourth ball, or the cap counts only from the first front-court touch.

3. The 24 spread across four or five offensive decisions is about six seconds a decision and the buzzer is a turnover. Severity: serious. Fix: a per-beat offense clock of 10 to 12 seconds with the balls as the real cap, or a possession clock of 40 with a 24 that only starts on the first front-court touch. The owner asked for one bright clock with a buzzer; he did not ask for it to fire every possession.

4. The miss, steal and shot-clock flip is not drawn. Severity: serious for a readability concept. Fix: add the beat. Who has the ball, what the strip says, what the slam says, where the balls stand.

5. The beat table contradicts the concept's own half-court snap (baseline inbound and a walk-up in a possession that supposedly started in the attacking half). Severity: minor as a mistake, serious as honesty, because the numbers in the table are the concept's evidence. Fix: redraw beats 2 and 3 to match the rule, or drop the snap.

6. The defensive row shows STAY as the only button while the real action lives on the floor. Severity: serious for the first defensive beat, minor after. Fix: the row reads SLIDE · STAY (· STEAL); tapping SLIDE lights every defender; the two-tap promise then holds on both sides.

7. The off-ball move gesture is undocumented and is three taps, not two. Severity: minor. Fix: the floor is the menu: any lit teammate can be tapped first, then a tile; MOVE on the row is a shortcut for the ball handler.

8. It is not faster. Fifteen taps against sixteen to twenty, and the same seconds, rearranged. Severity: serious against the brief ("something's gotta give"). Fix: the two-slides variant, a one-tap move for the ball handler, and no separate inbound beat when the play snap already places the ball (which is what the snap implies).

9. Adopts the "several small reaction windows" shape the research rejected, without saying so. Severity: minor as honesty, serious as risk. Fix: say it, and make the defense's replies fewer than the offense's moves so each reply is a choice.

10. Two numbers top right on the inbound beat (5 and 24). Severity: minor. Fix: the 5 is the clock on that beat; the 24 appears when the ball is in.

11. Nine seconds of glowing, ticking clock per possession on defense, and forfeiture on expiry. Severity: minor. Fix: 10 seconds, glow under 3, and expiry means the defender stays put with no fanfare.

12. The block card on a contested shot is named twice and drawn nowhere, so its place on the screen (and on a hot-seat phone during the offense's question) is unknown. Severity: minor now, serious once it is drawn. Fix: draw it, or drop it from this concept.

13. Departs from the owner's "move and then pass" as one turn without saying so. Severity: minor. Fix: one sentence in the concept.

## Strengths

Only what the table shows.

- The three balls in the strip at beats 3, 5, 7 and 8, and the two lines LAST MOVE · then the shot and LAST SLIDE · shot coming. Both sides read the same countdown; the defense always knows when the shot is coming. This is the concept's real invention.
- The SHOOT button carrying its price on every offensive beat (3 · very hard, then 3 · hard, then 2 · medium as the ball moves). The tile-sets-the-price law is on screen without a card or a coach.
- The other side's last move staying drawn as a trail through your whole beat (beats 4 and 5). It answers "you never really know that a turn ended" directly.
- The same two-tap gesture for a move and a slide (beats 3 to 8), so one learned gesture serves both sides.
- A slide after every move and before the shot (beats 4, 6, 8), so move-then-shoot is allowed and the defense always answers. This is the cleanest answer to that question in the brief.
- The early shot collapsing the beats without a new rule (the note after beat 10).
- Slams that say what happened in words (THEIR BUCKET · YOUR BALL, SPLASH · STEWART FROM THE CORNER) at beats 1 and 10, and an announcer line that arrives and leaves.
- The camera rule, everything tappable on screen, which is the only concept element that speaks to the owner's "you can't even see another player to pass to".

## Verdict

REPAIR. The three-ball countdown and the slide-between-move-and-shot are worth keeping; they are the clearest answer on file to the owner's "when does it start over" and "can they move then shoot" questions, and they do not reintroduce tap-every-player. But as written the concept is not faster than what shipped, it doubles the hand-offs, it breaks hot seat, it turns the fast break into a logo heave, its 24 is a trap for first-timers, and its beat table contradicts its own half-court rule and omits the miss. Repair in this order: draw the miss and steal beat; fix the fast break (backcourt moves free on a live-ball flip); replace the possession-wide 24 with a per-beat offense clock and keep the buzzer; put SLIDE on the defensive row; redraw beats 2 and 3 to match the snap or drop the snap; then take the concept's own two-slides-for-three-moves variant seriously, because it cuts hand-offs from seven to five and gives the defense a real choice each possession. If hot seat still needs seven phone hand-offs after that, the concept ships online and versus the computer only, and the owner should be told that out loud before he picks it.
