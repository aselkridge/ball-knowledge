# The tactics family: Mario + Rabbids, XCOM, Fire Emblem, Advance Wars, Into the Breach, Midnight Suns

Written for Aaron, 2026-09-05, as the designer who has played these. The question: "why not games like Rabbids, or other turn based RPGs?" The short answer first, then the games.

The whole family shares one skeleton: your side moves and attacks with every unit it has, then you press End Turn, then their side does the same with every unit it has. Move then attack is allowed in every one of them. The price is that the other side answers with a whole turn of its own, not with one step. That is the skeleton you walked away from on 09-05 ("click each player, move, click move, click move"), and it is a skeleton these games can afford because they field three to twelve units against a computer and a turn is expected to take a minute or two. A basketball possession has 24 seconds and a human on the other side. So the family does not give us its turn. What it gives us is the best screen craft in games for showing a move before you commit it, and two phone-sized descendants (Into the Breach, Midnight Suns) whose turn shapes are already inside our flow A. Details below.

## Mario + Rabbids Kingdom Battle (2017)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. Three heroes a side. Each hero gets one move, one attack and one technique per turn, in any order, and you may switch between heroes mid-turn (move Mario, shoot with Luigi, come back and shoot with Mario). Moving into an enemy on the way is a dash: free damage that does not spend the attack, and the hero keeps walking. Moving onto a teammate is a team jump: he throws you further than your range. Moving through a pipe teleports. All of that is inside the one move. When all three are done you press End Turn and the enemy plays its full turn, every enemy, one at a time. The enemy's answer to your move-then-shoot is its own move-then-shoot next turn, plus two pre-committed traps: Hero Sight (Mario shoots anything that moves in his view during the enemy turn) and the enemies' versions of the same.

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. Before you commit a move, the reachable tiles are shaded; hover any tile and a line is drawn on the ground from the hero to it, the dash and the jump are drawn on that line, and at the destination small shield icons show what cover you would have against each enemy (full cover, half cover, none). Hit chances are only three numbers, 100, 50 and 0, so the shield is the whole story. Nothing is spent until you confirm. A tactical view button pulls the camera straight up to see the whole arena. The camera zooms only on the attack animation, then returns. The hand-off is a banner ("Enemy turn") and the enemies move one by one with the camera following each, then "Your turn" comes back with your heroes brightened and the End Turn button back on screen.

STEAL. The preview before the commit: a destination shows you what you get from there before you tap it. For us that is the dot showing "3 · hard · open" or "2 · easy · contested" before the move, and the priced crossover tile drawn amber on the line. HURT. The any-order team turn: three heroes each with three things is nine choices per turn, and it takes a minute. Five players with that freedom is exactly the shipped complaint.

VERDICT: adapt. Take the preview, the line on the ground and the dash-as-crossover; reject the team turn and the pre-committed trap. Source: Game Informer's beginner tips (swap between characters' actions freely), GameSpot's technique guide (dash does not end the move, dash and team jump chain), Prima Games on hit chances (100, 50, 0). The cover shields on hover are from memory.

## Mario + Rabbids Sparks of Hope (2022)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. Same three heroes, but the grid is gone: each hero gets a white circle on the floor and walks freely inside it, dashing several enemies, team jumping, trying angles, and can shoot from anywhere in the circle at any moment. Once the main weapon is fired that hero is stuck where he stands. So the order is really "move as much as you like, shoot, done", and the answer to "can I move then shoot" is yes, with the shot as the full stop. Enemy turn follows as before.

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. The circle is the whole legal-move display; you see your remaining reach as you walk. The shot aim shows the target and the damage before you fire. Hand-off is the banner and the enemy parade again.

STEAL. "The shot is the full stop": once you shoot, nothing else happens for that unit. That is A's rule and B's rule already (the shot ends the turn and the possession). HURT. Free walking on a phone is a thumb on the screen for ten seconds; our tile court and one-to-three tile ranges are the right size for a tap.

VERDICT: reject as a turn, keep as confirmation that the shot is always the last thing. Source: GamingTrend and Nintendo Life reviews (free movement inside the area until the main weapon is used, then stuck).

## XCOM 2 (2016)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. Each soldier has two actions. Moving costs one; shooting, overwatch and a dash (double move) end the turn no matter what is left. So move then shoot is the standard turn and shoot then move is impossible without a special skill. The enemy answers with its whole turn, and with overwatch: a soldier who did not shoot fires automatically at the first enemy who moves in his view, at a penalty. The August defense research already named overwatch as the pattern that dominates or reads as luck; playing it confirms that. Half of XCOM's tension is guessing where the overwatch is.

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. The movement range is drawn in two colours: blue tiles cost one action (you can still shoot from there), yellow tiles cost both (you get there and that is your turn). Cover shields appear on the destination, hit chance as a percentage on the target. The hand-off is red text, "Alien Activity", with the camera cutting to each enemy move; then your soldiers light up. The cinematic camera swoops in on every shot, and most veterans switch it off because it slows the game down.

STEAL. The two-colour range: a tile's colour tells you what it costs you before you tap it. Ours already has green (free) and amber (a question) on the dots; XCOM proves players read that instantly. HURT. Overwatch as the defense's answer, and a camera that swoops on every action.

VERDICT: adapt the two-colour tiles; reject the two-action turn and overwatch. Source: gamepressure's XCOM 2 movement guide (shooting, overwatch and dash end the turn even with actions left) and the XCOM wiki's turn page. The blue and yellow tile colours are from memory of the screen.

## Fire Emblem (the series; Engage, Three Houses, Heroes on phone)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. Player Phase: every one of your units may move and then act (attack, item, or Wait), in that order only. A unit that has acted turns grey and cannot be touched again. Canto (Canter in Engage) lets some units move again after acting, and Engage removed even that if the unit took a counter-hit. Then Enemy Phase: every enemy does the same. The defense inside the turn is the counterattack: whoever you hit hits back on the spot, so "move then attack" always gets an answer at the moment of the attack, from the target himself. That is the family's fairest version of an answer, and it is the closest thing here to our block card.

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. The phase banner: PLAYER PHASE in blue, ENEMY PHASE in red, a sweep across the screen with a short sting, half a second, then gone. A unit's move range is blue and its attack range red the moment you pick it up. Hold a button and every enemy's reach is shaded at once (the danger zone). Before you confirm an attack a small forecast shows both sides' damage and hit chance. Spent units are grey. Battle animations are cutaways, skippable. Fire Emblem Heroes on phone: drag a unit and the path is drawn as you drag; lift to commit; the danger zone is a toggle.

STEAL. Three things. The half-second phase banner in the mover's colour with a sting (A's YOUR MOVE flash is this). The danger zone: shade every tile a defender guards while you hold a piece, so the closed lanes explain themselves. Drag-to-move with the path drawn and a lift to commit (Heroes proves it at phone size). HURT. Greyed spent units and Wait only make sense when you move many pieces a turn; in A there is one piece a turn, so nothing needs greying.

VERDICT: adopt the banner, the danger shading and drag-to-move; reject the phase turn. Source: Fire Emblem Wiki on Canto and Engage's Canter (move after acting, lost after a counter-hit). The banner, danger zone and Heroes drag are from memory.

## Advance Wars (and Wargroove)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. Every unit: move then fire, or fire without moving; artillery and rockets fire only if they did not move. Units that acted go grey. End Turn. The other side does the same with its whole army. Wargroove is the same skeleton with commander powers and positional critical hits, and the August research already found its mirrored full-team movement tedious; it is.

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. A full-screen "Day 4" card with the commander's portrait opens each side's turn. Movement range shaded, a path drawn as the cursor travels, and a damage forecast in percentages on both sides before you confirm an attack. On one handheld two people passed the console at a "next player" screen, which exists because of fog of war; with nothing hidden, we do not need it.

STEAL. The forecast on the attack before you confirm, as a number. Ours is SHOOT with its price on the button ("3 · hard · contested"), which A already has. HURT. A turn that lasts minutes while the other person watches. That is the exact shared-phone wait the memo worries about, and it says the number of hand-offs is not the problem; the length of each one is. A's hand-offs are three to four seconds.

VERDICT: reject the turn, keep the forecast. Source: Wars Wiki for the turn structure; the Day card and the forecast are from memory of the games.

## Into the Breach (2018; on phone via Netflix, 2022)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. The enemy shows its whole plan first: every attack it will make next turn is drawn on the board with arrows and target squares. Then you take three mechs, each move then act, no move after acting, and you may undo a move until you act. Then the enemy carries out exactly what it showed, then shows the next plan. The player is the defense, and the game is "read what they will do, answer with small moves".

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. Everything on one 8 by 8 board, no zoom ever. On phone: tap a mech, tap a tile (or drag), an Undo Move button sits there until you act, one action button row at the bottom. The enemy turn plays out with each attack landing in sequence; the hand-off is that your mechs light up and the Undo button returns.

STEAL. Two things. The board is fully revealed and nobody guesses: our floor already works this way (every guarded tile is a rule, not a secret), so keep it that way and show it. And the undo before the commit: a move can be walked back until the action is taken. HURT. Nothing on screen; the one danger is the think time a fully revealed board invites, which is why A keeps the defense's legal set small (one defender, his range).

VERDICT: adopt. This is the phone-proven shape of "everything revealed, then the small informed answer" that A's defensive step is built on. Source: Android Police's Netflix guide (tap or drag to move, Undo Move before acting, no movement after acting), TouchArcade and Droid Gamers reviews (touch controls suit it).

## Marvel's Midnight Suns (2022)

WHAT ONE TURN CONTAINS. Three heroes, one hand of cards. Per turn: three card plays, two redraws, and ONE move, for the whole team, not per hero. Moving is free and separate from the cards; the cards are the actions and they are where the cost lives. Enemies show who they will hit before you play. Enemy turn follows.

HOW IT SHOWS THE TURN. The hand across the bottom, the counters (plays, redraws, moves) as small pips, enemy intent lines drawn from each enemy to its target. Hand-off is the enemy turn banner and their attacks playing out.

STEAL. One move per turn for the whole team, as a single resource: that is A's MOVE (any of your five, once) stated by a shipped game. And "movement is free, the cards are the price" is the owner's law in someone else's game. HURT. Midnight Suns gives the move and the card plays in the same turn: move one hero, then play three actions, and the enemy answers after all of it. That is flow B's shape (move one, then pass or shoot), and it works there because the enemy answers with a whole turn. With a one-step defense, move-then-shoot in one turn leaves the step with nothing to answer, which is your own worry said back.

VERDICT: adopt the single team move; keep Midnight Suns as the honest argument for B, and note why B needed its "a shot is a turn by itself" rule. Source: PlayStation Blog's combat explainer and Twinfinite (three card plays, two redraws, one move per turn). Enemy intent lines from memory.

## DOES IT CHANGE THE RECOMMENDATION

No. Flow A stands, and this family is the strongest confirmation of it yet, for three reasons.

1. Every game here answers "move then shoot" with yes, and every one of them pays for it with a whole enemy turn or a pre-committed trap (overwatch, Hero Sight, the counterattack). You have ruled out the whole defensive turn (too many taps) and the research ruled out the trap (reads as luck). Take those two away and move-then-shoot has no answer left, which is exactly your sentence: "then defense doesn't really have the option to respond." A's rule (move, they step, shoot) is what remains.
2. The two members of this family that reached phones and stayed praised there, Into the Breach and Midnight Suns, are the two whose shapes are already inside A: everything revealed then a small informed answer, and one move per turn for the whole team.
3. The family's real gift is not its turn but its screen: it has spent twenty years making a single move readable before it is committed. A should take that craft. None of it changes A's grammar, taps or clock, so no new candidate is warranted.

Three adaptations to fold into flow A, each landing on an existing beat of A's table (beat numbers from the chess concept):

| Beat in A | Adaptation | From | What changes on the phone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 and 8 (offense holds a piece) | Every dot carries its preview: a move dot shows the shot from that tile ("3 · hard · open"), a crossover dot shows its question price; the path is drawn on the floor to the dot under your thumb. | Kingdom Battle's cover shields and line on the ground; XCOM's two-colour tiles | No new taps. The player reads the destination before the move, so a move is never a guess. |
| 4, 5, 7 (any move) | Drag to move: press the piece, the path draws as the thumb moves, lift on the dot to commit; lift anywhere else and nothing is spent. Tap-tap stays as the alternative. | Fire Emblem Heroes, Into the Breach on phone | One gesture instead of two taps for most moves; a mistaken tap no longer hands the turn away. |
| 3, 5, 7 (defense holds a piece) and 4, 8 | While a piece is lifted, shade every tile the other side guards, lightly, so a closed lane looks closed rather than merely missing a dot. | Fire Emblem's danger zone | Nothing new to tap; the eight-tile rule explains itself on the floor. |

One rule idea for later, not for the flow: Kingdom Battle's team jump (moving onto a teammate throws you further) is the screen in tile form. If playtests show A's off-ball moves feel too expensive (its filed weakness), a ball handler whose path passes a teammate standing beside his own defender could gain a tile. That is a rule change and it is yours to rule; I am filing it, not proposing it.

One warning from the family for the camera: every one of these games zooms only on the resolution (the shot, the hit) and never on the selection, and XCOM's players turn even that off. A's "the lift is the highlight, the camera is not" is right; the family says do not soften it.
