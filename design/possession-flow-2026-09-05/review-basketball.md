# The 09-06 message, read as a coach and as a game designer

What I read: the memo (flows A, B, C, One More), DESIGN.md sections 1 to 4 and 7, the sports lens, the three-moves concept and its critique. What I checked in the code today: the court is 15 squares long and 8 wide; distance is a king's move, so a diagonal step costs the same as a straight one; the middle column (the eighth) counts as backcourt, so the front court is seven columns; a pass of up to 3 squares is free unless a defender stands next to the passer on the rim side, a pass of 4 to 6 squares is free only with a clean lane and a medium card otherwise, a pass of 7 or more is a hard card; after a made basket the point guard inbounds from behind his own baseline, the shooting guard drops back to the third column as the receiver, and the other three already stand at their front-court spots; the shot contest is any defender within one square of the shooter, with no memory of who was just beaten; a failed steal costs the slide and nothing else; the 24 restarts every turn; the game is four quarters of six possessions, or a race to 11.

Two readings of his turn, and I do the numbers under both:

- Reading 1, his list taken literally: the ball moves only by pass or crossover. A plain dribble to an open square is not a thing.
- Reading 2, the shipped game kept: a plain dribble to open squares is a ball action too, and uses the turn like a pass does.

I think he means reading 2 and did not list the dribble because it is obvious. I have not assumed it; where it matters I say so.

## 1. Three turns then shoot, as the shot clock

### Is it a good stand-in for the 24

Yes, with one correction. He has found a real hole: the 24 restarts every turn today and nothing caps a possession, so a team can pass forever. A turn count is the right fix for a turn-based game, and a visible count both sides watch drain was the strongest single device in the three-moves concept even in the critique that rejected the concept.

The correction: his two counts (three in the backcourt, three more after half court) are not the shot clock. They are the 8-second rule and the 24 stacked end to end. In real basketball the 8 sits inside the 24; crossing half court does not give you a fresh 24. His version gives up to six turns a possession, each turn holding a free move plus a ball action plus a defensive answer. That is a long possession and a long stall budget. The fix is in section 6.

On the retirement of flow C: the sports lens retired "three moves" on Football, Tactics and Glory, where the three actions come in a row with no answer between them, so the defender watches. His three turns are each answered. The waiting complaint does not transfer. What does transfer is the research's thinner warning about several small reaction windows, and the critique of the three-moves concept already weighed that and called the countdown worth keeping. So the retirement of C does not settle his idea either way. I am saying that so nobody cites the lens against him.

### The three dribbles line

Partly true, and useful only as flavor. Youth coaches cap dribbles in practice drills, usually two, sometimes three, to force passing and cutting. That is a practice constraint, not a rule of basketball; the game has no dribble limit. (From memory of coaching practice; I have not checked a written source.) It works as a teaching hook on the screen: "three, then shoot" is a sentence a first-timer can repeat. It misleads if it goes into the rulebook as a basketball fact, and it conflates a dribble (one man moving the ball a few squares) with a turn (a free move, a ball action, and the defense's answer). Use it in the coach's mouth, not in the rules.

### The geometry, made basket

Start after the inbound, attacking the right rim. Ball with the shooting guard at column 3 (range 2). Point guard at column 1 (range 3). Small forward, power forward and center already at columns 12 to 14. Front court begins at column 9. The ball needs to gain six columns.

Reading 1 (pass or crossover only):

- Turn 1. Free move: the point guard steps from column 1 to column 4. Ball action: the shooting guard passes to him, 1 square, free. Defense answers with one step.
- Turn 2. Free move: the power forward drops from column 12 to column 10 (range 2). Ball action: the point guard passes from column 4 to column 10, a 6-square pass, free if the lane is clean. Against the Man shape the defenders stand at columns 11 to 13, so the lane is clean. Ball in the front court. Two turns.

Reading 2 (plain dribble allowed):

- Turn 1 as above, the ball reaches the point guard at column 4.
- Turn 2. He dribbles 3 to column 7. Still backcourt.
- Turn 3. He dribbles 3 to column 10. Front court, on the last turn of the count.

So a point guard who walks it up alone uses exactly three turns from the inbound, and a team that passes ahead of the ball crosses in two. The count of three is reachable by every route against a half-court defense, with no slack for pure dribbling and one turn of slack for the pass game. That is close to what the 8 does in real basketball: it is never felt unless somebody is pressing or dawdling.

Against the Diamond Press (defenders at columns 1, 4, 4, 8 and 12): the inbound is a pressured dish, a medium card, before any count starts. Then the same two-turn pass route works if the receiver runs to a square whose lane misses the interceptor at column 8; run him to the far sideline and the lane is clean; run him up the middle and the pass is a medium card. The press's counter is that its interceptor steps after turn 1, having seen where the receiver went. That is a read on both sides, which is what a press should be. Verdict: the backcourt count makes the press a real weapon for the first time. Today a press can only hope for a card; it cannot force anything.

The hole in reading 1: a lone ball handler cannot advance. A center who steals the ball at the rim with no teammate within 6 squares and no defender in front of him has no legal way to move it. If the dribble is gone, that is a broken position. I assume it is not gone.

### The geometry, fast break

A steal at column 12, the new offense attacking the left rim, so its front court is columns 1 to 7. Every man on both teams stands in columns 9 to 14. The floor to the left is empty.

- Turn 1. Free move: a wing runs from column 12 to column 10. Ball action: the stealer passes 2 squares (free unless a hand is on him) or, under reading 2, the point guard dribbles 3 to column 8.
- Turn 2. Free move: the wing runs to column 8. Ball action: the point guard dribbles to column 5, or the ball is passed ahead again. Front court in two turns.
- Meanwhile the beaten team has had two steps: its point guard (range 3) gets from column 10 to column 4. The break arrives with one defender back. That is a real fast break: numbers, one man back, shoot before the second arrives.

With his reset, crossing in two turns grants three fresh turns. The break can slow down and set up while the defense gets one more man back each turn. Real basketball gives no fresh clock on a break; the 24 runs from the steal. His version is generous to the team that took the ball, which matches his own rule (the team that took it keeps the advantage), so I would call it a choice, not a bug. The count-of-four in section 6 tightens it without killing it.

### What the half-court reset does

- To the fast break: makes it unhurried. The advantage still decays one defender per turn, so shooting early is still rewarded, just not forced.
- To the press: gives it a target (three turns) and a payoff (a violation). The 6-square free pass over the top is the press's weakness; the interceptor's step after each turn is its answer.
- To the half-court offense after a made basket: three backcourt turns are three free off-ball moves for the men already in the front court, answered by three defensive steps, before the front-court count even starts. That is setup time nobody asked for. The stall budget is in section 4.

## 2. One More, with a standing big man still contesting

### Does it build the strategy he describes

Yes, and it is the most basketball thing in the message. Beat your man, meet the big. A rim protector who stays home makes the drive a three-gate play: the crossover card, then the layup card, then the defense's contest card or block gamble. A spread defense that chases shooters leaves the paint empty and the drive becomes a two-gate play. Pack the paint and give up threes, or chase shooters and give up drives. That is the trade every real defense makes, and here it is chosen by where you stand, which is the whole defensive game.

Two things must be true for it to work, and one of them is not true in the code today:

1. The beaten man cannot be the contester. The shipped contest picks any defender within one square of the shooter and has no memory of who was beaten. A one-square blow-by lands the handler next to the man who bit, and that man would contest. That makes the blow-by a lie. Rule: the beaten man is out of the play for the One More; anyone else within a square contests as normal. Verified in the code.
2. The One More must be shoot or pass, the player's choice, never a second crossover. His message says the shot in range and a free pass out of range. Read literally that deletes drive and kick, the most basic play in basketball: blow by at the top of the key, the big steps up, kick to the corner. If the One More in range is shot only, the driver has to shoot into the big every time. Let him choose.

### Does it make the crossover the only move worth making

No, if the costs stand. The crossover is the one action that gives two things in one turn without a defensive step between, so it is the tempo move. Its price is a card, and a wrong answer is a stumble that hands the defender a steal card (shipped). Rough numbers with assumed hit rates, marked as assumptions: easy card 85 percent, medium 70, hard 55.

- Drive: medium crossover, easy layup, 2 points, empty paint: about 0.7 x 0.85 x 2 = 1.2 points, and either miss is a turnover.
- Same drive into a rim protector who wins his contest card 55 percent of the time: about 0.7 x 0.85 x 0.45 x 2 = 0.5 points.
- Pass to an open man, defense steps, open three next turn: 0.55 x 3 = 1.65 points if the step cannot reach him; open mid-range: 0.7 x 2 = 1.4.

So the pass game is worth more when a man comes open, and the drive is worth more when the defense has spread to stop the pass game. Neither dominates. A strong-knowledge player will drive more, which is the north star (knowledge is your athleticism), and the defense's counter is to stand a big in the paint, which is his strategy. The cheap play would be a point guard with easy crossover cards driving every turn against an empty paint; the counter exists, so it is a style, not a hole.

### What the offense does against a packed paint

Kick out and swing. One free off-ball move per turn puts a shooter in a corner; a clean-lane pass reaches him; the defense's one step can cover one shooter. Corner to corner is 7 squares on this court, a hard card, so the swing goes through the top and takes two turns and two defensive steps. It becomes cat and mouse with the defense one step behind if it reads right and one step late if it reads wrong. That is depth. Against a 2-3 zone the wing defender already stands next to the corner, so the corner is contested from the start and the offense has to move him first. Also basketball.

One thing I have not checked: DESIGN section 2 says a power forward's screen eases an adjacent teammate's shot question. The code's screens only stop a defender from gating a drive; nothing eases a shot. If that rule is ever built, his turn (free off-ball move, then shoot) becomes "move the screener, then shoot" in one turn with no answer, which is move-then-shoot by the back door. Watch for it.

## 3. The defense getting a move and an action

### The steal

Move then steal every turn is the slot machine the sports lens warned about, doubled. One step reaches adjacency almost every turn; the ball handler would have to run from his man every turn to avoid a card. The shipped steal chain is: the defender's card, then the handler's protect card (easy for a point guard, hard for a center), then a tap battle with the offense favored. With assumed rates (defender right 70 percent, point guard protects 85, center protects 55, tap battle 40 for the defense) a single attempt takes the ball about a third of the time against a point guard and nearly half the time against a center. Four attempts in a possession and the offense keeps the ball perhaps one time in five. Hoop League Tactics' review said exactly this: you cannot dribble or pass without the ball being stolen, and it is annoying. A blow-by on the wrong answer does not fix that; it is a positional cost against a 30 to 45 percent turnover chance.

What fixes it is the shape the game already ships: the steal replaces the step. One thing a defensive turn: step, or steal if you are already next to the ball when your turn starts. That "already" matters: it means the offense had a turn to get away and chose to stay in traffic. Add his cost (a wrong steal is a blow-by) and the steal is a gamble with an opportunity cost and a downside. If playtests still show spam, the second lever is basketball's own: a wrong steal is a reach-in foul, and team fouls put the offense on the line. That adds a system, so it is the second lever, not the first.

### The pass-lane pick

"You have that in the game somewhere right?" Not as a pick. What exists, and how it shows: a pass whose flight passes within about a square of a defender's body is a medium card if it is 4 to 6 squares long; a short forward pass is free unless a defender stands next to the passer on the rim side; anything 7 or longer is a hard card. So a defender picks a lane by standing in it, and the offense sees the price on the receiver at the moment of passing (the priced ring the memo already adopted). On the defense's turn nothing shows lanes; the defender has to imagine them. The classic rules in DESIGN section 4 mention a lockdown defender who can "jump a lane" once a possession; it was never built.

A hidden lane pick on top of that (I secretly declare I am jumping the pass to Curry) is the blind pre-commitment the research rejected by name: it dominates or reads as luck. And there is a plainer reason: on a shared phone nothing is hidden. The other player is looking at the screen. A secret pick is impossible in one of the three ways to play.

So: the step is the lane pick. Make it visible on the defender's turn: when a defender is lifted, faintly tint the pass lanes his new square would cut. That is the informed answer the research wants, and it teaches lane defense without a word.

### The free pass after a blow-by, when the defense stands in lanes

Free of the turn, not free of the price. The outlet after beating the press is a pass like any other: throw it into a body and it is a card. If it were free of every price, a right crossover card would buy a guaranteed clean catch anywhere within 6 squares, every time, and the defense's positioning would stop mattering the moment a card is won. Keep the lane rules on it. The memo's One More already says no defensive step; that is enough of a reward.

### Does it become rock paper scissors

Only if the lane pick is hidden. With full information and alternating answers it is chess: each side sees the last move and answers it. Rock paper scissors needs a simultaneous or secret choice, and the only secret in his message is the lane pick. Drop that and the exchange stays a read, not a guess.

### Is the defense fun

The defense already has an action every turn; it is just not a tap. Standing next to the shooter is the contest. Standing in the lane is the price on the pass. Standing in the paint is the rim protection. Basketball defense is position, and this game has made position the whole of it. What the defense needs is not a second tap but to see what its square does: the tinted lanes above, the contest ring already shipped, the closed lane already dark. One step, drawn honestly, is a full turn.

## 4. The cheap plays that break it

1. The offense that never crosses over. Pass, step, pass, step, shoot. A legitimate style, not a break. Its ceiling is the count: three front-court turns means two passes then a shot, and the shot is contested if the defense read the last pass. Real NBA offenses average about three passes a possession (from memory of tracking data, not checked), so the ratio is in the right neighborhood and a hair tight. Fine.

2. The defense that steals every turn. A break as written; fixed by steal-replaces-step plus the blow-by cost (section 3).

3. The stall. DESIGN section 7 counts a game in possessions, not seconds, and race to 11 has no clock at all. If no mode runs a real game clock (I have not checked whether any does), stalling burns the opponent's patience and nothing else: it is griefing, not strategy, and his count turns an unbounded grief (today) into a bounded one. What his reset does is set the bound at six turns, with each turn holding two offensive things and a defensive step. That is a long grief. Section 6 makes it four. If a timed mode exists, the stall becomes real strategy and the count is exactly the 24's reason for existing: Fort Wayne beat Minneapolis 19 to 18 in November 1950 by holding the ball, and the 24 arrived in 1954 to end that.

4. The automatic corner three. Not automatic. His turn has the shot as a ball action, so a pass and a shot are two turns with a defensive step between: every catch-and-shoot allows a closeout. Against the Man shape a range-2 wing reaches the corner in one step; against the 2-3 zone the wing already stands next to it. What the reset does make automatic is three backcourt turns of front-court setup for the three men already there, answered by three steps. Even, but slow.

5. Two I found that he did not name. First, the crossover into a One More shot that the beaten man contests (section 2, a code fact). Second, the setup snap: after a made basket three offensive men already stand in the front court, so "cross half court" is a fact about the ball only, and the backcourt count times the ball while the real work happens ahead of it. That is a quirk of the shipped shapes, not of his rule, but his rule makes it visible.

## 5. What real basketball does, and where the analogy helps or misleads

From memory, stated once: NBA 8 seconds to cross (since 2001, 10 before), college 10; the 24 since 1954; the 14-second reset after an offensive rebound (NBA 2018, FIBA 2014); five seconds to inbound everywhere; defensive three seconds in the NBA since 2001, and no such rule in college or FIBA.

| Real rule | His stand-in | Helps a first-timer | Misleads |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 seconds to cross | Three backcourt turns | "Get it over half court" is the first thing anyone learns, and it gives the press a point. | The real 8 sits inside the 24. His reset gives more time after crossing, not less. A player who knows basketball will expect one clock. |
| The 24 | Three front-court turns | Two passes then a shot is a possession with a shape. Both sides watch the count. | The real clock ticks while you think; his count does not, so the 24 that restarts each turn is the only thing timing a decision. Say so on screen: the number is turns, the ring is seconds. |
| 14-second reset on an offensive rebound | Not addressed | Nothing yet. | If a live offensive rebound grants a fresh three, the offense that misses gets more turns than the one that scored. Two turns after a board is the honest analog. |
| Five-second inbound | Not addressed; the 24 covers the inbound turn | Nothing to learn. | Nothing. Leave it. |
| Reach-in foul | A wrong steal is a blow-by | "Reach and you get beat" is true coaching. | The real deterrent is the foul and the bonus. A blow-by alone does not deter a defender who wins his card most of the time. Keep the foul as the second lever. |
| Help defense | A big already in position contests the One More | "Beat your man, meet the big" is the truest sentence in the message. | Real help rotates: the big steps up and someone drops to his man. Here help never rotates because the One More has no defensive step; the big only helps if he was already there. It plays like a zone. A man-to-man player will feel the help never comes. That is fine as a strategy game; it should be said out loud so nobody expects a rotation. |
| Defensive three seconds | Not addressed | Nothing. | The strategy he wants to build around, a big camping the paint, is illegal in the NBA and legal in college and FIBA ball. His call, but it is a ruling: which game is this? The offense already has three-in-the-key; a matching defensive rule would kill his rim protector. I would rule it legal and say so in the rulebook. |

## Scores

Strategy depth, as written (defense gets a move and an action, a lane pick possible, two counts of three): 5 out of 10. The count, the free-move-then-action turn and the One More with a contest are real depth; the steal-every-turn and the hidden lane pick turn it into a slot machine and a guessing game respectively, and the six-turn possession dilutes every decision.

Strategy depth, with the changes below: 8 out of 10.

Basketball feel, as written: 7 out of 10. The backcourt count, the press with a payoff, the outlet pass off a beaten press, the rim protector, and reaching having a cost are all basketball. The misses: a beaten man contesting, help that never rotates without saying so, two clocks where basketball has one inside the other, and (if reading 1) a ball handler who cannot dribble.

Basketball feel, with the changes below: 8 out of 10. The remaining gap is the help that never rotates, which is a design choice worth keeping.

## 6. The smallest changes that fix the worst problems

In order of the damage they prevent. None abandons his idea; each is one sentence in the rulebook.

1. The defense does one thing a turn. Step one man, or, if a defender already stands next to the ball when the defensive turn starts, try the steal instead of stepping. A wrong steal is a blow-by. No second action. (Kills the steal slot machine. Keeps his cost.)

2. The step is the lane pick, and it is drawn. No hidden lane declaration. When a defender is lifted on his turn, tint the pass lanes his target square would cut. (Kills rock paper scissors. The only version that works on a shared phone.)

3. The beaten man is out of the One More. Any other defender within a square contests. And the One More is the player's choice of shot or pass, never another crossover. (Makes the blow-by true. Restores drive and kick.)

4. One count, not two. Four turns to shoot, and the ball must be across half court by the end of turn two. Drawn as four balls with the second one marked "half". Live balls start the same count where the ball lies; an offensive rebound, if it is a live ball, gives two. If a paper walkthrough shows four is too rushed, his three-and-three with a cap of five total is the fallback. (Puts the 8 inside the 24 where basketball has it. Halves the stall. Keeps the fast break generous but not idle.)

5. The plain dribble stays a ball action. Moving the ball to open squares uses the turn, exactly as pass and crossover do. (Closes the stuck-handler hole under reading 1. I am assuming this is what he meant.)

6. A ruling, not a change: the camping big is legal. Say it in the rulebook as the college and FIBA rule so an NBA-minded player is not surprised.
