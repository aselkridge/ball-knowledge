# Critique: Menu Against Menu

Judged against brief.md, scout.md and the shipped rules in DESIGN.md (sections 3, 3b and 4 were checked for the block card, the release meter, the role ranges and the clock wording). Three lenses, then the doubt, flaws, strengths, verdict.

## Lens 1 · The first-time player

I am a tester on a 390 wide phone, no coach, never read a rulebook. I walk the eleven beats.

**Beat 1, hand-off.** A horn, a gold slam that says WARRIORS BALL, a ring on Curry. If I am playing online I know I am the Warriors because that is the only team I have. On one shared phone the slam names a team, not a person. Nothing on screen has ever told me "you are gold." First possession of my first game, I do not know if WARRIORS BALL means me. Confusion one, and it is the very first thing I see.

**Beat 2, my first move.** The header says "Curry has the ball. Your move." That sentence is the best thing in the concept: it says whose ball, whose turn, and what to do, in seven words. Then three pieces move on their own (Green, Looney, Thompson) and nobody says why. The announcer's last line was "Curry walks it up." I just watched the defense's shapes settle, now three more men slide, and I have no idea whether that was the other side moving. Confusion two. Then the row: four buttons, each carrying a second line ("3 lanes open", "3 open, 1 covered", "3 pts · hard · Davis on him", "let the play run"). At 390 wide that is four columns of about 90 pixels with two lines of text each. I can read DRIVE, PASS and SHOOT. HOLD says "let the play run" and I do not know what the play is. Confusion three, small. I tap DRIVE, tiles light in three colours, I tap a green one. I know what I did because the announcer says "Curry goes left" and a trail stays on the floor. Good.

**Beat 3, the defense answers.** The row drops, rises purple, header says "Curry drove left. Your answer." Online this is perfect: the colour flipped, a sound played, the phone buzzed, the clock froze. I know it is not my beat. On one shared phone I am still holding the phone and the screen says "Your answer." Your, meaning me? The header never names the team. Confusion four, and it repeats at every one of the nine hand-offs in this possession until the tester learns the colours. Meanwhile a five second ring is draining around the purple row. I have to notice, hand the phone across, and my friend has to read four buttons with sub-labels and pick, in five seconds, or the game picks for him in silence ("the defense holds if nobody taps"). A first-time defender on a shared phone will lose that beat without knowing it happened. Confusion five, and this one has teeth.

The concept says the defender who would move gets a thin ring "as the thumb rests on that button." Resting a thumb is not a phone gesture a first-time player discovers; a first-timer taps. So I tap HELP and Gobert moves, and I learn who moved from the announcer, after the fact. I did not choose Gobert; the engine did. Acceptable, but I felt steered on my first defensive decision.

**Beat 4, the pass.** PASS pulls the camera back and puts five face chips above the row with prices. This is the fix for the owner's zoom complaint and it works: I can see all five, I tap a face, the ball goes. I know what happened because the ring jumped to Looney and the announcer said so. Clear.

**Beat 5.** Same as beat 3, and by now I know the purple row is the other side. Fine.

**Beat 6, the priced pass.** The chip read "Thompson · medium · Reaves in the lane." I did not read "medium" as "you will be asked a question." A card takes the whole screen. Small surprise, not a real confusion, because the card is self-explaining. But if I answer wrong, the concept says the ball is Reaves's "at once: no slam, no snap, no play step." So the biggest possession change in basketball, the steal, is the one hand-off with no callout at all. The row changes colour and a ring appears on Reaves, the same signal as an ordinary defensive beat. The owner said "it should be very clear whose possession it is." Here it is quieter than a routine slide. Confusion six, and it is the worst one, because it happens exactly when the tester is most rattled.

**Beats 7 to 9.** The rhythm is learned by now. The prices on the buttons change and I can see why (the middle lane went dark after Help; Curry's chip went WIDE OPEN after Double). Clear.

**Beat 10, the shot.** SHOOT reads "3 pts · hard · Davis on him." I tap, the card takes the screen, I answer, the release meter fires. The concept says the Lakers "hold the block card on their side during the same 15 seconds." Online, fine. On one shared phone there is one screen and it is showing my question. Where is the defender's card? The concept does not say. That is not a confusion for the tester, it is a hole in the design, but it lands on the tester as a screen that does not know what to do.

**Beat 11, flip.** Horn, slam, camera swing. Clear.

**Count.** Six moments of confusion for a shared-phone first-timer (beats 1, 2, 2 again, 3, 3 again, 6-on-a-miss), three for an online first-timer (beats 2, 2 again, 6-on-a-miss). Four of the six are fixable with one sentence each (name the team in the header; narrate the play's own step; keep a short slam on a steal). The five-second defensive ring on a shared phone is not fixable with a sentence.

**Is the hand-off visible without reading?** Online, yes, and better than anything shipped: colour, sound, vibration, trail, and the clock that only ticks on your beat. On a shared phone, only if you already know which colour you are. On a steal, no.

**Score: 6 / 10.** The row and header are a real step up; the shared-phone hand-off and the silent steal drag it down.

## Lens 2 · Tempo and waiting

**The concept's own arithmetic, checked.** Taps by beat: 2, 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1, 2. Offense 11, defense 4, total 15. That matches its claim. Seconds by beat, cards excluded: 3, 5, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 3, 3, 3, which is 41. Add two cards at up to 15 each: 41 to 71. The concept says "about 45 seconds plus the time spent on two question cards." Honest. Its "common case" of one card and three ball beats comes to about 26 seconds plus one card, so roughly 32 to 40. Its claim of "closer to 30" is a touch rosy but not a lie. Its "Today's shipped possession is about 20" taps picks the top of the shipped range (16 to 20); the fair comparison is 15 against 16 to 20.

**So is it faster?** In taps, barely, in total: 15 against 16 to 20. The difference is who taps. Today the offense does nearly all of it (about 15 to 18 of the taps); here the offense taps 7 to 11 and the defense 3 to 5. Per person it is a real reduction for the offense, roughly a third to a half fewer taps, and the taps that are gone are exactly the ones the owner named (tap a player, move, tap a player, move). In seconds, it is the same length as today: 30 to 45 with one card, up to 70 with two, against the shipped 45 to 75. The possession is not shorter. It is redistributed. That is a fair thing to be, but the concept should say "same length, split evenly" rather than let "Tempo" imply a shorter possession.

**The 24 second clock against the beats.** It ticks only on the offense's beats: 24, 19, 15, 12, 9, 6. Each offensive beat costs 3 to 5 seconds and there is no reset on a pass, so the offense gets about five beats a possession before the buzzer. In the sample that is drive, pass, pass, pass, shoot, with the shot going up at 6. That is basketball-shaped and I like it. But it means a first-time offense thinking 8 seconds a beat will be buzzered on beat four, every time, until it learns the row. Expect a lot of shot-clock violations in game one. The shipped rule says :24 per turn for both sides (DESIGN.md section 3; the 07-23 note "defense clock is now :24 too, it's chess"). The concept freezes the clock on the defense's beats and gives the defense a five second ring instead. It says the freeze out loud; it does not say out loud that the defense's 24 has become 5.

**The 15 second question clock.** The shot clock stops when a card opens and resumes after. Clean. No collision.

**Waiting, online.** The offense waits 4 to 5 seconds per defensive beat, four or five times a possession: 16 to 25 seconds of watching a purple ring per possession, with the shot clock frozen so no pressure and nothing to do. The defense waits 3 to 5 seconds per offensive beat plus every card. The concept claims "the true idle wait is about 8 seconds" because the defender holds the block card during a contested shot. That is true for the shot. It is false for the priced pass in beat 6 and for any crossover card: there the defender sits through 15 seconds with nothing on his screen. The longest idle wait is 15 seconds, and the concept's Numbers section says 8. That number is not honest.

**Waiting, shared phone.** Here the arithmetic turns. Beats 2 through 10 are nine hand-offs of the phone in one possession, against about four today (defense pick, offense pick, offense free moves and action, one defensive slide). Each physical pass of a phone costs a second or two and a glance. Over a 24 possession game that is around 200 phone passes, against about 100 today. And the defense's five second ring starts draining the moment the row turns purple, before the phone has crossed the table. On a shared phone this concept is slower and more fiddly than what is shipped, and its defensive beat defaults to nothing for a slow-handed pair.

**Over a game.** 24 possessions at 35 to 50 seconds is 14 to 20 minutes online, in a strict ball, answer, ball, answer rhythm, about 100 defensive calls and 100 offensive verbs per game. Race to 11 is six to eight possessions, four to seven minutes. The rhythm is regular enough that the row becomes muscle memory by the second quarter, which is the good reading. The bad reading is that 100 four-second defensive decisions a game is the "several small reaction windows" shape by count, whatever the concept says about it. The concept's defence against dullness (the prices punish Contest every time) is a claim, not a measurement, and it says so.

**Is it faster and less tedious, or just different?** Less tedious for the offense online, yes: no figurine tapping, no DONE button, no ladder. Same length in seconds. Worse on a shared phone. Different more than faster.

**Score: 6 / 10.**

## Lens 3 · Rules and research

**Question gates on shots and crossovers.** Kept exactly. Shoot from the tile, crossover through a man, pass through reach or under pressure. Wrong is a miss or a turnover. No change.

**The tile sets the price.** Kept, and made visible on the SHOOT button every beat. Good.

**Every defender guards eight tiles, closed lanes.** Kept. The four words move one defender at his role range and the guarding rules do the rest. One new rule underneath: the engine chooses which defender moves (nearest for Contest and Back off, nearest off-ball man for Help and Double). That is an input change, not a guarding change, and the concept says so. It should give a little: a coach who wants a named defender to step has no way to say it. Rule holds, concept adds a fifth-tap variant; fine.

**The 24 second clock.** Changed twice. First, from a per-turn clock to a per-possession clock that only ticks on offensive beats; the brief itself describes the 24 as a possession clock, so the concept is following the brief, but DESIGN.md still prints "per turn" and the owner needs to rule which. Second, the defense's thinking time drops from 24 to 5 seconds. Here the rule should give, because a 24 second defensive clock on every one of five beats would make a possession three minutes long, and the owner asked for fast. But five is the wrong number for a human. The concept picked it for tempo, not for reading four buttons. Eight to ten seconds, with the default announced ("Lakers hold"), keeps the tempo and stops the silent forfeits.

**Plays picked once per quarter, half or game.** Kept, and made the engine of the idea. But the concept adds something the owner did not rule: a play is now "a starting shape plus three or four scripted steps" that advance at the start of every offensive beat. The owner ruled a list of plays with a small 2D board showing the floor shape. Scripted step sequences are new. They carry the whole off-ball game, they need authoring for every play against every defensive shape, and the concept admits they can railroad a possession into Hold and a timeout. The concept should give here for the first build: ship plays as standing shapes (which is what the owner described) and add scripted steps as a later layer if playtests want them.

**Offense-only timeouts.** Kept on the button, broken in the small print: "The defense gets its play list at the same stop." The owner said timeouts are the offense's tool to change its play. If the defense also changes at the offense's stop, the offense has paid a timeout to reset both sides, which halves the point of having it. Concept should give: only the side that called it changes.

**Live-ball continuation after steals.** Kept in the mechanics (no reset, winner is the character where he stands, first defensive answer after his first beat). Two additions the owner did not rule: the winner's teammates "run one fast-break step" automatically, and there is no slam. The fast-break step is in the owner's spirit ("unless it's a fast break"). The missing slam is a mistake, covered in Lens 1. The rule should stay as the concept has it and the callout should come back.

**The walk-up.** The concept automates the inbound and the walk over half court after a made basket unless the defense's play is a press. That is a real rule change said out loud with a real reason (Curry at three tiles a beat needs three beats to cross an empty floor). Concept should win this one; owner should rule.

**The block card and the release meter.** I checked: both are shipped law (DESIGN.md section 3b, locked 07-27; "the block card is the defense's whole counterplay on contested looks"). The concept is not inventing them. What it does not say is how the block card works on a shared phone or against the computer when both cards run "during the same 15 seconds." Today's game already answers that somehow; the concept needs to inherit that answer rather than assume two screens.

**Research verdict: no mid-beat prompts to the defender.** The defense's beat is a full beat, so the core loop passes. The block card during the shooter's card is a prompt to the non-active side inside the offense's beat. The research says two things here: "no mid-beat prompts, ever" (Blood Bowl 3) and "give the defender something to do inside the question beat" (finding A7). The concept takes the second without mentioning the first. Since the block card is already shipped, this is not the concept's sin, but it should name the collision.

**Research verdict: one informed, last-moving, small defensive answer reads fair; several small reaction windows read as unbearable.** The concept claims it is the first and "never" the second. That is not true. It is the first, four or five times per possession. Each answer is informed, capped and last-moving, which is the fair shape; but there are four or five of them, which is the count the research called unbearable. The concept should own this instead of denying it. Should the rule give? Provisionally, yes: the scout shows the "unbearable" finding rests on snippet-level sources, the alternating one-piece shape was never weighed on its merits because the earlier brief forbade it, and the owner asked for exactly this on 09-05. But it is a bet that only a playtest count can settle: how many defensive answers a possession before a tester says "just let me shoot."

**Research verdict: safe moves first, then the gamble.** Kept, and cleanly: Drive into a clean lane, a free pass and Hold cost nothing; the card is always the last thing.

**The owner's words on 09-05, where the concept overrides them.** Two places, and only one is said out loud. First, the owner said "movement of the person with the ball or somebody off the ball is free, so we're still keeping that as free." The concept has no off-ball move at all; off-ball players are moved only by the play. It justifies this by saying the owner "walked that back himself on the fifth," but what he walked back was everybody-moves-every-time, not the one free off-ball move. That is a misreading of the brief, and it removes the one thing that lets a human coach put a shooter where he wants him. Second, the owner said "they can still move and then pass"; the concept says no, two beats, and argues its case out loud. That one is allowed by the brief. The first is not.

**Does it quietly reintroduce tap-every-player?** No. Nobody taps a figurine. The pass is one chip tap. The only place taps could creep back is the "tap a defender first, then the word" variant, which the concept lists as a cost, not a default. Clean pass on this test.

**Score: 5 / 10.** Most locks kept and visible on the buttons; but a misread of the owner's off-ball rule, an unruled scripted-play layer, a defence play change at the offense's timeout, and a denial of the reaction-window count.

## The owner's doubt

"The offense picks a player and moves them, then the defense picks a player and moves them and then the offense chooses to shoot or pass? And then the offense goes again to start it over? That feels confusing."

**Yes, this concept answers it, on structure.** The confusion in the owner's sentence comes from a turn with three parts: I move, they move, then I act, then it all starts again. The concept collapses the offense's turn to one part. There is no "move, then shoot." There is a verb, then an answer, then a verb. A player never has to know whether he is in the moving phase or the acting phase, because there is only one phase and it has one row. And "then the offense goes again to start it over" stops feeling like starting over because the prices on the four buttons have changed: the same row, different numbers, so the second beat reads as the next line of a story rather than a reset. The header sentence ("Curry has the ball. Your move." / "Curry drove left. Your answer.") is the digest, and it fits in one breath.

Where the answer is incomplete is the second half of his question: "how will you make the visual part clear so that the repeated flow feels fluid and fun and not tedious." Fluid online, yes. On a shared phone the flow is nine hand-offs and a five second fuse, and that is the opposite of fluid. And the concept's honesty about whether four or five defensive answers a possession stays fun is "needs a playtest," which is the right answer but not a yes.

## Flaws

1. **Shared-phone play is undesigned.** Nine phone passes a possession, a five second defensive ring that starts before the phone has moved, a silent "hold" when it runs out, and a block card that needs two screens. Severity: serious, fatal for the shared-phone mode as written. Fix: on a shared phone the defensive ring does not start until the receiving player taps once ("Lakers, tap when ready"); the block card runs the way the shipped game already runs it on one phone; say all of this in the concept.

2. **A steal has no callout.** A lost card hands the ball over with no slam, the quietest hand-off in the design at the loudest moment in basketball. Severity: serious. Fix: a short slam ("STEAL. LAKERS BALL") with no shape reset and no play step; the live-ball rule is about positions, not about silence.

3. **The free off-ball move is gone, against the owner's words.** He kept one free move for "the person with the ball or somebody off the ball." The concept has no way to move a teammate by hand. Severity: serious. Fix: HOLD becomes MOVE, a tap on a teammate's chip then a lit tile, at role range, free; "let the play run" is what happens if you tap nothing, or lives on a long press.

4. **Scripted play steps are a new, unruled layer.** Every play now needs three or four authored steps that behave against every defensive shape, and a bad script leaves the offense with only Hold and a timeout. Severity: serious for the first build. Fix: plays are standing shapes in the first build, as the owner described; add scripts later if playtests want the off-ball players to do more.

5. **The defense changes its play at the offense's timeout.** Halves the value of the offense-only timeout. Severity: minor, one sentence. Fix: only the caller changes.

6. **The five second defensive ring with a silent default.** Too short to read four sub-labelled buttons, and losing it makes no sound. Severity: serious. Fix: eight to ten seconds, and the default is announced ("Lakers hold").

7. **The play's own step is not narrated.** Three men move at the start of the offense's beat and the announcer says nothing, so it reads as the other side moving. Severity: minor. Fix: one announcer line per step ("Thompson curls off Green's screen"), or run the step inside the previous hand-off animation so it is over before the row rises.

8. **The header never names the team.** "Your move" on a shared phone means nothing until the colours are learned. Severity: minor. Fix: "Warriors, your move. Curry has the ball."

9. **Button sub-labels are too dense for 390 wide.** Four columns, two lines each, three facts on SHOOT. Severity: minor. Fix: one fact per button (points and a colour for the question level); the detail is on the tile or chip after the tap.

10. **Which defender moves is shown only on a thumb rest.** Not a gesture a first-timer finds. Severity: minor. Fix: all four candidate defenders wear a faint ring colour-keyed to the four buttons while the purple row is up.

11. **The wait number is wrong.** "True idle wait about 8 seconds" ignores the 15 second pass and crossover cards during which the defender has nothing. Severity: minor, but it is the kind of number the owner will quote. Fix: say 15, and say why the shot is the exception.

12. **The concept denies it is the many-windows shape.** It is one fair window, four or five times a possession. Severity: minor as a claim, serious as an unmeasured bet. Fix: say so, and name the playtest count that settles it.

13. **The shot clock is spent across about five beats.** A slow first-time offense gets buzzered on beat four in every early game. Severity: minor, playtest. Possible fix: the clock only starts ticking after the first hand-off animation and the play step, not during them.

14. **The defense's 24 becomes 5 without saying so.** The freeze is said out loud; the shrink is not. Severity: minor. Fix: one sentence in the rules section.

## Strengths

Only what the beat table shows.

- **One sentence says the turn, every beat.** "Curry has the ball. Your move." and "Curry drove left. Your answer." (beats 2 through 10). No ladder, no note bar, no "tap a player." It is the first design in this game where the tester never has to work out whose beat it is from the board.
- **The row is fixed and the prices move.** DRIVE, PASS, SHOOT, HOLD in the same four slots, with the numbers changing after every answer (beat 4: the middle lane goes dark after Help; beat 8: Curry's chip goes WIDE OPEN after Double). That is what makes beat five not feel like beat one.
- **Drive and Shoot are separate beats, so the defense always answers once.** Beat 9 sits between the swing pass in beat 8 and the shot in beat 10. This is the mechanical answer to the owner's doubt, and it is visible in the table, not just claimed.
- **The pass beat pulls the camera back and uses face chips** (beat 4). Nobody hunts for a figurine, the zoom bug is gone by construction.
- **The clock ticks only on your beat** (24, 19, 15, 12, 9, 6 across beats 2 to 10). The clock is a fourth turn signal without being a fourth thing on the screen.
- **The gamble is last.** Every card in the table is the last thing in its beat, and the cheap verbs come first (beats 2, 4, 8 are free; 6 and 10 are cards).
- **The announcer talks like a broadcast.** "Gobert slides over to help!", "Lakers double Thompson!", "Curry from deep... SPLASH." Never a tile name. That is the voice the owner asked for.
- **The tap count is honest and checkable.** Fifteen, by beat, and it adds up.

## Verdict

**REPAIR.** The core is right and it is the first shape that actually answers the owner's doubt: one verb, one answer, the same row with different prices, a sentence that says the turn. Keep the row, the header, the face chips, the clock that ticks only on your beat, and the announcer. Repair six things before it goes to option round: design the shared-phone mode (tap-when-ready before the defensive ring, block card as the shipped game does it); bring back a short slam on a steal; restore the one free off-ball move the owner kept (HOLD becomes MOVE); ship plays as standing shapes and drop the scripted steps until a playtest asks for them; lengthen the defensive answer to eight to ten seconds and announce the default; and keep the timeout's play change to the side that called it. Then fix the small honesty problems (the 15 second wait, the "never several windows" claim, the defense's 24 becoming 5) so the numbers the owner quotes are true. What remains after repair is a real bet that no shipped game has made: four or five informed defensive answers a possession. That bet is the owner's own direction, the research against it is thin, and only a playtest can call it. Send it with the bet named.
