# Beat Your Man

**One line:** Every possession is you against the man guarding you: you make one move, he takes one step, again and again, until you shoot or give it up.

## The loop

The plays were picked once, so at every dead ball all ten pieces glide into their shapes by themselves and the ball is already in the hands of the play's ball handler. From there the possession is a duel between two lit pieces: the ball handler and the defender whose eight tiles hold him (the man on the ball). On the offense's beat the ball handler does exactly one thing from a three-button row: MOVE, PASS or SHOOT. On the defense's beat the defense does exactly one thing from its own row: HOLD, STEP (one defender moves, up to his range) or STEAL (only when a defender is on a tile next to the ball). Then it is the offense again. A pass moves the duel to a new pair and the camera follows it; the defense always gets one step before the new man can shoot. The possession ends on a shot (make: dead ball, other team's play snaps in at the far end), on a turnover (wrong crossover answer, a stolen ball, or the shot clock hitting zero), or on a miss and a rebound. Steals and defensive rebounds do not reset anything: the piece that took the ball is lit immediately as the new ball handler and that team's beat starts at once, which is the advantage the owner ruled they keep.

The rhythm a player can say out loud: **my move, his step, my move, his step, shoot.**

## One possession, beat by beat

The example: Cream City on offense, The Town on defense, a made basket just happened. Screen assumptions for every row: the scoreboard strip stays at the top (his ruling); the whole court stays in view at all times (no zoom on tap, ever, because the zoom hid pass targets); the two duel pieces wear rings (bright in the squad colour for the ball handler, dim red for the man on the ball) with a thin line drawn on the floor between them; every other piece is plain. The last move's from-tile and to-tile stay tinted until the next move, the way chess apps show the last move, so nobody has to ask what just happened.

## Move then shoot?

**No.** One action per beat, and shooting is an action.

The reason is the duel itself. If the handler can dribble past his man and shoot in the same beat, the defender's step never happens and beating your man means nothing beyond the crossover card. With the step in between, beating your man creates a real basketball problem for the defense: the beaten man can only recover one tile, so someone else has to come, and whoever comes leaves someone open. That is where the kick-out, the open corner and the closeout all come from. The whole possession story in the table above exists only because there is a step between the drive and the shot.

What the defense gets: exactly one step between every offensive action and the shot, taken after seeing the action, with full information, capped by the mover's range. That is the shape the defense research found reads as fair (the one small move made last), and it never prompts the defender in the middle of an offensive beat. What the offense gets in return: a beaten defender's next step is capped at one tile, so a won crossover buys a real window; the catch-and-shoot defender has to reach an adjacent tile to make the shot contested; and a failed steal leaves that defender beaten for the next exchange, so the defense pays for its gambles too (Blood Bowl's rule: a failed gamble costs you).

## Why it reads clearly

The owner's doubt, answered plainly. There is no separate "then the offense chooses to shoot or pass" step and no round to "start over." Every offense beat is the same question with the same three answers: MOVE, PASS or SHOOT. Every defense beat is the same question with the same three answers: HOLD, STEP or STEAL. The screen on your fourth beat looks exactly like the screen on your first. Shoot is not the end of a sequence; it is one of the three things you can do on any beat, and choosing it ends the possession. So the process a first-time player learns is one sentence: my move, his step, my move, his step, until I shoot.

Whose turn it is, said in three places that agree and never repeat each other:

1. **The buttons.** You have buttons only on your beat. The three-button row rises when it is yours and folds into a slim strip when it is theirs. This is already the game's own grammar ("it is your turn exactly when your buttons exist") and it is the one place that says what to do: the header on the row ("Curry has it") plus the three verbs. No banner and no grey bar repeat it, which is the "said once" rule.
2. **The clock.** One number, one meaning: the shot clock, top right, big, in the offense's colour, counting only while the offense's row is up. It glows and grows under five and a buzzer fires at zero (shot clock violation, ball flips, announced). On the defense's beat it dims and holds; the defense's eight seconds are a shrinking ring on the lit defender, the piece they are about to touch. So "the corner is bright and counting" always means "my possession is burning."
3. **The announcer line.** Under the scoreboard, one line, always past tense, always names the player and the thing that happened: "Green slides over, still in front." Never a tile name. It reports; it never instructs. That is the split with the row: the top says what happened, the bottom says what you can do.

The hand-off itself is a small ritual that takes one second and cannot be missed: the row folds or rises, the clock swaps state, the ring appears or vanishes, and there is a sound for each direction (a dribble bounce when it becomes the offense's beat, a sneaker squeak when it becomes the defense's). A possession flip is the big version of the same ritual: the slam in the new team's colour, the horn, the pieces gliding into shape. Small ritual for a beat, big ritual for a possession, same vocabulary.

The 3D chess question he raised is answered by three borrowed habits: the piece in hand is lit and its legal squares are lit, the last move stays tinted from and to, and the camera never leaves the board. The duel line between the two rings is the one addition basketball needs that chess does not: it tells the player which defender the possession is about right now.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

- The taps per beat are two, and the beats are short, so the back-and-forth has the tempo of chess bullet play rather than a form to fill out. Nobody taps five players in a row.
- Each beat is a real decision with a visible consequence: go around or through, hold or help, steal or stay honest. The crossover card is a gamble; the step after it is the answer; the kick-out is the punishment for helping. Players learn to read the floor because the floor is small and the story is one pair at a time.
- The defense has something to do every few seconds and every choice it makes changes who is open. The defender also plays a card inside the shot beat (the contest) and inside the steal beat, so the waiting player is never only waiting.
- The camera and the rings make every possession a story with a name in it. The announcer says the name. Beating your man is a moment; getting beaten is a moment; the closeout is a moment.
- Nothing is on screen that the beat does not need: three buttons or none, one clock, one line, two rings.

## Fit with the locked rules

- **Question gates:** untouched. Shoot is priced by the tile (near, mid, arc, logo). Move through a guarded tile is a crossover card at the locked prices (full price head-on, one step easier from the corner, one step harder for deep crosses). A pass through a guarded lane is a card. A steal is the card duel the game already has (the defender's card, then the handler's). A contested shot answered right runs the defender's contest card and the release meter as locked. Wrong answers are turnovers or misses as today.
- **Eight-tile guarding:** it is what defines the duel. The man on the ball is the defender whose eight tiles hold the ball handler's tile; if two do, the one who was on him last beat, then the one nearer the rim. The duel line is drawn to him.
- **Closed lanes:** shown as dark tiles when MOVE is open, refused, never duelled, exactly as locked.
- **The 24 second clock:** kept at 24, with one rule I would state out loud: it counts only while the offense's row is up. It freezes during question cards (they have their own 15) and during the defense's beat (which has its own 8). The offense never pays for the defense's thinking, and a slow defender cannot run the offense out. If the clock today runs as wall time across everything, that is the change.
- **Plays picked once:** at every dead ball both shapes snap in with no taps, and the ball starts with the play's handler. The four off-ball men hold the shape; they are the context. MOVE can target a teammate instead of the handler (one extra tap, no card, uses the beat), which keeps the owner's "off-ball movement is free" ruling without a free-move phase.
- **Offense-only timeouts:** a small TIME OUT control lives in the scoreboard strip and lights only during the offense's beat. It opens the play list.
- **Live-ball continuation:** a steal, a wrong crossover answer picked up by the defender, or a defensive rebound lights the taker as the ball handler on the spot, with that team's beat first. No snap, no inbound. That is also the owner's fast-break exception to the once-picked play.
- **Two things I would change, said out loud:** (1) the inbound becomes automatic and shown, not tapped: the play says who takes it out and who catches, the announcer says it, and no inbound clock is needed because there is no inbound beat; (2) the trip up the floor after a made basket is not played, the shapes snap in at the attacking end. Both cut beats from every possession, which is where the research said the seconds should come from (speed the middle). If the defense's picked play is a press, the inbound catch would happen in the backcourt and the duel would start there, one exchange earlier; that is the one place the trip up the floor would return.

## Weaknesses

- The 24 counts only on the offense's beats, so a possession is four or five decisions at four to six seconds each. That is the chess pace he asked for, and it will feel fast to a first-timer on day one. If it is too fast, the clock goes to 30 or a pass refills four seconds; the structure does not change.
- The off-ball men stand still unless the offense spends a beat on one of them. The play shape does the spacing, but a July complaint was "pieces just sit there," and this concept accepts that on purpose (the research's finding: everyone else is context). The upgrade, if playtests want more motion, is the play running itself: one scripted cut per exchange, drawn on the small board when the play is picked. That is a bigger build and it makes the offense's own pieces move without a tap, which the defense research warns against for defenders; for the offense's own play it is honest, but it must be shown.
- Nothing about turn structure was ever verified in a shipped game. This is design reasoning, and it needs the same playtest the research asked for.
- The defense's beat can degrade into HOLD, HOLD, HOLD for a player who does not see a reason to move. The OPEN mark and the duel line are the reads; the coach's first tip has to teach "step to stay in front" and nothing else.
- The rule that picks the man on the ball when two defenders qualify has to be written down and drawn; if the line ever points at the wrong man the whole framing feels like a lie, and the telegraph research is clear that a telegraph the player can catch lying is worse than none.
- A pass changes which two pieces are lit, and the camera glide has to keep both the passer and the catcher in view on a 390 pixel screen. That is a camera job to prove with screenshots before anyone plays it.
