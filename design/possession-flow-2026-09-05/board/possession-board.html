The possession board

Your question on 09-05: "what is the overall process, and is that easy to digest as a player?" Eight designers each drew a different way a possession could run, three critics went through every one, and this page carries what survived. Every phone frame is a mock drawn to phone size. The court, the pieces and the prices are the real game's; the flow is not. Nothing here ships until you pick.

1

Your doubt, answered

"The offense picks a player and moves them, then the defense picks a player and moves them, and THEN the offense chooses to shoot or pass? And then the offense goes again to start it over? That feels confusing."

It feels confusing because that sentence has two kinds of offensive turn in it: a moving turn and a shooting turn, with the defense wedged between them. Every clean version the panel found does the same thing: it makes every offensive turn the same turn.

One thing with the ball. Then they get one step. Again, until you shoot.

Move, pass or shoot are the three things, and shoot is on the row every time, so there is never a "then shoot" stage and nothing ever "starts over." The offense is not starting again after the defense moves. It is making its next move on a board that remembers, the way white moves again after black. The defense's step is not a turn wedged in; it is the answer to what you just did, made after seeing it, which is the one shape the August research found reads as fair.

Four things are on the screen and nothing else: the announcer line at the top saying what just happened, one clock in the top right wearing the colour of the side on the clock, the court with the ball handler lit and the last move still tinted on the floor, and a row of buttons at the bottom that exists only when it is your turn. The row's header is the one place the game says whose turn it is.

B

Move one, then pass. Or shoot.

Your own words read the other way: "they can still move and then pass." One offensive turn is a free move by any one player and then a pass, or a shot instead. The defense steps once after the whole turn. Half the hand-offs of A, and the shot is still always answered, because a shot is its own turn with no move in front of it.

What it buysThree or four hand-offs a possession instead of six to ten. The best fit for a shared phone. The drive-and-kick is one turn, which is how it feels in real basketball.
What it costsTwo things in one turn, so the row changes shape mid-turn (MOVE, then PASS lights). The rule is one breath longer than A's. A slow first-timer has two decisions on one clock.
Move then shootNo, by rule: the shot is a turn by itself, taken from where the ball already is, after their step. A drive that ends in a shot is two turns. That is what keeps it fair.
C

Three moves, then shoot

The same grammar as A with a visible cap: three balls in the strip. Every move spends one and draws one step from the defense. When the balls are gone the row shows only SHOOT, so both sides always know when the shot is coming.

What it buysThe most learnable shape on the board: three, then shoot. The defense knows the shooter on its last step. A possession is never more than ten beats.
What it costsEvery possession has the same skeleton, which may feel formulaic by the second quarter. Three moves cannot cross the floor on a live steal, so the fast break needs its own rule. The critics scored its tempo lowest of the alternating flows.
D

Build, answer, finish

The fewest taps on the board and no loop at all: the offense makes one move, the defense makes one move seeing the price of every shot on the floor, the offense shoots or passes to the man who shoots. Three words in a fixed order, then the ball changes hands.

What it buysSeven to eleven taps, 25 to 35 seconds, two hand-offs. The defense's move is a small puzzle with everything revealed. Nothing ever starts over.
What it costsIt removes the back and forth you asked for ("more like chess", "until there's a flip of possession"). No swing-swing-swing possessions. After a steal all ten players are at the wrong end and two beats cannot cross the floor, so live-ball play needs a separate rule. Pick this on purpose or not at all.
E

Lock and play

Both sides pick at the same time, then the beat plays out. The offense picks where the ball goes; the defense picks one of four calls. A guess only moves a price one step; the card still decides. Nobody waits for a turn.

What it buysEvery beat is a small reveal both players watch for. "He bit" and "beat him to the spot" happen every six seconds. Fewest offense taps of any candidate.
Why the critics killed it for this questionYou asked for a back and forth where the defense gets a turn to respond. This has the defense predict instead. On a shared phone the hiding is social, and the August research rejected hidden simultaneous calls by name (Tecmo Bowl). Worth a paper playtest some day; not the answer to this brief. Its tags (OPEN, TRAFFIC, CONTESTED on every piece) are worth taking into A.
F

The play pick, the way you described it

Once a quarter (or half, or game, by format), and at an offense's timeout. The list takes most of the screen; the small board above it moves your five into the shape of whichever play you tap, against the other side's pick. Plain words on every card. The shapes are the ones already in the game from the August playbook research.

Open on you: blind or sighted picks, and who picks first. The research's answer is on file (defense first, shown), and it was given for an every-possession pick; a once-a-quarter pick may want a different answer.

G

Madden, Rabbids and the role-playing games

Your pushback: "why not games like Madden and Rabbids as well? Or even other turn based RPGs?" The eight angles above were shapes of a turn, not games, and two of your three were thin or missing: Madden fed the play-runs concept and the play-pick screen, Mario + Rabbids appears in the archive only as the team-turns idea you offered yourself on 08-01 and called "wack", and the role-playing games appear nowhere. Three designers who have played them went back through twenty-two games. Files: lens-sports, lens-tactics, lens-rpg.

Madden and the sports gamesConfirm A from your side of the aisle. Madden's thirty-year answer to "how much defense can one human play" is one controlled defender while the rest run the call, which is A's defensive turn. Hoop League Tactics shows dribble, pass, shoot is a full offense when the defense answers each one. Blood Bowl rations move-then-act to once a turn and prices it: A already has that action, the crossover. Football Tactics and Glory is C in a different shirt, and its reviews praise the three-pip counter and complain about the waiting behind it, so C is retired on evidence. Retro Bowl cut the defense entirely, which is not a two-player game.
Mario + Rabbids and the tactics familyEvery one of them (Rabbids, XCOM, Fire Emblem, Advance Wars, Into the Breach, Midnight Suns) lets a unit move and shoot in one turn, and every one pays for it with a whole enemy turn or a pre-committed trap. You ruled out the whole turn on 09-05 (too many taps) and the research ruled out the trap (reads as luck). Take those away and move-then-shoot has no answer left, which is your own sentence. The two that reached phones and stayed praised, Into the Breach and Midnight Suns, are shapes already inside A. What the family gives is screen craft: a preview on every dot before you commit, drag-to-move with the path drawn and a lift to commit, and a light shading of every guarded tile while a piece is held.
The role-playing gamesPokemon, Final Fantasy, Persona, Paper Mario, Darkest Dungeon, Slay the Spire, Octopath, Baldur's Gate 3, Chrono Trigger. Every one that reads clearly does one thing from a fixed menu, narrates the result, then raises the other side's menu, which is A. The two that let one piece do several things a turn are the two with the most complained-about waits. Settled by them: the announcer line lands before the row rises (Pokemon's message box), a two-chip NOW and NEXT beside the clock (Final Fantasy X), the price drawn on the path before the tap (Baldur's Gate 3). Paper Mario's guard press is the block card you already have; add no more reflexes.

Four cuts to A that keep its grammar

  • The receiver is the button. With the ball in hand every teammate wears a ring with its price; tapping the ring is the pass. PASS stays lit on the row as the label. One tap, not two (Madden's icons, Retro Bowl). About thirteen taps become eleven.
  • The steal costs a blow-by. A wrong answer on the steal card gives the ball handler one free tile past the reacher with no defensive answer, so STEAL stays a gamble and never a habit (Hoop League Tactics' "cannot dribble or pass without a steal" complaint).
  • The play stays painted. On the offense's turn, a faint arrow from each off-ball player to his next spot in the picked play. Moving him along it is still one turn (NBA 2K's Playvision).
  • The shot call runs across the card. "Curry from deep..." starts on the SHOOT tap and finishes when the card resolves, so the waiting player is watching a shot, not a quiz (NBA Street).

One rule for you to rule, from Persona 5: One More

A drive through a defender's tile is priced by the crossover card as today. Answer it right and the offense acts again at once, no defensive step in between, capped at one per turn and never earned off a pass.

The defense already responded (it put a man there); you beat it with knowledge, which is the game's thesis; and the blow-by is how basketball rewards a beaten defender. It is the one place move-then-shoot is allowed, and it is earned out loud with a "ONE MORE!" slam. Without it the crossover card is barely worth taking in A (go around the man instead and the defense recovers anyway). With it, the chain is the danger, so the cap is the rule.

3

The recommendation, and what you rule

A, with the four cuts from section G and One More on your desk; B held as the shared-phone fallback. Build A as a paper walkthrough first (a printed court, two people, one afternoon), then a clickable mock on the real renderer, then the game.

  • Rule 1, the grammar: A (one thing a turn) or B (move one, then pass). Everything else on this page works under either.
  • Rule 2, the defense's step clock: 8 seconds is the panel's number. Today the defense has a 24 of its own (ruled 07-23, "it's chess, both sides get to think"); this changes that.
  • Rule 3, the shared phone: the phone lies between two people and both tap the same screen, or each end gets its own row like the buzz race. The hand-off count only matters under the first.
  • Rule 4, the play pick: blind or sighted, who first.
  • Rule 5, the steal: what a wrong answer costs the defender. The sports games say a blow-by.
  • Rule 6, One More: yes or no to the earned second action off a right crossover answer (section G).
4

What the research already says, and what it never asked

On file, from August: the defense's fair answer is one small step made after seeing the offense's move (Kill Team, Into the Breach); several small reaction windows read as unbearable (Magic Arena, Hero Academy, both on thin sources); safe moves first and the gamble last (Blood Bowl); a possession is one story about the ball handler and his man (Strat-O-Matic); the play menu is all visible, one tap, defense first and shown. Turn order at the level of which side acts came back empty three times and was closed.

Never asked: what a single turn may contain (move then act, and what the other side gets), how phone games make the hand-off and the clock impossible to miss, what a possession costs in taps and seconds in the shipped games nearest ours, and how 3D board games at phone size show the piece in hand without a zoom that hides the board. Your move-then-shoot doubt has been filed as open since 08-02.

Is a run worth your time? Yes, one narrow run of four questions, and it should not block the paper walkthrough: it feeds the second draft. The four questions and the games to study are in the memo and on row 239.
