The possession board

You asked: "what is the overall process, and is that easy to digest as a player?" This page answers that. Eight different ways a possession could run were drawn up, each one was picked apart by three separate reviewers, and then three more passes went through Madden, Mario + Rabbids and the turn-based role-playing games you asked about. What survived is here. Every phone picture on this page is a drawing at real phone size, not a screenshot of the game. The court, the players and the question prices are the real game's. The way the turns work is the idea being shown. Nothing on this page exists in the game yet.

The words this page uses

A possession
The whole stretch from when your team gets the ball to when the other team gets it. A basket, a miss they rebound, a steal, or the shot clock running out all end a possession.
A turn
One side's go inside a possession. The offense has a turn, then the defense has a turn, and so on. A possession is made of several turns.
The question card
The trivia question that fills the screen when you shoot, or when you dribble past a defender who is guarding you. Four answers, fifteen seconds. Get it right and the shot goes in or the dribble gets through. Get it wrong and it is a miss or a turnover.
The button row
The big buttons across the bottom of the screen: Move, Pass and Shoot for the offense, and Hold, Step and Steal for the defense. The buttons only appear when it is your turn, and the small line of text above them says whose turn it is and who has the ball.
A step
The defense's turn: one defender moves a short distance. That is the whole defensive turn.
A square that costs a question
The court is drawn in squares. Green squares are free to move to. An orange square means a defender is guarding it, so moving there brings up a question card first (that is the crossover). A dark square is blocked by two defenders and cannot be entered.
The shot clock
The 24 second clock in the top right corner. It only counts down during the offense's turns, and it stops while the defense is stepping and while a question card is up. The defense gets its own short clock for each step.
The announcer line
One line of text at the top of the screen, written like a TV announcer, that says what just happened: "Curry kicks it to Thompson in the corner!" It never tells you what to do; the button row does that.
The big word across the court
When the ball changes teams, a big word like YOUR BALL or CREAM CITY BALL flashes across the court with a horn, so nobody can miss that the possession changed.
The turn changing sides
The moment the offense's turn ends and the defense's turn begins, or the other way round. Your buttons fold away, the other side's buttons come up in their colour, and a sound plays. How many times this happens in one possession matters a lot when two people are sharing one phone.

Your question, answered

"The offense picks a player and moves them, then the defense picks a player and moves them, and THEN the offense chooses to shoot or pass? And then the offense goes again to start it over? That feels confusing."

It feels confusing because the way you described it, the offense has two different kinds of turn: a moving turn and a shooting turn, with the defense's turn squeezed between them. Every version that reads cleanly fixes that the same way: every offensive turn is the same kind of turn.

You do one thing with the ball. Then they move one defender. Then you do one thing again. That repeats until you shoot.

The one thing is always a choice of three: move a player, pass the ball, or shoot. Shoot is on the button row every single turn, so there is never a separate "now decide whether to shoot" moment, and nothing ever "starts over." When the defense finishes its step, you are not starting again. You are simply making your next move on a court that still shows what everyone just did, the same way in chess white moves again after black moves. The defense's step is not a turn wedged into yours. It is their answer to what you just did, made after they have seen it. When we studied other games in August, that was the one shape of defensive answer that players consistently said felt fair.

The screen shows four things and nothing else. At the top, the announcer line saying what just happened. In the top right, one clock, coloured for whichever side it is currently timing. In the middle, the court, with the player who has the ball glowing and the last move still tinted on the floor so you can see it. At the bottom, the button row, which is only there when it is your turn. The small line above the buttons is the one and only place the game says whose turn it is.

Can you move and then shoot in the same turn?No, never. You move, they get their step, then you can shoot. So the defense always gets one chance to react before any shot goes up. Back in early August you said it yourself: "defense first before you can shoot, right?" Beating your defender still matters, because a defender who just got beaten can only recover one square on his next step. That means a teammate has to come over and help, and whoever he leaves behind is now open.
The free off-ball move you wanted to keepThe Move button lets you move any one of your five players, whether or not he has the ball. Moving him uses up your turn, and then the defense gets its step. So you move the one player who matters right now, not all five. That is what gets rid of the "click move, click move, click move" problem you described.
The clockOne clock, top right. The 24 second shot clock only counts down while your buttons are on screen. It freezes while the defense is taking its step and while any question card is up. The defense's step has its own short clock, about 8 seconds (the exact number is yours to decide), shown in their colour in the same corner. When any clock gets under five seconds it grows and glows, and at zero a buzzer sounds.
When the turn changes sidesIt looks and sounds the same every time. Your buttons fold away, the other side's buttons rise up in their colour, a sound plays (a dribble bounce when it becomes the offense's turn, a sneaker squeak when it becomes the defense's), and the last move stays tinted on the floor. When the ball changes teams it is the big version: the big word across the court and the horn.
The numbers, honestlyIn one possession the offense makes four to six decisions and the defense makes three to five steps. That is about 14 taps on the phone and 35 to 50 seconds, including one question card. The longest the other player ever waits is the 15 seconds of a question card. The game as it is today takes about 18 taps and 45 to 75 seconds per possession, and most of those taps happen before the defense gets to do anything.
What the reviewers could not fixThe turn changes sides six to ten times in one possession. That is fine online and against the computer. On one phone shared between two people it has to be tested with two real people before we can say it works. Also, a wrong answer on a steal attempt has to cost the defender something, or people will try to steal every single turn.
B

Move one player, then pass. Or shoot.

This is your own words read the other way. You said "they can still move and then pass." So in this version, one offensive turn is a free move by any one player followed by a pass, or a shot instead of the move. The defense takes one step after your whole turn is done. The turn changes sides about half as often as in A, and a shot is still always answered, because a shot is a turn by itself with no move allowed in front of it.

What you gainThe turn changes sides only three or four times a possession instead of six to ten. That makes it the best fit when two people share one phone. The drive and kick-out happens in one turn, the way it feels on a real court.
What it costsYou are doing two things in one turn, so the buttons change partway through your turn (first Move lights up, then Pass). The rule takes one more breath to explain than A's. A first-time player who is slow has two decisions to make on one clock.
Move and then shoot?No, by rule. A shot is its own turn, taken from wherever the ball already is, after the defense has stepped. A drive that ends in a shot takes two turns. That is what keeps it fair.
C

Three moves, then shoot

This is the same idea as A, but with a visible limit. Three balls are drawn at the bottom of the screen. Every move you make uses up one ball and gives the defense one step. When the balls are gone, the only button left is Shoot. So both sides always know exactly when the shot is coming.

What you gainThe easiest version to learn: three moves, then shoot. The defense knows who is going to shoot when it takes its last step. A possession never runs longer than ten moments.
Why it was retiredEvery possession has the same skeleton, which may feel repetitive by the second quarter. Three moves cannot get the team down the floor after a steal, so fast breaks would need their own rule. And there is a real football game built exactly this way, Football Tactics and Glory, whose reviews praise the three-move counter and complain about all the waiting behind it. So this version is off the table unless you want it back.
D

Build, answer, finish

This one has the fewest taps and no back and forth at all. The offense makes one move. The defense makes one move, and while it does, it can see what the shot would cost from every spot on the floor. Then the offense either shoots or passes once to a teammate who shoots. Three steps in a fixed order, and then the ball changes teams.

What you gainSeven to eleven taps, 25 to 35 seconds, and the turn only changes sides twice. The defense's move is a small puzzle with everything visible. Nothing ever starts over.
What it costsIt removes the back and forth you asked for ("more like chess", "until there's a flip of possession"). There are no possessions where the ball swings around three or four times. And after a steal, all ten players are at the wrong end of the court, and a three-step possession cannot cross the floor, so live-ball play would need a separate rule. This one is a choice you would have to make on purpose.
E

Both sides pick at the same time

In this version nobody waits for a turn. Both players pick at the same moment, then the play resolves. The offense picks where the ball goes (drive, pass or shoot). The defense picks one of four calls (shade left, stay, shade right, or send help). A wrong guess by the defense only makes the shot a little easier or the drive a little harder. The question card still decides whether it goes in.

What you gainEvery moment is a small reveal that both players are watching for. "He bit" and "beat him to the spot" happen every six seconds. The offense taps the phone fewer times than in any other version.
Why the reviewers ruled it out for this questionYou asked for a back and forth where the defense gets a turn to respond. In this version the defense has to predict instead of respond. On one shared phone the other player can see what you tap. And when we studied other games in August, the hidden simultaneous pick was rejected by name (it is how the old Tecmo Bowl works, and it turns into rock paper scissors). Worth trying on paper someday, but it does not answer what you asked. One piece of it is worth keeping for version A: the little labels on every player that say OPEN, TRAFFIC or CONTESTED.
F

The play pick screen, the way you described it

This happens once a quarter (or once a half, or once a game, depending on the game format), and whenever the offense calls a timeout. The list of plays takes up most of the screen. The small court above the list moves your five players into the shape of whichever play you tap, shown against the play the other side picked. Every play card is written in plain words. The shapes are the ones already in the game from the playbook research we did in August.

Still your call: whether each side picks blind or gets to see the other side's pick first, and which side picks first. Our August research said the defense should pick first and the offense should see it, but that answer was for a pick that happened every single possession. A pick that happens once a quarter might deserve a different answer.

G

Madden, Rabbids and the role-playing games

You asked: "why not games like Madden and Rabbids as well? Or even other turn based RPGs?" Fair question. The eight versions above were built around different shapes of a turn, not around specific games, and two of the three families you named were thin or missing. Madden did shape one of the eight (the version where the play runs itself) and the play pick screen. Mario + Rabbids only appears in our notes because back on the first of August you brought up its team turns yourself and called the idea "wack." The role-playing games had never been looked at. So three more passes went through twenty-two games from those families. Here is what came back.

Madden and the other sports gamesThey agree with version A. Madden's answer to "how much defense can one person play at once" has been the same for thirty years: you control one defender while the other ten run the coverage that was called. That is exactly the defense's turn in A. Hoop League Tactics, the closest thing to our game on phones, shows that dribble, pass and shoot is a complete offense as long as the defense answers each one. Blood Bowl lets a player move and then act in the same turn only once per turn, and makes it risky; A already has that exact move, the crossover. Football Tactics and Glory is version C, and its reviews are why C was retired. Retro Bowl, the most successful football game on phones, removed the defense entirely, which is not a two-player game.
Mario + Rabbids and the tactics gamesEvery one of these (Rabbids, XCOM, Fire Emblem, Advance Wars, Into the Breach, Midnight Suns) lets a unit move and shoot in the same turn. But every one of them pays for that in one of two ways: the other side gets a whole turn of its own with every unit, or the defense sets a trap in advance that fires automatically. You already ruled out the whole-turn answer (too many taps), and the August research ruled out the automatic trap (it feels like luck). Take those two away and there is no fair way to allow move-then-shoot, which is what you said yourself. What these games do give us is craft on the screen: show what you will get from a square before you commit to it, let the player drag a piece with the path drawn on the floor and lift a finger to confirm, and lightly shade every square the defense is guarding while you are holding a piece.
The role-playing gamesPokemon, Final Fantasy, Persona, Paper Mario, Darkest Dungeon, Slay the Spire, Octopath Traveler, Baldur's Gate 3 and Chrono Trigger. Every one that is easy to follow does the same thing: you pick one action from a fixed menu, the game narrates what happened, then the other side's menu comes up. That is version A. The two games in this family that let one character do several things in one turn are the two whose players complain most about waiting. Three small screen details come from this family: the announcer line should finish before your buttons appear (Pokemon's message box does this); a tiny "now" and "next" marker beside the clock (from Final Fantasy X); and the cost of a move drawn on the path before you tap it (from Baldur's Gate 3). Paper Mario's timed button press during the enemy's attack is the same idea as the block card the defense already gets during a contested shot. No more reflex moments should be added beyond that.

Four small changes to version A that keep it simple

  • Tap the teammate to pass. When you have the ball, every teammate wears a ring showing what a pass to him would cost. Tapping the ring is the pass. The Pass button stays lit as a label so the button row still reads Move, Pass, Shoot. One tap instead of two. (From Madden's receiver icons and Retro Bowl.) A possession drops from about thirteen taps to about eleven.
  • A failed steal attempt costs the defender. If the defender tries a steal and gets the question wrong, the ball handler gets one free square past him with no defensive answer. That keeps Steal as a gamble, not a habit. (Hoop League Tactics' reviews complain that you cannot dribble or pass without risking a steal every time.)
  • The play stays faintly drawn on the floor. During the offense's turn, a faint arrow shows each off-ball player's next spot in the play you picked. Moving him along it still uses your turn. (From NBA 2K's play diagrams.)
  • The announcer call runs across the question card. "Curry from deep..." starts the moment you tap Shoot and finishes when the question is answered, so the other player is watching a shot, not a quiz. (From NBA Street.)

One new rule for you to decide on, borrowed from Persona 5

Driving through a defender brings up a question card, the same as today. If you answer it right, you get one more action straight away, with no defensive step in between. Only once per turn, and never after a pass.

Here is the thinking. The defense already responded by putting a man in your way. You beat him with knowledge, which is the whole point of the game. And in real basketball a beaten defender means a blow-by: the shot goes up before help arrives. This is the one place where moving and then shooting would be allowed, and you have to earn it out loud, with a big ONE MORE across the court. Without this rule the crossover is barely worth trying in version A, because you could just go around the defender and the defense recovers anyway. The danger is chaining several of these together, so the limit of one per turn is the important part.

3

The recommendation, and the six things you decide

Version A, with the four small changes from the Madden and Rabbids pass, and the Persona "one more" rule left to you. Version B is the fallback if sharing one phone turns out to be a problem. Version C is retired. The way to build it: first a paper walkthrough (a printed court, two people, one afternoon), then a clickable mock-up on the real court, then the game.

  • Decision 1, which version: A (one thing per turn) or B (move one player, then pass). Everything else on this page works with either.
  • Decision 2, the defense's clock: how many seconds the defense gets for each step. The suggestion is 8. Today the defense gets a full 24 seconds of its own, which you decided back in July because "both sides get to think." This would change that.
  • Decision 3, two people on one phone: does the phone sit between the two players and both tap the same screen, or does each player get their own set of buttons at their own end of the phone, the way the buzzer race already works? The number of times the turn changes sides only matters in the first case.
  • Decision 4, the play pick: do both sides pick blind, or does one side see the other's pick first, and which side picks first?
  • Decision 5, a failed steal: what it costs the defender. The sports games say the ball handler should get a free square past him.
  • Decision 6, the "one more" rule: yes or no to getting one extra action after a right answer on a crossover.
4

What we already learned from other games, and what we never looked at

Already learned, from the August research: the fairest defensive answer is one small move by one defender made after seeing what the offense did. Giving the defense several small chances to react during one offensive turn feels unbearable to players, although that finding came from thin sources. Good possessions do the safe moves first and the gamble last. A possession is really one story about the ball handler against his defender, and everyone else is context. The play menu should show everything at once with one tap to pick. Whether whole teams should take turns or possessions should alternate was asked three times and never got a real answer, so we stopped asking.

Never looked at: what a single turn should contain (can you move and then act, and what does the other side get). How games on phones make it impossible to miss that the turn changed and the clock is running. What a possession costs in taps and seconds in the games closest to ours. How 3D board games on a phone show the piece you are holding without zooming in and hiding the rest of the board. Your question about moving and then shooting has been sitting open since early August.

Is another research run worth your time? Yes, one small run of four questions, but it should not hold anything up. It would feed the second draft, not the first. The four questions and the games to study are written in the memo file that goes with this board.

5

Rules already written down that this would change

These are things we decided earlier and wrote into the rulebook. Going with what you said on the fifth of September would overturn each of them, so they need your say-so.

  • Picking a formation at every single inbound, with different menus depending on where the ball went out. Your pick-once-a-quarter idea replaces that.
  • "No play pick after the jump ball." You confirmed that in mid August. Your new idea puts a pick right after the jump ball.
  • Every off-ball player moves for free at full range each turn (decided in August). Now it is one free move per turn.
  • The free move draws no defensive response (also decided in August). Now the defense answers every move.
  • The order of free moves, then the defensive slide, then the action, and the step ladder on screen that you said you loved. All of it replaced.
  • The big word across the court only appears when the ball changes teams. With one-move turns, the turn changes sides many times per possession, so how each of those is shown needs a new rule.
  • The 24 second clock belongs to each turn, and the defense has its own 24 (decided in July). The new clock belongs to the possession and only counts the offense's decision time.
  • Substitutions happen at quarter breaks. Your idea uses timeouts for that, later on.
  • The August research advised against rebuilding how turns work at all. That was reasoning, not evidence, and you can overrule it.
6

Ideas that were dropped, and why

The play runs itselfYour team runs the play you picked as an animation, and the game pauses whenever the ball handler has a decision to make. The cleanest screen of all eight, but it takes away the free move you said you wanted to keep, and every play would have to be authored like a short film with three decision points. Kept as a future upgrade to version A: one scripted cut per turn, drawn on the small court when you pick the play.
The clock is the turn (real time)The 24 second clock runs for real, both sides move whenever they like, and only a question freezes the floor. That is a different game, not a turn-based one. Its home would be the streetball mode already reserved in the rulebook under the name Blacktop. Two pieces are worth taking anyway: the players walk into their play positions at the whistle, and the question card turns to face whoever is answering.
Both sides pick at the same timeVersion E above. Dropped for this question, but its OPEN, TRAFFIC and CONTESTED labels on every player go into version A.
