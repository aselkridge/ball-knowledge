The words this page uses

A possession
The stretch from when your team gets the ball to when the other team gets it.
A turn
One side's go. You take a turn, then they take a turn, back and forth until the ball changes teams.
A question
The trivia card that fills the screen. Four answers, fifteen seconds. Shooting always brings one up. Dribbling into a square a defender is guarding brings one up (that is a crossover). A long pass or a pass through traffic brings one up for the passer. Get it right and the thing you tried works. Get it wrong and it fails.
The balls (the shot clock)
The little row of balls above your buttons. Each ball is one of your turns. Two to get the ball over half court, then three to shoot. When the last ball is gone you must have shot, or the whistle says SHOT CLOCK VIOLATION and the ball goes to the other team. This is the shot clock of this game, counted in turns instead of seconds, and the coach's first run teaches it that way. The 24-second ring is a different thing: it times one turn, so nobody can sit and think forever. It needs a name that is not "shot clock" (see the open list).
A free move
Once a turn, before you do anything with the ball, you may move one teammate who does not have the ball. It costs nothing and you can skip it.
End turn
A small control that is always on screen for both sides. Tap it to give up the rest of your turn. On offense it still spends a ball.
The step
The defense's turn: move one defender. That is the whole turn, unless you go for a steal instead.
A steal
A defender who is already standing next to the ball handler when the defensive turn starts can try to take the ball. The defender answers a question. Wrong, and he is knocked to the side, leaving the ball handler an open lane past him. Right, and the ball handler gets his own question to hold on. If both are right nothing happens and the reach is spent. If the defender is right and the handler is wrong, the ball is stolen.
A blow-by
You dribbled into a defender's square, answered your question right, and he failed to stay in front of you. You are past him. When that happens you get one more thing straight away, a shot or a pass, before the defense gets its step. The screen says ONE MORE.
A lane
The straight line a pass would travel. A defender standing on that line makes the pass cost the passer a question. Nobody "picks" a lane in secret; you guard a lane by standing in it, and the screen shows you which lanes your defender is cutting while you hold him.
A live ball
The ball changes teams without a stop: a steal, or a rebound. Everyone stays where they are and the new offense has to bring the ball up the floor.
The glide
After a made basket, both teams slide into their chosen plays at the other end of the floor automatically, with the ball already in the point guard's hands. No inbound pass to tap, no walking it up.
A violation
The whistle. Either you did not get the ball over half court in time, or you did not shoot in time. The other team gets the ball.
1

The rules, all of them, on one screen

This is the whole rulebook as recommended. Eleven sentences. Everything below this section is these sentences played out.

  1. Your turn: one free move of a teammate without the ball (you can skip it), then one thing with the ball: dribble, pass or shoot. That one thing ends your turn. End turn is always on screen if you want to give the turn up; on offense it still spends a ball.
  2. Their turn: one defender moves. Or, if a defender is already next to the ball, he can go for the steal instead. Never both. End turn is on their screen too.
  3. A crossover: dribbling into a guarded square brings up a question. Answer right and the defender gets a question to stay in front of you. If he fails, you are past him and you get ONE MORE: a shot or a pass, right away, no defensive step in between. Once a turn. The man you beat cannot contest that shot. Anyone else standing next to you and between you and the rim still can.
  4. A steal is two questions and no tap race. The defender answers. Wrong, he is knocked to the side and the ball handler has an open lane past him. Right, the ball handler answers to hold on. Both right, nothing happens. Defender right and handler wrong, the ball is stolen.
  5. The balls are the shot clock: two turns to get the ball over half court, then three turns to shoot. The moment the ball crosses, any unused crossing ball disappears, so crossing early never banks a turn. After a made basket you are already over, so it is three. Miss the crossing and it is HALF-COURT VIOLATION; miss the shot and it is SHOT CLOCK VIOLATION.
  6. The clocks: the 24-second clock times each offensive turn, as today. The defense gets 10 seconds for its step. Let either run out and you lose that turn.
  7. Lanes: a defender guards a pass by standing in its path. While you hold a defender on your turn, the squares where he would cut a pass are tinted. There is no secret lane pick.
  8. After a made basket both teams glide into their plays at the other end, and you watch it happen: a short automatic clip, the ball to the point guard, the pass, everybody sliding into the play. After a steal or a rebound, play goes on from where everyone stands.
  9. Picking your plays: each team picks its offense the first time it has the ball and its defense the first time it does not. The defense picks first and the offense sees it. A timeout, which only the offense can call, brings the pick screen back.
  10. Bookkeeping: an offensive rebound gives two more turns. A timeout changes nothing on the balls. Over-and-back, three seconds in the key and out of bounds work as they do today, with the key counted in turns.
  11. Three seconds, counted in turns: a defender may stand in the key for two of his team's defensive turns. The count lives on his piece. On the third turn it says STEP OUT; if nobody moves him, the game walks him to the nearest square outside the key and that was the defense's step.

The big man, drawn both ways (you picked b)

Same moment in both: Cream City has the ball, you are on defense in your 2-3 zone, and Looney, your centre, has been standing in the key for two of your defensive turns. The key is the painted rectangle under the rim.

your rulingWay b: three seconds, counted in turns, and the game does the shouting

A big may stand in the key for two of your defensive turns. From the moment he steps in, his piece wears a small count. On your third turn the count turns red, his piece pulses, and the header says he must step out this turn. If you do not move him, the game moves him out for you to the nearest square outside the key, the announcer says it, and that was your step for the turn. No whistle, no free throw, nothing to learn beyond "two turns in the paint, then out".

Way c, not picked: he may camp, and a camped big contests softer

Nothing stops him. After two defensive turns in the key his piece wears a CAMPED tag, and from then on any shot he contests is one notch easier than a fresh contest: the offense's Shoot button says "contested, soft" instead of "contested". The defense sees the tag but nothing pushes him; the cost only shows up in the price of a shot that may never come.

Way b, plainlyLoud and physical: a count on the piece, a red pulse, a line in the header, and the piece moves. The defense cannot miss it. It costs the defense a step every third turn, so the paint opens for one turn and a beaten man can earn a real layup then. One rule sentence.
Way c, plainlyQuiet and priced: a tag on the piece and a softer word on the offense's button. The defense never loses a step and the paint is never empty, so a beaten man still meets the big every time, just with an easier question. The thing you were worried about, that the defense finds out late and quietly, is real here.
your rulingWay bPicked 7 September: "b, go with the count." It answers your own question, "where do we shout it out": on the piece, in the header, and with the move. And it is the only one of the two that ever gives a layup for free to someone who beat his man.
