# The sports video games, read for our possession

Written against the memo of 2026-09-05 (candidates A to E) and the two concepts nearest this family, One Piece One Move and The Play Runs Itself. Seven games, the ones with something to say. The question each time: what does one turn hold, what does the other side get, how does the screen say whose turn it is, and does it move our recommendation.

The short answer up front: this family confirms A, one thing with the ball then their one step, and it hands A three small cuts. It does not produce a new candidate. It also kills C on evidence rather than taste.

## 1. Madden NFL (the play call, the pre-snap window, the snap, the progression)

**What one turn contains.** A turn is a down. Before the snap both sides have a window: the offense picks a play from a screen, then can change routes, protection and motion; the defense picks its play and can shade coverage or shift. Both sides see each other's formation, not each other's call. Then the snap, and the down is real time: the quarterback reads through his receivers, each with a button on his head, and throws or runs. The defense controls one man; the other ten run the coverage that was called. The play clock is 40 seconds (25 after a stoppage), and at 15 seconds the adjustment menus close; a snap that never comes is a delay-of-game penalty.

**How it shows the turn.** The play clock sits beside the game clock, small until it is under ten, then it flashes and the whistle and flag say the rest. Whose turn is never asked, because both sides act in the same window and the snap is the hand-off: one sound, one camera drop behind the quarterback. The receiver icons are the single best piece of screen in the family: every legal target carries a button, the whole time, and the camera never zooms tight enough to lose one.

**Worth stealing.** The one controlled defender. Madden has known for thirty years that a human plays one defender well and eleven badly; the rest run the called coverage. That is our play picked once plus one step by one man, exactly. Second: the receiver buttons. When you hold the ball, every teammate wears a tappable ring with the price on it, no PASS button first.

**Would hurt us.** The pre-snap ladder. Audible, hot route, protection slide, motion, coverage shade: that is the tap-tap-tap complaint at scale, and Madden itself has spent five editions cutting it down and closing the window earlier. Our owner has already made this cut by moving the pick to once a quarter; do not let it creep back as per-possession adjustments.

**Verdict.** Adapt: the one-defender rule and the always-visible receiver icons go into A; the pre-snap adjustment stack stays out.

**Source.** Pre-play controls and the 15-second close of the adjustment window: Madden Prodigy's Madden 27 pre-play guide and Madden School's controls page (search results, not fetched in full). Receiver icons and the one controlled defender: from memory, well known.

## 2. NBA 2K (play calling, Playvision, the shot meter, the pass icons)

**What one turn contains.** No turns; a possession is 24 real seconds. The ball handler dribbles, passes with an icon on each teammate, or shoots with a timed meter. Plays are called mid-possession by holding a button and picking from a menu, and Playvision then paints the play's routes on the floor as it runs, so the ball handler can see where the cutter is going before he gets there. The defense picks a coverage from a similar menu and controls one man.

**How it shows the turn.** The shot clock is on the scoreboard strip, small, and turns red under five. Nothing tells you whose possession it is except the ball. The floor paint is the tell for the play.

**Worth stealing.** Playvision. The play we pick once a quarter must not vanish into a starting shape by the second possession. A faint painted arrow from each off-ball player to his next spot in the picked play, drawn on the floor on the offense's turn only, means the play is present in every possession without the offense having to run it. One move of a cutter along his painted arrow is still one turn (A's rule), but the player sees what the play wants.

**Would hurt us.** The mid-possession play menu (the two-hop tree the research already rejected for phones) and the shot meter: our shot is the question, and putting a timing skill on top of a knowledge skill double-gates the shot.

**Verdict.** Adapt one visual: the painted next cut. Reject the menu and the meter.

**Source.** From memory (NBA 2K's Playvision and pass icons are in every edition since about 2K14). The research's own NBA 2K25 finding on curated short sets is cited in the scout file.

## 3. NBA Street and NBA Jam (the announcer, the trick meter, the pace)

**What one turn contains.** No turns. Real time, two or three a side, no fouls, a Gamebreaker meter that builds on style and cashes as a special shot. The pace is the point.

**How it shows the turn.** The announcer. He never says where anyone is or what to do; he says what just happened, names the player, and starts the call before the shot lands ("from downtown..." then the result). Possession changes get a bark, not a banner. The screen has almost nothing on it.

**Worth stealing.** The two-part call around the card. The announcer line starts as the SHOOT tap lands ("Curry from deep...") and finishes when the card resolves ("GOOD!" or "off the iron"). That turns the 15-second question into a suspense beat for the player who is waiting, which is the longest wait in A and the one the memo cannot shorten. Also: the hand-off gets a bark, the flip gets a louder one, and the bark is past tense.

**Would hurt us.** The trick meter and the twitch. Both of these games are about hands, not choices. A Gamebreaker-style meter would be a second scoring path beside the question, which the owner's law forbids (cards raise floors, never ceilings).

**Verdict.** Adopt the announcer grammar for every beat, as A already does; reject the pace and the meter.

**Source.** From memory. The scout file records that NBA Street's play-by-play language was never studied by the research.

## 4. Retro Bowl, with Tecmo Bowl (the smallest play loop)

**What one turn contains.** Retro Bowl: one tap picks the play from three or four, the snap is automatic, then one gesture: drag from the quarterback to throw (direction and power in the drag), or tap to run and steer the carrier with a finger. The defense is not played at all; it is simulated. Tecmo Bowl: four plays, hidden pick on both sides, one button after the snap. A drive is a string of these, each ending in a result line (gain, incomplete, touchdown) and a fresh down.

**How it shows the turn.** The down and distance strip, the play clock small, the result printed after every play. You always know a play ended because the screen stops and prints it. The pick screen and the play screen are the same view; no scene change.

**Worth stealing.** The pass is one gesture. In A a pass is PASS then a receiver, two taps. Retro Bowl says: with the ball in hand, the receiver is the button. Tap a ringed teammate and the ball goes; the PASS word can stay on the row as a label, lit, so the row still reads "move, pass, shoot". That is the single largest tap cut available in A without changing its grammar. Second: the result line after every play, which is A's announcer line.

**Would hurt us.** Automatic defense. Retro Bowl is the most successful phone football game and it cut the defense entirely. Our owner wants the defense to have a turn, the research found automatic defense reads as luck or as a nightmare in the two closest games, and a two-player game needs two players. Tecmo Bowl's hidden pick was rejected by the research by name.

**Verdict.** Adapt the one-gesture pass; reject the no-defense loop.

**Source.** Retro Bowl controls (drag to throw, tap to run, defense simulated): Goomba Stomp's how-to-play guide and the Retro Bowl wiki, from search snippets. The wiki's Gameplay page returned a 402 and was not read. Tecmo Bowl: from memory.

## 5. Blood Bowl (the rationed move-then-act)

**What one turn contains.** A team turn: every player on the active side may activate once, and an activation is Move, or Block (hit an adjacent enemy), or one of the rationed combined actions. The rulebook rations by name: once per team turn a Pass, once a Hand-off, once a Blitz (a Move and a Block in one activation, the Block costing one square of movement), once a Foul. Any failed action is a turnover: the turn ends at once, moved or not. Tournament play puts four minutes on the team turn; Blood Bowl 3 puts a turn timer on screen. The other side gets nothing during your turn except the mid-turn prompts (dodge re-rolls, skill choices) that the research found to be exactly where Blood Bowl 3 bleeds.

**How it shows the turn.** The turn counter on the sideline (eight a half), the timer, and the turnover: a horn, the pieces freeze, the board hands over. Players who have acted are marked. The last action is not painted on the pitch; you learn to watch.

**Worth stealing.** The ration. Blood Bowl's answer to "may a piece move and act in one turn" is yes, once a turn, and that action is the gamble. This is the exact shape of the owner's move-then-shoot doubt, and Blood Bowl resolves it without forbidding it: it makes the combined action rare and risky. In our game the crossover already is this action (move past your man, priced by a card, in one turn), so A already contains the Blitz. It is worth saying that out loud in A: the drive is the one move-and-beat-your-man turn, and it is priced.

**Would hurt us.** The team turn. Move all eleven, then the other side moves all eleven, is the shipped complaint with more pieces and longer waits.

**Verdict.** Adopt the ration as a statement of what A already does (the crossover is the Blitz); reject the team turn.

**Source.** Blood Bowl 2020 core rules cheat sheet at bloodbowlbase.ru (fetched: "Once per team turn, a player on the active team may perform a Blitz action"; Pass and Hand-off also once per team turn; the turnover list). The Blitz square cost: BoardGameGeek and the Blood Bowl 3 Steam discussion, search snippets.

## 6. Football, Tactics and Glory (three actions, then three)

**What one turn contains.** Three action points a team turn, spent on any players: move, pass, dribble, tackle, shoot. When the three are spent, or a shot is taken, the other side gets its three. Defenders fire automatically by position during the attacker's turn (a tackle chance when you run past a man), which is the part reviews call luck.

**How it shows the turn.** Three lit pips at the top that go out one by one; the turn hands over when the last one goes. A very learnable counter.

**Worth stealing.** The visible counter of three is candidate C exactly. And the family's evidence on C is now in: reviewers praise the counter and complain about the waiting and the luck. Three offensive actions in a row means the defender watches for fifteen to thirty seconds with nothing to do but hope the automatic tackle fires, which is the Hoop League Tactics complaint in a different shirt.

**Would hurt us.** Both halves: the auto-defense in the attacker's block (the research's standing warning) and the three-then-three rhythm (long waits, every action of the three unanswered).

**Verdict.** Reject, and retire C on this evidence: the counter is nice but the wait behind it is the problem.

**Source.** Nintendo Life's review of Soccer, Tactics and Glory ("each turn gives you three moves... if you don't score within those three turns, the other team takes over"); Games Asylum and Big Red Barrel reviews for the three-action block; search snippets, not fetched in full.

## 7. Hoop League Tactics (the closest cousin, its offense side)

**What one turn contains.** Alternating offense and defense turns on a grid court. On offense the ball handler dribbles to a tile, passes, or shoots from where he stands; each costs stamina; the shot's odds depend on the shooter, the distance, and the defender in front. On defense you position to block passing lanes or contest. Single player only; the computer plays the other side. From memory: teammates off the ball are moved by the play or the computer, not the player, most turns.

**How it shows the turn.** A side-on court, the active player circled, buttons for the actions, a stamina bar per player. No shot clock in the basketball sense. The App Store reviews say what the screen cannot: "you can neither dribble nor pass without risk of having the ball stolen" at higher difficulty, making it "annoying to play"; and the defending system "is an absolute nightmare."

**Worth stealing.** The ball handler is the only piece you touch on most turns, and it never felt thin: dribble, pass, shoot is a full menu when the defense answers each one. That is A's offensive row.

**Would hurt us.** A steal chance on every dribble and every pass. This is the warning for A's STEAL button, which lights whenever a defender is adjacent. If STEAL is free to attempt, the defense will tap it every turn and the offense will feel Hoop League Tactics' complaint by possession three. The memo's open question 5 (what a wrong steal answer costs) is not optional; the family says it must cost, and the cleanest cost is a blow-by: a wrong answer moves the ball handler one free tile past the man who reached.

**Verdict.** Adapt: keep the three-verb offense; price the steal with a blow-by so it is a gamble, not a habit.

**Source.** App Store listing and reviews (fetched: the two quoted complaints); the TouchArcade review returned a 403 and was not read; turn structure from the search summary and from memory.

## Does it change the recommendation

No. The family confirms A, one thing with the ball then their one step, and it does so from the side the owner named. Madden's thirty-year answer to "how much defense can one human play" is one man while the rest run the call, which is A's defensive turn. Hoop League Tactics shows that a ball handler with only move, pass and shoot is a complete offense as long as the defense answers every one. Blood Bowl shows that move-then-act is best allowed once and priced, and A already has that action: the crossover. Football, Tactics and Glory shows what C costs, and Retro Bowl shows what a game with no defensive turn becomes, which is not the two-player game the owner is building.

B does not gain from this family; its move-then-pass turn has no cousin here. D is Retro Bowl with a defense bolted on and loses the rally. E was already dead. C should be retired now, on Football, Tactics and Glory's evidence, not held as the learnable one.

Three cuts to A, none of which change its grammar:

1. **The receiver is the button.** With the ball in hand, every teammate wears a ring with its price; tapping the ring is the pass. PASS stays on the row as the lit label so the row still reads move, pass, shoot. One tap, not two. (Madden's icons, Retro Bowl's drag.)
2. **The steal costs a blow-by.** A wrong answer on the steal card gives the ball handler one free tile past the reacher, with no defensive answer. STEAL stays a gamble. (Hoop League Tactics' warning.)
3. **The play stays painted.** On the offense's turn, a faint arrow from each off-ball player to his next spot in the picked play. Moving him along it is still one turn. (Playvision.)

And one line of copy for the announcer, from NBA Street: the shot call starts on the SHOOT tap and finishes on the card, so the waiting player is watching a shot, not a quiz.
