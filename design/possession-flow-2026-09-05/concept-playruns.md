# The Play Runs Itself

**One line:** Your team runs the play you picked on its own, the game freezes every time the ball has a decision, you make that decision with one tap, and the defense answers with one step.

## The loop

The offense never moves a player. The play chosen at the start of the quarter runs as a short animation: cutters cut, screeners screen, the coverage the defense chose shadows them by its own rules. The animation stops at a read point, which is any moment the ball handler has a new option (a screen just landed, a pass just arrived, a drive just finished). At a read point the ball handler is lit and four slots sit at the bottom: SHOOT, DRIVE, PASS, RUN IT, each with its price printed on it. The offense taps one. The play resolves that tap (the pass flies, the drive lands, or the next segment of the play runs), then the defense gets one answer: tap one defender and he steps toward the ball, or tap HOLD. Then the next read appears. Ball, help, ball, help, until the shot goes up or the ball is lost. A made shot, a miss the defense rebounds, a shot clock violation or a wrong answer on a drive or pass ends the possession; a steal or a defensive rebound flips it live with no reset.

Answer to the doubt, straight: no, the offense does not pick a player, move him, wait, and then pick again. There is only ever one lit player, the one with the ball, and every offensive turn asks the same question: what do you do with the ball? Every defensive turn asks the same question: who steps up? Two questions, always in the same order. A first-time player has the whole rhythm after one possession.

## Move then shoot?

No. A drive ends the offense's turn at a new read point. The shot is a separate turn, and the defense's one step comes between the two. What the defense gets is exactly the moment real basketball gives it: the drive lands, the help steps up, and the shot is now contested (the block card is live) or the lane to the next drive is gated (a crossover question). The reason it does not feel like a stall is that the drive animation, the defender's step and the new read arrive as one motion, about three seconds, and the read the offense lands on is a real basketball read: finish, or kick to the man the help just left.

The same rule covers pass then shoot. A pass lands at a read, the defense gets its closeout step, then the receiver's SHOOT slot shows the price after the closeout. If no defender can reach the receiver in one step, the shot stays open. That is how spacing gets rewarded: the pass to the man the help cannot get to.

## Why it reads clearly

Four devices, and nothing else on the screen.

1. One lit player, always the ball. The ring and the cone of light are on the ball handler and nobody else. On the defense's beat the ball handler wears a red threat ring instead, and the defenders who can reach him glow. The player never has to work out who the game is asking about.
2. The clock is the turn. There is exactly one clock on the screen, top right, big, and its colour is the colour of the side that is on the clock. Your colour and 24 means your read. Their colour and 5 means their step. Anyone's colour and 15 means a question card. It grows and glows from 5 down. When the clock changes colour, the turn changed. That alone answers "you don't even realize it's your turn again."
3. The choice row is the only instruction, and it always has the same four slots in the same places: SHOOT top left, DRIVE top right, PASS bottom left, RUN IT bottom right. A greyed slot says why in three words. The defense's row is one line and one button. Nothing says "your turn" three times; the row header says it once with the player's name.
4. The hand-off is a swing of light and a sound. The court's sideline light sweeps from your colour to theirs with a soft tick when the step is theirs, and back with a tick when it is yours. The announcer line at the top says, in the past tense, what just happened ("Kick to Ionescu"), so a player who looks up late knows both what happened and that it is now on them. A change of possession is louder: buzzer, full-screen stamp, the light swings and stays.

When the computer is the defense, its step still plays out at real speed with the squeak and the announcer line, held for half a second, so the human sees the defense move before the light swings back. It is never skipped.

The play's own reads are the pacing. The player never decides when to stop; the game stops for them at the moment the ball has a choice, and only then.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

The chores are gone and the decisions stayed. Moving five bodies was the chore; deciding whether to pull up off the screen is the game. Every read point is one of basketball's real moments: the screen is open, the help came, the corner is open, the clock is at four. The four slots are a menu of bets with the price printed on each, which is why it feels like the Pokemon move list the owner named: four moves, each a different gamble.

The play unfolding is a little film every possession, and watching it is fun because the choice is coming and you can see it coming. RUN IT is a live option, not a skip: letting the play run buys a better read but burns the clock, so the tension is real. The defense is playing too, every beat, with one guess: where does the ball go next, and who do I send. Sending Brown to the corner leaves Curry alone at the top; the offense sees it next read. Help and recover, back and forth, is the whole conversation, and it is a conversation because each side speaks in one tap.

Safe moves first, the gamble last, which the research found in Blood Bowl: the play (free, safe) runs, and the shot (the question) ends it. The announcer calls it like NBA Street. The buzzer closes it.

## Fit with the locked rules

- Question gates: unchanged. SHOOT asks the tile's question at the tile's price; DRIVE past a man asks the crossover question at the engine's price; PASS through a body asks the pass question. The slots print the price from the same rules the card uses, so the row can never promise a price the card will not ask.
- Eight-tile guarding: unchanged, and it is what prices the reads. Where the coverage puts its bodies between reads, plus the human's one step, decides which defenders contest, gate or close.
- Closed lanes: unchanged. DRIVE greys with "two men in the lane".
- The 24 second clock: CHANGED in how it counts, said out loud. Today it is 24 per turn. Here it is one 24 per possession, in real time: it runs during the offense's reads and while the play moves, and freezes during the defense's five-second step and during any question card. The bring-up after a made basket costs a flat four seconds. Why: RUN IT has to cost something or every read is free, and a clock that only the offense can spend is the only clock a human defender cannot stall.
- Plays picked once: this design is built on it. The picked play is the possession's animation, three authored reads and then freelance. The defense's coverage, picked once, is the shadowing rule between reads (man follows through screens one tile behind, the pack keeps the big home, a zone holds spots, a trap puts two on the ball at the read and closes the lanes, a press adds a read during the bring-up).
- Offense-only timeouts: a small chip left of the clock, only on offense reads, only while timeouts remain. Tap it, pick a new play on the quarter-start screen, come back to the same spot with the same clock. The new play's first segment runs from wherever the team stands.
- Live-ball continuation: kept and made stronger. A steal or a defensive rebound gives the new offense its first read at once, ball where it is, with two freelance reads (teammates fill the lanes on their own) before the team flows into its play from wherever it stands. The buzzer and stamp fire, the shot clock resets, nothing else resets.

Rules I would change, said plainly:

1. The free off-ball step (one square, off-ball only) goes. The play runs off-ball movement. The owner already said this on 09-05.
2. The defense's slide comes after every offensive beat, RUN IT included, not only after the main action. In this design every offensive beat is an action.
3. The dead-ball setup ritual (defense shows a shape, offense picks seeing it, every inbound) leaves the possession and lives only at the quarter start and in timeouts.
4. The turn tray (SETUPS, BALL IN, FREE MOVES, SLIDE, ACTION), which the owner said he loved on 08-16, retires. It listed steps; now there is one step, repeated. Saying this because he loved it.
5. One thing the research warned against that I am doing anyway: defensive bodies move on their own between reads. The research found delegated defense drew an "absolute nightmare" complaint in the closest mobile cousin, and auto-firing zones read as luck in another. My answer is the two rules the research did approve: the coverage is honest and coarse (drawn on the floor, it never lies, it never says the exact square), and the human's one step per beat is the real defense. Every shadow move follows a visible rule, and nothing fires without the player seeing it.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 3 to 5 (the inbound plus three authored reads, sometimes a freelance read). Typical 4.
- Defense decisions: one per offensive decision, 3 to 5, some of them HOLD. Typical 4.
- Total taps: offense 5 to 7 (a pass is two taps, a question is one), defense 3 to 5. About 11 per possession, against roughly 16 today.
- Seconds per possession: about 45 (three reads at four seconds, two segments at two seconds, four defensive steps at three seconds, one question card at about eight, resolution and flip at three). Fifty-five with a block card.
- Longest wait for the other player: the question card, 15 seconds at most, typically eight. The defender waiting on a read is bounded by the shot clock, typically four seconds, worst case whatever is left on the 24. The offense waiting on the defense: five seconds, hard cap.

## Weaknesses

- Authoring. Eight plays times three reads, each with its four slot contents, its camera frame and its segment animation, plus six coverages as shadowing rules. A play with a dull read feels like a cutscene; a play with too many reads feels like today. This is a content job the size of a question pack.
- Rails. The offense cannot improvise off the ball. A player who wants to move Tatum to the wing cannot. The answers are the timeout and the freelance reads, and some players will still miss the toy.
- Stale reads. One play per quarter means the defense has seen every read by the third possession. The coverage and the step keep it live (who steps is a fresh guess each beat), but the research's staged fixes for stale reads are queued behind this from day one.
- The tight 24. A first-timer who thinks for eight seconds a read eats a violation on possession one. One coach line on the first buzzer, and the numbers above are tuneable.
- Watching. Four segments of two seconds is eight seconds of watching per possession, and online both phones must watch the same thing, so there is no skip.
- The camera. Each read's frame is authored, which is a strength, but every frame must be proven at phone width to hold the ball handler and every receiver. The corner shooter off the edge of the screen is the exact complaint from 09-05.
- The auto-moving defense, above, is the one place the research says be careful.

## Open questions for the owner

1. Sighted or blind picks at the quarter start. The research rejects hidden simultaneous calls (they collapse into a coin flip) and recommends the defense picking and showing first. Yes or no.
2. Does the announcer name the play out loud each possession ("Horns"), so the defense hears it, or is the play only readable from the floor?
3. Does the defense get a gamble on its own beat (REACH: a steal question, right is a live steal, wrong is a blow-by), or does the defense's gambling stay inside the crossover duel and the block card as today?
4. The shot clock: one real 24 with freezes (my pick), or a fixed clock per read with the 24 as decoration.
5. Hot seat: on the defense's beat, does the five-second clock start on the first touch of the phone, or does a half-second "hand it over" card sit first?
6. When the play is spent, RUN IT becomes RESET (dribble it out, three seconds off the clock, the play restarts from the top). Keep, or grey it and let the clock force the shot?
7. Does the small score and game clock stay on screen during reads, or show only between possessions? The opening ruling kept the scoreboard; this is a smaller version of the same question.
8. The announcer: text only, or voice lines too, the NBA Street way.

## Borrowed from

- American football play-calling and the quarterback's progression: the play runs, the passer reads through his options. Madden's receiver icons (a button on every receiver) is the PASS slot's shape: tap the man.
- Pokemon's four-move menu: fixed slots, fixed places, a cost on each.
- Kill Team's Counteract, which the research confirmed: one capped move, after the enemy acts, with full information.
- Into the Breach and Slay the Spire, from the research: the telegraph is honest and coarse, drawn on the board, and out-reading it feels earned.
- Blood Bowl, from the research: safe moves first, the gamble last, and the failed gamble ends the turn.
- Strat-O-Matic, from the research: one matchup is the story of a possession; everyone else is context. Here the context moves by itself.
- Football Manager: you set the plan, the engine plays it, you step in at the moments that matter.
- The broadcast freeze frame: the film room pauses the play at the decision and draws the options on the floor.
- Chess: strict alternation and a clock that belongs to one side at a time. Not chess's content; the player decides, the pieces do not wait to be dragged.
- NBA Street: the announcer says what happened, never where on the grid.
