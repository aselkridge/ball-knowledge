# The Clock Is The Turn

**One line:** The 24 runs for real, both teams move whenever they like, and the only time anyone waits is when a question freezes the floor.

**Where this belongs, said up front.** This is a different game from the one on the first line of the project ("turn-based basketball strategy"). I recommend it as its own way to play, the streetball mode the design file already reserves under the name Blacktop, sitting next to the turn-based game rather than replacing it. Two reasons. First, the main game promises that knowledge decides and thumbs never do; here thumbs decide which question you get, even though they never decide whether a right answer scores. Second, the computer opponent, the coach, the drills and the online relay are all built around a world that stops between actions, and this world only stops for cards. So: a separate mode. It is still designed in full below, because it answers the owner's doubt more completely than any turn order can, and because two of its parts (the walking piece and the question as the only freeze) are worth taking into the main game whatever he rules.

## The loop

The 24 second clock starts at the catch and runs for real. The offense taps a tile and the ball handler walks there at a fixed pace; taps a teammate and the ball flies; taps SHOOT and the floor freezes for a question. The defense, at the same time and with no turn to wait for, taps a defender and a tile and that defender slides; the eight tiles around him shade as he goes, and the moment two shadows cover one lane it goes dark and cannot be entered. Nothing takes turns except the card: a shot, a crossover through a man, a pass through traffic, or a reach for a steal freezes everything, one person answers with the 15 second question clock, and the floor unfreezes into the result. The possession ends on a made basket (dead ball, both teams walk into the plays they picked at the start, the other side inbounds on a five second count), on a wrong answer (turnover, live, the defender has it in his hands and the clock flips), on a miss (a rebound card, live, no reset), or on the 24 running out (the buzzer, dead ball, other team's ball).

## Move then shoot?

Yes, always, and the question disappears because there is no turn to steal. Moving is a walk that takes real seconds, so the defense's answer is not a turn it is owed but the seconds it is given: a guard covering three tiles takes about two seconds, and a defender can slide in the same two seconds. The moving figurine is the telegraph, honest and visible, the way the research praised telegraphs that never lie. What the defense gets: time, sight, the live repricing of every lane, and one button of its own (REACH, a steal card, only when standing on a tile touching the ball; wrong answer is a foul). The defense can freeze the floor exactly as the offense can, so neither side can run the other off the screen.

## Why it reads clearly

There is no "whose turn is it" to misread outside a card, because both players are always on. Your figurines are your colour; the one with the ball wears the one bright ring on the floor; the 24 in the top right is the offense's colour and flips colour with the ball. When the floor freezes, the card faces the person who answers and the other end goes dim, so the hand-off is not a message, it is the phone turning its face to you. Every freeze opens with a slam across the floor (CROSSOVER, FOR THREE, TURNOVER, SHOT CLOCK) and every slam has its sound; the buzzer belongs to the 24 and to turnovers only, so a buzzer always means the ball moved sides. The announcer speaks only about the ball, in names, never in tiles: "Curry finds Thompson", not "moved to a tile". One place says what to do: the choice row at the offense's end, two buttons plus the court; the defense's end shows the court and, at most, one button. Nothing else is on the screen: no step ladder, no grey note bar, no strip. The shipped shared-phone buzz race already taught this phone position, one player at each end, bottom always meaning you.

## Why it is fun, not tedious

Nothing waits. The tedium the owner named (click, move, click, move, then tap done, then watch) came from setting up a whole team in turns; here nobody sets anyone up, the pieces are already in their plays and the possession is one story about the ball. The rhythm is basketball's own: the 24 ticking, a drive, a kick, a shot, and then the whole floor holding its breath while one person reads a card. The defense is engaged every second instead of waiting for a slide window, and its REACH button gives it a way to go and get the ball rather than only to stand in the way. The clock does the job the step ladder tried to do: it gives the possession a shape and an ending without a single tap. And the freeze makes the question land harder, because it interrupts motion.

## Fit with the locked rules

- **The question gates.** Untouched. Every score, every crossover through a man, every pass through traffic is a card at the tile's price; wrong is a turnover. The floor never promises a price the card does not ask, because the price is read from the tile at the moment of the tap.
- **Eight tile guarding and closed lanes.** Kept exactly, but computed live: a defender's shadow moves with him, and a lane goes dark the instant two shadows cover it. A tap on a dark lane is refused. A lane that closes while the handler is already walking it does not stop him: the tap is the commitment.
- **The 24 second clock.** It becomes the real thing. It starts at the catch, holds during any card, and its buzzer is the possession's only hard ending. The defense no longer has its own 24; the defense lives on the offense's clock, the way it does in basketball.
- **Plays picked once, offense-only timeouts.** Kept. The picked shapes are where all ten walk on every dead ball. A timeout is a small whistle chip beside the clock at the offense's end, freezes the floor, and reopens the play list; it is the one non-basketball control on the screen.
- **Live-ball continuation.** Kept and made stronger: a steal or a rebound never stops the clock's colour flip; the row slides ends and play continues.
- **Rules I would change, said out loud.** (1) The 15 second question clock is the only time the 24 stops, so the possession is no longer "a turn with a 24"; that is the mode's whole point. (2) Role movement ranges (one to three tiles) become the length of one walk before the figurine plants for one second; the numbers stay, their meaning changes from "per turn" to "per burst". (3) The inbound gets a five second count, which the owner already asked for. (4) The defense's separate 24 is dropped. (5) The one law I bend, not break: "reflex only ever as upside." A right answer still scores; nothing about speed can erase it. But speed of thought before the freeze now decides which card you draw and whether it is contested, and the owner should rule with that in front of him.

## Numbers

- Offense decisions per possession: 4 to 7 (an inbound pass, one to three walks, one or two passes, a shot).
- Defense decisions per possession: 3 to 6 slides, plus a reach when adjacent.
- Total taps: offense 6 to 10; defense 6 to 12 (two a slide, or one drag each), so 12 to 22 on the phone in a possession.
- Seconds per possession: 25 to 40 (15 to 24 live, plus one or two cards at 6 to 10 seconds each for a quick reader; the ceiling is 24 plus 15 plus 15).
- Longest wait for the other player: 15 seconds, and only inside a card that concerns them; on the floor the wait is zero.

## What stops it becoming a tapping race

- **Tapping does nothing faster.** A tap is a destination, not a teleport. Figurines walk at one fixed pace, about half a second a tile, for both teams and every player. A fast thumb sends the same order a slow thumb sends.
- **One order per piece at a time.** A walking figurine ignores taps until it arrives and plants for one second. There is no queue, so mashing buys nothing and planning buys everything. This is the rule that made real-time chess playable twenty years ago.
- **The ball has weight.** A pass takes half a second a tile in the air, the receiver stands still until the catch, and a shot stops the world. The offense cannot outrun the defense's sight of it.
- **Symmetric freezes.** The offense can stop the floor with SHOOT; the defense can stop it with REACH. Neither can be run off the screen.
- **Contest is decided by arrival, not by the order.** A defender still walking is not contesting, so the offense cannot be jumped by a tap sent a tenth of a second before the shot, and the defense cannot claim a contest it had not reached.
- **The clock is long against the burst.** Six to ten orders a side in 24 seconds leaves room to think; the question clock, not the 24, is where the game's real pressure sits.

## Hot seat and online, the two homes

- **Hot seat (one phone).** The phone lies flat between the players, held the long way, one at each end; the court is drawn from above with a slight tilt so neither end reads it upside down (the owner's chess-board instinct, made literal for this mode). Each player taps their own figurines directly on the court. The phone cannot tell fingers apart, so this is the board game rule: you do not touch the other side's men, and on a shared table everyone can see a hand that does. The alternative if that proves unpleasant: five small chips at each end, tap the chip then the tile, which makes every order start from your own end; it costs nothing to build and one tap per order to play. Cards face the end that answers; the other end dims. Only one card is ever up, so nobody reads over anyone's shoulder for an answer that is theirs.
- **Online (two phones).** Each phone keeps its own tilted camera and its own end of the floor. Every order carries the shared clock's time when it was sent. The offense's phone is the authority for the ball and the freezes, the defense's phone for the defenders, and the shared stream already carries orders as events. At a freeze, the offense's phone takes every defensive order stamped before the freeze and applies it before deciding contested or open; ties go to the defender, because "he got there" is the fair-sounding read and it makes the offense commit a beat earlier rather than a beat later. A reconnect mid-possession is a dead ball, the same play shapes, the offense inbounds with a fresh 24.
- **Versus the computer.** The computer slides on a reaction delay by level (Rookie late, All-Star early) and never on a perfect reflex, so the level is felt as how much time the player has, not as a machine that cannot be beaten.

## Weaknesses

- It is not the game on the tin. A player who came for turn-based will find a clock running against them the moment they think, which is why it is a mode and not the game.
- Thumb speed is not zero. It does not decide the card, but a slow player spends seconds of the 24 finding the tile; on the phone's small floor a guard's three-tile reach and a closed lane can be hard to read in a hurry. Big tiles and few lit choices are the only defence, and they need testing at phone size.
- The shared phone runs on honour. Nothing technical stops a hand touching the other side's men; the chips option costs a tap per order to fix it.
- Online latency lands exactly on the moment that matters, the freeze. The arrival rule and the defender's tie make it explainable, not invisible; a bad connection will still produce a contest that one phone saw and the other did not.
- The coach cannot teach while the floor runs. Every tip has to land at a freeze, a dead ball, or a timeout.
- The camera for hot seat (from above) is a second camera to build and keep, next to the tilted side view the entrance drop lands on.
- Two players tapping one screen at once is a touch-handling job the shipped game has never done; multi-touch from both ends must be proven on real phones before any of this is promised.

## Open questions for the owner

1. Mode or main game? My recommendation is the mode, Blacktop, with the walking piece and the question-as-the-only-freeze taken into the turn-based game regardless. Does he want it as the game instead?
2. Hot seat control: direct taps on your own men (honour rule), or five chips at each end (one extra tap an order, no honour needed)?
3. Should the defense have REACH at all, or is standing in the way its whole job? REACH gives it a freeze of its own; without it the offense is the only side that can stop the clock.
4. Contest decided by arrival at the freeze (my call), or by the order having been sent before the shot?
5. Should a crossover card freeze the whole floor, or only the two men in it, with the other eight still able to slide? Whole floor is clearer; two men is more basketball.
6. Does the release meter survive here on a contested make, or is the block card enough?
7. Five seconds on the inbound, and does the defense pressing during the count feel right or feel like a trap for a first-time player?
8. The camera for hot seat: from above with a slight tilt, or keep the side view and accept that one end reads the floor upside down?

## Borrowed from

- **Kung Fu Chess** (the real-time chess of the early 2000s): no turns, pieces move at a fixed pace, and each piece rests after it moves; that rest is the whole answer to the tapping race.
- **NBA Street and NBA Jam**: the announcer who calls what happened in names, the running clock, and the floor as the only thing on screen.
- **Basketball itself**: the 24 as the one hard ending, the five second inbound, the defense living on the offense's clock, contest decided by who got there.
- **Blood Bowl** (from the research): a failed gamble ends everything; the wrong answer is a live turnover with no reset.
- **Into the Breach and Slay the Spire** (from the research): the telegraph is honest and visible; here the telegraph is the figurine walking.
- **The shipped shared-phone buzz race**: the phone flat, one player at each end, bottom always meaning you, the card facing the answerer.
- **Board game table manners**: you do not touch the other side's pieces; the table sees it if you do.
