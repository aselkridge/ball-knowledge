COMPARATIVE GAME-DESIGN RESEARCH, third pass, narrow and deep. Four questions.
Two earlier sweeps verified answers on board design and pacing; these
questions returned nothing verified and are re-run here with full depth.
Study SHIPPED GAMES and their documented mechanics, not theory.

THE GAME (context): Ball Knowledge — two-player, turn-based basketball
strategy on a tile-grid court. Every scoring attempt is gated by a basketball
trivia question (~15-second clock); the tile sets difficulty and points.
Real NBA/WNBA players as pieces. Private cards on phones; a TV "couch mode"
(board on TV, cards on phones, question revealed to the room when the shot
goes up) ships in the first release to a 20-person friend group. Planned but
undecided: a solo daily mode ("The Daily Five"), per-player question memory
across games ("cards remember you"), adaptive difficulty, and whether
practice drills become the tutorial.

QUESTION 1 — TRIVIA MECHANICS FROM THE BEST QUIZ GAMES. Four halves, each a
live decision; answer each separately and name it if it comes back thin:
(a) REPEATED QUESTIONS: how do shipped quiz games (You Don't Know Jack,
Trivial Pursuit editions, Trivia Crack, Jackpardy-likes, pub quiz apps,
Duolingo's review system if relevant) handle a player seeing the same
question twice? Is remembering a previously-seen answer treated as a bug
(dead question) or a feature (spaced repetition / mastery)? Numbers where
they exist: bank sizes, cooldown windows, retirement rules.
(b) MIXED-KNOWLEDGE GROUPS: how does an expert play a novice without either
being bored? Handicaps, difficulty asymmetry, category choice, wagering
(Wits & Wagers is the famous case — how exactly does its betting layer let
non-knowers compete?). What shipped, what's documented as working?
(c) TEACHING ON A WRONG ANSWER: which games show/explain the right answer
after a miss, and is there designer commentary or player evidence on whether
it helps retention vs slows the game? (Duolingo, Kahoot, Brilliant, quiz
shows' "the answer was...")
(d) SOLO DAILY LOOPS: what makes a daily quiz a habit — and what kills one?
Wordle's one-per-day scarcity, NYT games' streaks, Duolingo streak mechanics
(and streak-freeze monetization), Immaculate Grid's sports-daily loop,
LearnedLeague's season structure. Numbers: streak effects on retention where
published.
→ decides: The Daily Five design, cards-remember-you, adaptive difficulty,
and the wrong-answer-teaches moment.

QUESTION 2 — TEACHING WITHOUT A MANUAL, WITH A RECOMMENDATION. How do
mechanically complex games onboard players without a rulebook wall of text?
Study documented cases: tutorial-as-first-level (Plants vs Zombies' famous
gradual reveal, Portal's test chambers), teach-one-rule-per-scenario
(chess.com lessons, Into the Breach), guided first game (Wingspan's automa /
board game companion apps), progressive unlock (Hearthstone's staged modes).
Designer post-mortems outrank reviews. END WITH A RECOMMENDATION for a game
whose rules include movement ranges, question tiers, contest rules, fouls
and heat — should the shipped practice drills BECOME the tutorial (drills
promoted into the first release), or is there a verified better pattern?
A comparison without a recommendation is a FAIL.

QUESTION 3 — SPECTATORS OF HIDDEN INFORMATION. The half that came back empty
last time. When information is hidden from some participants, what keeps the
NON-ACTING viewer engaged? Study: poker broadcasting's hole-card cam (what
did revealing hole cards to the AUDIENCE change about poker as a spectator
product — this is documented history), Hanabi's inverted information (the
table sees your cards, you don't), social deduction games as spectator
experiences (Among Us streams), and game shows that let the home audience
know the answer before the contestant answers. The design question: in our
TV mode, WHEN should the room see the question and the card — before the
shooter answers (dramatic irony), simultaneously, or after? What does shipped
evidence say each choice does to spectator fun?
→ decides: the reveal timing in TV mode, which is in the first release.
